Topic 1: Properties of Fluids

Syllabus line: Fluid properties — specific gravity, viscosity, surface tension, vapour pressure.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 1 question
  • Real fluid: viscosity causes shear resistance and converts mechanical energy into heat (Q148, statement type).

1. Basic idea

A fluid is anything that flows: liquids and gases. A solid resists a sideways (shear) push by deforming a little and stopping. A fluid keeps on deforming as long as the shear force acts — that is why water takes the shape of the bottle. Every property below tells us how a fluid behaves when we push it, squeeze it, heat it or leave it in a thin tube.

2. Why engineers need these properties

Density decides pressure (Topic 2) and buoyancy (Topic 3). Viscosity decides pipe friction losses (Topic 6) and the boundary layer (Topic 7). Vapour pressure decides cavitation in pumps and turbines (Topics 8–9). So this small topic feeds every later topic.

Viscosity and temperature (a favourite trap): in a liquid, viscosity comes from cohesion between molecules; heating loosens them, so viscosity falls (hot oil flows easily). In a gas, viscosity comes from molecules jumping between layers (momentum exchange); heating makes them jump more, so viscosity rises.

Essential formulas — surface tension and capillarity
  • Pressure inside a liquid droplet: p = 4σ/d  ·  soap bubble (two surfaces): p = 8σ/d  ·  liquid jet: p = 2σ/d
  • Capillary rise: h = 4σ cosθ / (ρ g d). Water–glass θ ≈ 0° → rise. Mercury–glass θ ≈ 130°–140° → cos θ negative → fall (depression).
  • Narrower tube → higher rise (h ∝ 1/d). That is why fine pores in soil and bricks suck up water.
  • Cavitation: when local pressure drops to the vapour pressure, the liquid boils even at room temperature; the vapour bubbles collapse violently and pit the metal (Topics 8–9).

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a pack of playing cards on a table. Push the top card sideways — every card slides a little over the one below. The "stickiness" between cards is viscosity; the bottom card stuck to the table is the no-slip condition.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Poise vs stoke: poise is for μ (dynamic); stoke is for ν (kinematic). 1 poise = 0.1 N·s/m², not 1.
  • Ideal fluid = no viscosity and incompressible — imaginary, used to simplify theory (Bernoulli). A real fluid has viscosity, so energy is lost as heat.
  • Soap bubble = 8σ/d because it has two surfaces (inside and outside); a droplet has one.
  • Specific gravity has no unit; specific weight has a unit (N/m³).

6. Connections

Viscosity → Reynolds number (Topic 6) → laminar or turbulent flow. Vapour pressure → NPSH and cavitation (Topic 9). Capillarity → soil mechanics (Paper 3 Civil: capillary rise in soils).

Topic 2: Pressure and Its Measurement

Syllabus line: Pascal's law, pressure measurement — piezometer, U-tube and differential manometers.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 5 questions
  • Mechanical gauges balance the fluid column by a spring or dead weight (Q52).
  • Name the principle: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted everywhere — Pascal's law (Q55).
  • Convert a pressure of 3.924 N/cm² into head of water — 4 m (Q56).
  • Pressure measured with atmospheric pressure as datum — gauge pressure (Q57); gauge pressure 3 m below a liquid surface (Q58).

1. Basic idea

Pressure is force per unit area, p = F/A (N/m² = pascal). In a fluid at rest, pressure at a point acts equally in all directions (Pascal's law) and increases with depth because the fluid above has weight.

2. Why it exists

Each layer of water must carry the weight of all the water above it. Go down by h and you add the weight of a column of height h on each square metre: p = ρ g h. This is the hydrostatic law, dp/dz = −ρg (pressure falls as you go up).

4. Everyday analogy

Diving into a deep pond: your ears hurt more the deeper you go — more water above means more pressure. Squeezing a toothpaste tube anywhere pushes paste out at the nozzle — pressure is transmitted equally (Pascal).

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Gauge pressure can be negative (vacuum); absolute pressure can never be negative.
  • 3.924 N/cm² = 39,240 N/m² → head = 39240/9810 = 4 m of water. Always convert cm² to m² first (× 10⁴).
  • The factor 12.6 applies only when the flowing liquid is water and the manometer liquid is mercury. For oil (S = 0.8): x(13.6/0.8 − 1) = 16x.

6. Connections

Hydrostatic law → forces on dams and gates (Topic 3) → venturimeter readings (Topic 5). Soil mechanics uses the same idea for pore water pressure (Paper 3 Civil).

Topic 3: Hydrostatic Forces, Buoyancy and Floating Bodies

Syllabus line: Hydrostatic forces on plane and curved surfaces; buoyancy, floating bodies and stability.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 6 questions (+1 cancelled)
  • Centre of pressure of an inclined rectangular plane surface (Q54).
  • Total pressure on a vertical rectangular plate whose top edge is 1 m below the surface (Q60).
  • Formula for depth of centre of pressure, h* = IG/(A x̄) + x̄ (Q61).
  • Weight of a floating body from its depth of immersion (Q62); meaning of metacentre (Q63); time period of rolling (Q64).

1. Basic idea

Water pushes on every wetted surface — a dam, a sluice gate, the side of a tank. Because pressure grows with depth, the push is larger at the bottom. We need two answers: how big is the total force, and where it acts (the centre of pressure).

2. Why the centre of pressure is below the centroid

Pressure is small near the top and large near the bottom (a triangle of pressure). The resultant of a triangle acts at its lower third, so the force acts below the centroid of the plate. Only for a horizontal plate (uniform pressure) do the two points coincide.

3. Curved surfaces (gates, pipes, domes)

  • Horizontal component FH = force on the vertical projection of the curved surface (use F = ρgAx̄ on that projection).
  • Vertical component FV = weight of the liquid actually or imaginarily lying above the curved surface up to the free surface.
  • Resultant F = √(FH² + FV²), inclined at tan⁻¹(FV/FH).

4. Buoyancy and floating bodies

Archimedes' principle: a body in a fluid is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of fluid displaced. This upward force acts at the centre of buoyancy B — the centroid of the displaced volume. A floating body: weight = buoyancy, so depth of immersion = Sbody/Sliquid × depth.

Essential formulas — stability
  • Metacentric height GM = I/V − BG (I = second moment of the water-line plane about the tilt axis, V = volume displaced)
  • Floating body: stable if M is above G (GM > 0), neutral if M coincides with G, unstable if M is below G.
  • Fully submerged body (submarine, balloon): stable if B is above G.
  • Time period of rolling T = 2π √(k² / (g · GM)) (k = radius of gyration). Large GM → quick, jerky rolling; small GM → slow, comfortable rolling but less safe.

5. Everyday analogy

A loaded country boat with passengers standing up feels tippy — G has gone up and GM has shrunk. Ask them to sit on the floor and the boat steadies: G comes down and GM increases.

6. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Total force F = ρgAx̄ does not depend on the inclination; the centre of pressure does (sin²θ term).
  • As a plate goes deeper, IG/(A x̄) gets smaller, so the centre of pressure moves closer to the centroid.
  • Metacentre M is a point; metacentric height is the distance GM. The 2023 paper asked to tell them apart (Q63).

7. Connections

Dam and gate design (Paper 3 Civil — gravity dams: water pressure is the main force); retaining walls (earth pressure uses the same triangle idea); ship design.

Topic 4: Fluid Kinematics — Types of Flow, Continuity, Stream Function and Flow Net

Syllabus line: Kinematics — types of flow, velocity and acceleration, continuity equation, velocity potential, stream function, flow net.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 20 questions — the largest block in Fluid Mechanics
  • Continuity: velocity after the diameter changes (Q98, Q100); compressible flow with area and density change (Q99); two pipes joining into one (Q101).
  • Velocity components from a stream function ψ = 5x − 6y (Q103); condition for irrotational flow (Q97); possible irrotational ψ (Q111).
  • Rotation, vorticity (= 2 × rotation), shear strain rate (Q106, Q112, Q113, Q117).
  • Local and convective acceleration (Q114, Q116); finding the third velocity component (Q115).
  • Flow net — uses and statements (Q104, Q105); type of flow when a tank drains (Q107); which parameters exist in both rotational and irrotational flow (Q108).

1. Basic idea

Kinematics describes how fluid moves — velocity and acceleration — without asking what forces cause the motion (that is dynamics, Topic 5). We describe the velocity at every point: V = u i + v j + w k.

2. Types of flow (learn in pairs)

2023 example (Q107): water drains from a tank through a small pipe of uniform diameter. Along the pipe the velocity is the same (uniform), but it falls as the tank level drops (unsteady) → unsteady and uniform.

3. Continuity — mass is never lost

Whatever mass enters a section per second must leave the next section: ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂. For a liquid (ρ constant): A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ = Q. Since A ∝ d², V ∝ 1/d²: halve the diameter → four times the velocity.

Essential formulas — continuity and acceleration
  • 3-D continuity (incompressible): ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0 — use it to find a missing velocity component (2023 Q115).
  • Acceleration = local (∂u/∂t, change with time) + convective (u∂u/∂x + v∂u/∂y + w∂u/∂z, change with position).
  • 1-D steady flow along a streamline: a = V·dV/ds. Example (2023 Q116): V rises uniformly from 2 to 5 m/s over 1 m → at B, a = 5 × 3/1 = 15 m/s².
  • Two pipes (each d, velocity v) joining one pipe D with velocity v/3: 2(πd²/4)v = (πD²/4)(v/3) → D² = 6d² (2023 Q101).

4. Rotation, vorticity and strain (the "spin" of a particle)

Advanced / exam extension — rotation formulas (2-D, x–y plane)
  • Rotation about z: ωz = ½ (∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y)
  • Vorticity = 2 × rotation = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y
  • Irrotational flow: ω = 0 → ∂v/∂x = ∂u/∂y
  • Shear strain rate (Bansal): ½ (∂v/∂x + ∂u/∂y). Example (2023 Q106): u = 8x³, v = −10x²y → ½(−20xy + 0) = −10xy.

6. Streamline, pathline, streakline

  • Streamline: an imaginary line whose tangent gives the velocity direction at that instant.
  • Pathline: the actual path traced by one particle over time.
  • Streakline: the line joining all particles that passed through one fixed point (dye or smoke injected at a point).
  • In steady flow all three coincide.

Topic 5: Fluid Dynamics — Bernoulli, Momentum and Flow Measurement

Syllabus line: Euler's and Bernoulli's equations and applications; momentum equation; flow measurement — venturimeter, orificemeter, rotameter, pitot tube, hot-wire anemometer.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 16 questions
  • Total head of water from pressure, velocity and datum (Q53, Q85); piezometric head = elevation + pressure head (Q70).
  • Assumptions of Bernoulli and Euler equations — which is INCORRECT (Q65, Q73); HGL and TEL definitions and statements (Q71, Q86, Q94, Q95).
  • Venturimeter with a differential mercury manometer (Q69); negative pressure at the throat (Q72); orifice meter statements (Q67).
  • Pitot tube — measures point velocity (Q68, Q83); velocity from stagnation and static heads (Q74); moment-of-momentum statements (Q75).

1. Basic idea

Flowing water carries three kinds of energy per unit weight (each measured in metres, called head): pressure head p/ρg, velocity (kinetic) head V²/2g and datum (potential) head z. Bernoulli's equation says that for an ideal fluid their sum stays constant along a streamline:

p/ρg + V²/2g + z = H = constant

2. Why it is true

It is the law of conservation of energy. Start from Euler's equation (Newton's second law for a fluid particle moving along a streamline), dp/ρ + g dz + V dV = 0, and integrate for an incompressible fluid. Assumptions: ideal (non-viscous), steady, incompressible, along a streamline, irrotational. So "the flow is rotational" or "the flow is unsteady" is the INCORRECT assumption (2023 Q65, Q73).

3. How it is applied: HGL and TEL

  • HGL (hydraulic gradient line) joins the piezometric heads p/ρg + z. It can rise or fall (it rises after an enlargement as velocity head turns back into pressure).
  • TEL (total energy line) joins p/ρg + V²/2g + z. It always falls in the direction of flow, unless a pump adds energy. TEL is always above HGL by V²/2g.
  • A sudden change of slope of the HGL in a straight pipe of constant diameter shows a leakage (discharge — and so friction loss per metre — changes there; 2023 Q84).
Essential formulas — flow measurement
  • Venturimeter: Q = Cd · A₁A₂ √(2gh) / √(A₁² − A₂²); Cd ≈ 0.95–0.99 (gradual cones, little loss). Mercury manometer reading x → h = 12.6 x (water).
  • Orifice meter: same formula with A₀ (orifice) and Cd ≈ 0.6–0.65; orifice diameter usually 0.5 × pipe diameter (0.4–0.8). Cheap and compact, but larger head loss.
  • Pitot tube measures velocity at a point: V = Cv √(2gΔh), Δh = stagnation head − static head.
  • Rotameter: a float rises in a tapered glass tube until the flow area balances its weight — variable-area meter, read directly.
  • Hot-wire anemometer: a heated fine wire is cooled by the flow; the cooling gives velocity and its rapid fluctuations (turbulence in gases).
  • Momentum equation: ΣF = ρQ(V₂ − V₁) (force on bends, nozzles, jets). Moment of momentum: T = ρQ(V₂r₂ − V₁r₁) — turbines, pumps, lawn sprinklers (2023 Q75).
  • Correction factors for laminar pipe flow: kinetic energy α = 2, momentum β = 4/3.
Advanced / exam extension — vortex flow (asked in 2023, Q118)
  • Forced vortex (liquid rotated by an outside torque, e.g. a stirred or spinning tank): v = ω r; free surface is a paraboloid.
  • Depth of the paraboloid z = ω²R²/2g. The volume of a paraboloid is half that of the enclosing cylinder, so the liquid at the centre falls by z/2 and rises at the wall by z/2.
  • 2023 Q118: tank of 20 cm diameter at 300 rpm → ω = 2π × 300/60 = 31.4 rad/s; z = 31.4² × 0.1²/(2 × 9.81) = 0.503 m ≈ 50.3 cm.
  • Free vortex (no outside torque, e.g. water draining through a sink hole, whirlpool): v × r = constant; Bernoulli's equation applies across streamlines.

4. Everyday analogy

Money in three pockets: pressure, speed and height. You can move money between pockets (the throat moves pressure into speed), but friction is a small fee that is never returned — so the total (TEL) slowly falls.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Pitot tube → point velocity. Venturimeter and orifice meter → discharge. Piezometer → pressure.
  • Throat velocity is maximum and pressure minimum. (2023 Q72 S1 wrongly said the velocity is minimum, so S1 was false.)
  • Venturimeter Cd (≈0.98) > orifice meter Cd (≈0.62) because the gradual divergent cone avoids eddies.

6. Connections

Bernoulli + continuity solve almost every pipe problem (Topic 6). Momentum → force of jets on vanes and turbines (Topic 8). Same energy idea in open channels — specific energy (Paper 3 Civil hydraulics).

Topic 6: Flow Through Pipes — Losses, Pipe Networks and Water Hammer

Syllabus line: Pipe flow — Reynolds experiment, major and minor losses, pipes in series and parallel, TEL and HGL, water hammer.

  • Head loss by Chezy's formula (Q89); loss at a sudden enlargement (Q90); ratio of loss to velocity head when the diameter doubles — 9/16 (Q91); loss due to an obstruction (Q92).
  • Rise of pressure when a valve closes in 25 s (Q93); condition for maximum efficiency of power transmission — hf = H/3 (Q96, Q120); power at the outlet of a pipe (Q122).
  • Critical velocity for an oil of viscosity 0.25 stoke in a 10 cm pipe — 0.5 m/s (Q109); leakage shown by a change in HGL slope (Q84).

1. Basic idea

Real water rubs against the pipe wall and against itself, so it loses head as it flows. Losses along the length are major losses (friction); losses at fittings — bends, valves, enlargements, entrances — are minor losses.

2. Laminar or turbulent? — Reynolds' experiment (1883)

Osborne Reynolds injected dye into water flowing in a glass tube. At low speed the dye stayed as a straight thread (laminar). As the speed rose, the thread wavered and then mixed completely (turbulent). What decides this is the Reynolds number — the ratio of inertia force to viscous force:

Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν    Re < 2000 laminar · 2000–4000 transition · Re > 4000 turbulent (pipes)
Essential formulas — major (friction) loss
  • Darcy–Weisbach: hf = f L V² / (2 g D), f = Darcy friction factor. (Some Indian books write hf = 4 f′ L V²/(2gD) with f′ = f/4 — check which f the question gives.)
  • Laminar flow: f = 64/Re; Hagen–Poiseuille hf = 32 μ V L / (ρ g D²) — loss ∝ V.
  • Chezy's formula: V = C √(m i), hydraulic mean depth m = A/P = D/4 for a full pipe, i = hf/L.
  • Critical velocity: V = Rec ν / D (Rec = 2000). 0.25 stoke in 10 cm pipe → 2000 × 0.25×10⁻⁴ / 0.1 = 0.5 m/s.
Essential formulas — minor losses
  • Sudden enlargement: he = (V₁ − V₂)² / 2g. Diameter doubles → V₂ = V₁/4 → loss = (3/4)² V₁²/2g = 9/16 of the inlet velocity head.
  • Sudden contraction: hc = (1/Cc − 1)² V₂²/2g ≈ 0.5 V₂²/2g (when Cc is not given, use 0.5).
  • Entrance = 0.5 V²/2g · exit = V²/2g · bend / fitting = k V²/2g · obstruction = [A/(Cc(A − a)) − 1]² V²/2g
  • Equivalent length of a minor loss kV²/2g: Le = kD/f. Example: k = 5, D = 0.08 m, f = 0.04 → Le = 10 m.

4. Power transmitted through a pipe

Power at the outlet P = ρ g Q (H − hf). It is maximum when hf = H/3, and the maximum efficiency is (H − H/3)/H = 66.7 %. (2023 asked this twice.)

5. Water hammer

When a valve at the end of a long pipe is closed quickly, the moving column of water is stopped; its momentum turns into a sharp pressure rise that travels up and down the pipe as a wave (speed C ≈ 1400 m/s in water). It can burst the pipe. Surge tanks, slow-closing valves and relief valves protect against it.

Water hammer
  • Time for the wave to go and return: 2L/C.
  • Gradual closure (closing time t > 2L/C): p = ρ L V / t.
  • Sudden closure (t < 2L/C), rigid pipe: p = ρ V C.
  • 2023 Q93: L = 2500 m, V = 1.5 m/s, t = 25 s, C = 1460 m/s → 2L/C = 3.4 s < 25 s → gradual: p = 1000 × 2500 × 1.5/25 = 1.5 × 10⁵ N/m² = 15 N/cm².

6. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Check whether the given f is Darcy's f or the friction coefficient f′ (= f/4).
  • Sudden enlargement loss uses (V₁ − V₂)², not V₁² − V₂².
  • In the water-hammer formula, first compare t with 2L/C; then pick the formula.
  • In a horizontal pipe of constant diameter with only friction, the velocity head and datum head stay constant while pressure head falls (2023 Q86).

Topic 7: Boundary Layer, Drag and Lift

Syllabus line: Boundary layer — thickness, laminar and turbulent boundary layers, separation; drag and lift.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 11 questions
  • Length at which the boundary layer becomes turbulent (Q140); boundary layer thickness on a plate — 15.5 mm and 92.1 mm (Q145, Q146).
  • Momentum thickness for a linear profile u/U = y/δ — δ/6 (Q141); displacement thickness after separation increases (Q149).
  • Statement types: BL thickness vs Reynolds number (Q142), von Kármán momentum equation (Q144), turbulent flow (Q147), real fluid (Q148), laminar sub-layer and BL thickness definition (Q150).
  • Resistance of a flat plate moving normal to water, CD = 1.15 (Q143).

1. Basic idea

When fluid flows over a solid surface, the layer touching the surface sticks to it (no-slip, Topic 1). A little away from the surface the fluid moves at nearly the full stream velocity U. The thin region in between, where the velocity rises from 0 to 0.99U, is the boundary layer (Prandtl, 1904). Outside it, viscosity can be ignored; inside it, viscosity is everything.

Essential formulas — boundary layer on a flat plate
  • Local Reynolds number Rex = U x / ν; laminar up to about 5 × 10⁵ → transition distance x = 5×10⁵ ν / U.
  • Laminar (Blasius): δ = 5x / √Rex  ·  Turbulent: δ = 0.37 x / Rex1/5
  • δ grows along x; for a given x, δ/x decreases as Re increases (faster flow → thinner layer). So 'BL thickness increases with Re' is false (2023 Q142).
  • Displacement thickness δ* = ∫(1 − u/U)dy · momentum thickness θ = ∫(u/U)(1 − u/U)dy · energy thickness = ∫(u/U)(1 − u²/U²)dy
  • Linear profile u/U = y/δ: δ* = δ/2, θ = δ/6, energy thickness = δ/4. Shape factor H = δ*/θ (= 3 here; Blasius ≈ 2.59).
  • Von Kármán momentum integral: τ0 / (ρU²) = dθ/dx — gives skin-friction drag for laminar and turbulent layers.

2. Separation — why it matters

Where the outside flow slows down, pressure rises in the flow direction (adverse pressure gradient, dp/dx > 0). The slow fluid near the wall cannot push against it; it stops and turns back. At the separation point the wall velocity gradient (du/dy)y=0 = 0. Behind it forms a wide, eddying wake with low pressure — this causes large pressure (form) drag. Separation is delayed by streamlining, suction, slots, or making the layer turbulent (dimples on a golf ball). Displacement thickness increases after separation.

Drag and lift
  • Drag (along the flow): FD = CD A ρU²/2  ·  Lift (normal to the flow): FL = CL A ρU²/2
  • Total drag = skin-friction drag (shear on the surface) + pressure (form) drag (due to the wake). Streamlined body → mostly friction drag; bluff body → mostly pressure drag.
  • 2023 Q143: plate 1 m × 1 m normal to water at 5 m/s, CD = 1.15 → F = 1.15 × 1 × 1000 × 25/2 = 14,375 N.
  • Very slow flow past a sphere (Re < 1): Stokes' law FD = 3πμDV (settling of fine particles — sedimentation tanks).

3. Everyday analogy

Ride a bike: hold your palm flat to the wind and it is pushed back hard (bluff body, big wake → pressure drag). Turn the palm edge-on and the push almost disappears (streamlined → only skin friction). Tilt it slightly and it lifts up — that is lift.

4. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • BL thickness is defined at u = 0.99U (not 0.19U — a 2023 distractor).
  • The laminar sub-layer exists inside the turbulent boundary layer, not in a laminar one.
  • Turbulent BL is thicker and has higher skin friction, but it resists separation better — so it often reduces total drag on bluff bodies.

5. Connections

Topic 8: Impact of Jets and Hydraulic Turbines

Syllabus line: Impact of jets on vanes, velocity diagrams; turbines — Pelton, Francis, Kaplan, draft tube; performance — unit and specific quantities, characteristic curves, governing, cavitation.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 8 questions
  • Match turbines with specific speeds — Pelton (one jet) 10–35, Pelton (two jets) 35–60, Francis 60–300, Kaplan 300–1000 (Q121).
  • Power developed when moment of momentum falls by 12 kN·m at 600 rpm — 754 kW (Q123).
  • Statements: pressure in impulse vs reaction turbines (Q124); match nozzle size, pressure, velocity, cavitation erosion (Q125).
  • Work done per second by a jet on a series of flat plates (Q126); force of an oil jet on a stationary plate (Q127); force of a nozzle jet with Cv = 0.95 (Q128); match bucket, guide blade, volute, percent slip (Q139).

1. Basic idea

A jet of water has momentum. When it hits a plate or vane and changes direction, the plate pushes on the water and the water pushes back on the plate (Newton's third law). F = ρQ × (change of velocity). A turbine is simply many vanes on a wheel, arranged so that this force keeps turning a shaft.

Essential formulas — impact of jets (a = jet area, V = jet velocity, u = plate velocity)
  • Stationary flat plate, normal: F = ρ a V²  ·  inclined at θ to the jet: Fn = ρ a V² sin θ
  • Single moving flat plate: F = ρ a (V − u)² (the plate runs away from part of the water)
  • Series of flat plates on a wheel: F = ρ a V (V − u); work/s = ρ a V (V − u) u; maximum efficiency 50 % at u = V/2
  • Series of curved (semicircular) vanes: maximum efficiency 100 % in theory, at u = V/2 — why Pelton buckets are curved
  • Moment of momentum → torque T; power P = 2πNT/60. 2023 Q123: 2π × 600 × 12/60 = 754 kW.

Performance — unit and specific quantities
  • Specific speed (turbine) Ns = N √P / H5/4 (P in kW) — the speed of a geometrically similar turbine producing 1 kW under 1 m head. It decides the turbine type.
  • Unit quantities (under 1 m head): Nu = N/√H · Qu = Q/√H · Pu = P/H3/2 — used to predict performance at a different head.
  • Efficiencies: hydraulic ηh · mechanical ηm · overall ηo = ηh × ηm; shaft power P = ηo ρ g Q H.
  • Characteristic curves: main (constant head), operating (constant speed — the normal running condition), constant-efficiency (muschel) curves.

3. Draft tube and cavitation

The draft tube is a gradually widening pipe from the runner exit to the tail race. It (1) lets the turbine be placed above the tail-water level for easy maintenance without losing that head, and (2) recovers kinetic energy of the exit water by slowing it down. Because it creates suction below the runner, the pressure can drop to vapour pressure → cavitation (pitting and noise). It is checked by Thoma's cavitation factor σ.

4. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Higher head → lower specific speed (Pelton). Low head, large flow → high Ns (Kaplan).
  • Series of flat plates: 50 %. Series of curved vanes: up to 100 %. Both at u = V/2.
  • Guide blades belong to Francis; buckets to Pelton; volute casing to centrifugal pumps; percent slip to reciprocating pumps (2023 Q139).

Topic 9: Centrifugal Pumps and Hydraulic Control Valves

Syllabus line: Centrifugal pumps — work done, efficiencies, specific speed, NPSH; hydraulic control valves.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 10 questions
  • Power to drive a pump delivering 50 L/s against 40 m (Q129); a pump transfers energy from rotor to fluid (Q130).
  • Number of identical pumps needed for a deep well (Q131, Q136) — from specific speed.
  • Net head with radial outlet vanes, D = 100 mm, 3000 rpm, ηmano = 0.8 — 20 m (Q132); manometric efficiency (Q133).
  • Discharge and head when speed is halved (Q134); head with a different liquid (Q135); series/parallel statements (Q137); rotodynamic pump statements (Q138).

1. Basic idea

A centrifugal pump is a turbine in reverse: a motor spins an impeller; its curved vanes fling water outward. The water gains velocity and pressure (energy from rotor to fluid), is collected in a spiral volute casing that slowly widens to turn velocity into pressure, and leaves through the delivery pipe.

Essential formulas
  • Power required P = ρ g Q Hm / ηo. Example (2023 Q129): 1000 × 9.81 × 0.05 × 40 ≈ 19.6 kW (output power).
  • Radial vanes at outlet: Hm = ηmano × u₂²/g. 2023 Q132: u₂ = π × 0.1 × 3000/60 = 15.7 m/s → 0.8 × 15.7²/9.81 ≈ 20 m.
  • Specific speed (pump) Ns = N √Q / Hm3/4 (Q in m³/s). Number of pumps: in series if the total head is too large for one pump; in parallel if the discharge is too large.
  • Affinity laws (same pump, speed changed): Q ∝ N · H ∝ N² · P ∝ N³. Halve the speed → half the discharge, one-quarter the head, one-eighth the power.
  • Minimum starting speed: u₂²/2g − u₁²/2g = Hm (the centrifugal head must at least equal the manometric head).
  • NPSH (net positive suction head) available = head at suction above vapour pressure. Keep NPSHavailable > NPSHrequired, else cavitation.

2. Practical points

  • Priming: a centrifugal pump must be filled with liquid before starting — an impeller spinning in air cannot create enough suction (air is ~800 times lighter). A reciprocating pump is self-priming.
  • Series pumps → heads add (multistage pumps for high head, e.g. deep borewells). Parallel pumps → discharges add.
  • Reciprocating pump: positive displacement (piston). Slip = Qtheoretical − Qactual; percent slip; negative slip possible with long delivery pipes; air vessels smooth the flow and save work.

Topic 10: Hydro-Power Projects — Layouts, Storage, Dams and Spillways

Syllabus line: Hydro projects and power plants — layouts, pumped storage, hydrographs, storage and pondage, dams and spillways.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper II (verified): 1 question
  • Correct sequence of installations in the direction of flow in a hydropower plant (Q119).

Our analysis: only one question came from this area in 2023, but it is a full syllabus line, links to Paper 3 Civil (hydrology, dams) and Paper 3 Electrical (generating stations). Learn the layout, the role of each part and the definitions below.

1. Basic idea

A hydro-power plant turns the potential energy of stored water into electricity: P = η ρ g Q H. You need a head (height difference, created by a dam or a natural fall) and a discharge (river flow, regulated by storage).

PartJob
Dam / weirCreates head and storage

Surge tankAbsorbs water-hammer pressure rise on sudden load rejection; supplies extra water on sudden load increase

Draft tube + tail raceRecover exit energy and return water to the river

2. Classification of hydro plants

  • Run-of-river (no or little storage) — uses the river flow as it comes; with pondage it can store water for a few hours or days behind a small weir to meet daily peaks.
  • Storage plant — a large reservoir stores monsoon water for use through the dry months.
  • Pumped storage — two reservoirs at different levels. In off-peak hours surplus power pumps water up; in peak hours the water generates power. It consumes more energy than it gives back (about 70–80 % is recovered) but turns cheap off-peak power into valuable peak power. Reversible pump-turbines are common.
  • By head (commonly quoted): low head (< 30 m, Kaplan/propeller), medium head (30–300 m, Francis), high head (> 300 m, Pelton). Books differ slightly on the limits.

3. Hydrographs, storage and pondage

  • Hydrograph: graph of river discharge against time. Its area = volume of water.
  • Flow-duration curve: discharge against the percentage of time it is equalled or exceeded — tells the firm (dependable) power.
  • Mass curve (Rippl's method): cumulative inflow against time; tangents drawn parallel to the demand line give the storage capacity needed.
  • Storage = long-term (seasonal) holding of water in a reservoir; pondage = short-term (hours to a week) holding to follow load changes.

4. Dams and spillways

  • Dams: gravity (resists water by its own weight — masonry/concrete), arch (transfers load to the valley sides — narrow rocky gorges), buttress, earth and rock-fill (wide valleys, weak foundations).
  • Spillway: a safety valve that passes surplus flood water so it never overtops the dam. Types: ogee (overflow), chute, side-channel, shaft (morning-glory), siphon. Energy at the toe is destroyed by stilling basins (hydraulic jump), buckets.

5. Connections
