Topic 1: Fundamentals — Principles, Classification, Scales and Errors

Syllabus line: Classification of surveys, scales, accuracy.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question
  • Assertion–Reason on the principle of surveying: the paper wrote 'working from part to whole', which is wrong — the principle is whole to part, and working from part to whole magnifies errors. Key: both A and R are false (Q131).

1. Basic idea

Surveying is the art of finding the relative positions of points on, above or below the earth's surface by measuring distances, directions and elevations, and then showing them on a plan or map. A plan is drawn to a large scale (a building site); a map is drawn to a small scale (a district).

2. Why it is needed

No road, canal, building, pipeline or dam can be designed until the engineer knows the shape of the ground. Surveying gives that picture before construction, and sets out the design on the ground during construction.

3. How it works

Two principles govern every survey:

  1. Work from the whole to the part. First fix a few widely spaced control points very accurately; then fill in the details with less precise work. Errors stay small and local, and do not pile up.
  2. Fix every new point by at least two measurements from two known reference points (two distances, or a distance and an angle, or two angles).

Scales
  • Representative fraction (RF) = map distance ÷ ground distance, both in the same unit. 1 cm = 50 m → RF = 1/5000.
  • Plain scale reads two dimensions (metres and decimetres). Diagonal scale reads three (metres, decimetres, centimetres). Vernier scale reads a fraction of the smallest main division. Scale of chords sets out angles.
  • Least count of a vernier = smallest main-scale division ÷ number of vernier divisions. Direct vernier: n vernier divisions = (n − 1) main divisions.
  • Shrunk map: shrinkage factor = shrunk length ÷ original length; shrunk scale RF = shrinkage factor × original RF.
  • Measured with a wrong scale: correct length = (RF of wrong scale ÷ RF of correct scale) × measured length; for areas use the square of that ratio.

Accuracy means closeness to the true value; precision means closeness of repeated readings to each other. A wrongly calibrated tape can be precise yet inaccurate.

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a jigsaw puzzle. If you first fix the four corners and the border (whole) and then fill the inside (part), small misfits stay where they are. If you start from one inner piece and grow outward, a small tilt keeps growing until the border does not close.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Whole to part, never part to whole — the 2023 paper tested exactly this reversal.
  • Cumulative error ∝ L; compensating error ∝ √L.
  • A larger RF denominator means a smaller scale (1:50,000 is smaller than 1:5,000).
  • Diagonal scale works on similar triangles; it does not need a vernier.

6. Connections

Errors and their adjustment return in Topic 8. Scale returns in aerial photographs (Topic 9). Reconnaissance → preliminary → final location is also the order of highway surveys (2023 Q142, Transport module).

Topic 2: Measurement of Distances — Chain and Tape

Syllabus line: Measurement of distances — direct methods.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • Length across a river by similar triangles on a continuing chain line: PQ = 45 m (Q136, figure-based).
  • Error that can be classed as a compensating error: variation in pull (Q140).

1. Basic idea

In chain surveying only lengths are measured — no angles. The area is divided into a framework of triangles, because a triangle is the only figure that can be plotted from its sides alone.

2. Why it is used

It is the simplest and cheapest method. It suits small, fairly level, open areas with simple details. It is not suitable for large areas, crowded towns or hilly ground.

3. How it works

Lines of the framework: base line — the longest line, the backbone of the survey; check (proof) line — proves the accuracy of the framework; tie line — joins points on main lines to pick up interior details; offset — a short lateral measurement from the chain line to a detail. Triangles should be well-conditioned: no angle less than 30° or more than 120°.

Instruments for right angles: cross staff, optical square (two mirrors at 45°, works on double reflection) and prism square. Ranging means placing intermediate points on a straight line: direct ranging when the ends are intervisible, indirect (reciprocal) ranging when a hill hides them.

Wrong length of chain
  • True length = (L′ / L) × measured length, where L′ = actual length of the chain and L = its designated length.
  • True area = (L′ / L)² × measured area; true volume = (L′ / L)³ × measured volume.
  • Chain too long → it fits fewer times → measured distance is too small → correction is positive. Chain too short → correction negative.
Tape corrections
  • Temperature: Ct = α (Tm − T0) L — positive when the field temperature is above the standard.
  • Pull: Cp = (P − P0) L / (A E) — positive when the applied pull is more than the standard pull.
  • Sag: Cs = W² L / (24 P²), where W = weight of the tape between supports — always negative.
  • Slope: C = h² / (2L), or L (1 − cos θ) — always negative.
  • Reduction to mean sea level: C = L h / R — negative for points above sea level.
  • Normal tension is the pull at which the pull correction just cancels the sag correction.

4. Everyday analogy

A tailor's tape that has stretched reads less than the true cloth length, so the customer gets extra cloth. In the same way a chain that is too long gives a measured distance that is too small, and the correction must be added.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Chain too long → measured value too small → add. Students often reverse this.
  • Sag and slope corrections are always negative, whatever the conditions.
  • In the sag formula W is the total weight of the tape between supports; with weight per metre w, Cs = L (wL)² / 24P².
  • Variation in pull is compensating; a pull that is always higher than standard is cumulative.

6. Connections

Well-conditioned triangles return in triangulation (Topic 8). Slope and sea-level reductions are applied automatically by a total station (Topic 7).

Topic 3: Measurement of Directions — Compass Surveying

Syllabus line: Measurement of directions, prismatic compass, local attraction.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • The zero/360° graduation of a prismatic compass is marked at the south end (Q134).
  • For a whole circle bearing between 270° and 360°, reduced bearing = 360° − WCB (Q141).

1. Basic idea

A chain gives only lengths. To plot a line we also need its direction. The direction of a line is given by its bearing — the horizontal angle it makes with a reference direction called a meridian.

2. Why a compass

A freely suspended magnetic needle always settles along the magnetic meridian. So a compass gives the bearing of every line independently; an error in one line is not carried to the next. It suits traverses along roads, rivers and forest boundaries.

3. How it works

MeridianMeaning

Fore and back bearing: the fore bearing (FB) is taken in the direction of progress, the back bearing (BB) in the opposite direction. In the whole circle system BB = FB ± 180°. In the reduced system the angle stays the same and both letters are reversed (N 32° E ↔ S 32° W).

Why zero is at the south end: the observer reads the ring through the prism at his own end, which is diametrically opposite the object. When the line of sight points north, the south end of the ring is under the prism — so it must carry the zero.

Declination, dip and local attraction
  • Magnetic declination = angle between the true and magnetic meridians. True bearing = magnetic bearing + east declination (− west declination).
  • Isogonic lines join places of equal declination; the agonic line joins places of zero declination.
  • Dip = inclination of the needle to the horizontal: 0° at the magnetic equator, 90° at the magnetic poles. Isoclinic lines join places of equal dip; the aclinic line has zero dip. A sliding rider on the needle balances the dip.
  • Variation of declination: diurnal (daily), annual, secular (the largest, over centuries) and irregular (magnetic storms).
  • Local attraction — the needle is pulled aside by nearby iron, steel or electric cables. Test: if FB and BB of a line differ by exactly 180°, both its end stations are free. Start from such a line and correct the bearings at the affected stations.
  • Check for a closed traverse: sum of interior angles = (2n − 4) × 90°. Included angles are not affected by local attraction, because both bearings at a station are deflected by the same amount.

4. Everyday analogy

A compass near a motorcycle or a steel almirah points the wrong way — that is local attraction. Every bearing read at that spot is wrong by the same amount, like a wall clock that runs 10 minutes fast: each reading is wrong, but the difference between two readings is still right.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Prismatic: zero at south. Surveyor's: E and W interchanged. Do not mix the two.
  • East declination is added, west declination subtracted, to get the true bearing from the magnetic bearing.
  • Isogonic = equal declination; isoclinic = equal dip; agonic = zero declination; aclinic = zero dip.
  • Local attraction changes bearings, not included angles.

6. Connections

Bearings feed directly into latitude and departure calculations (Topic 4). The true meridian is found by astronomical observation (Topic 10).

Topic 4: Theodolite, Traversing and Computation of Coordinates

Syllabus line: Theodolites — types; establishment of control by traversing; computation of coordinates.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): no direct question in this shift
  • This paper (22 Aug 2023, Shift 2) had no question on the theodolite or traverse computations. They are named in the syllabus and are standard in other AEE papers, so learn them fully.

1. Basic idea

A theodolite measures horizontal and vertical angles precisely. A traverse is a chain of connected lines whose lengths and directions are measured. From these we compute the coordinates of every station.

2. Why it is needed

A compass reads only to 30′. Control points for a town, a canal or a bridge need angles correct to seconds. Coordinates let us plot each station independently, so a plotting error at one station is not carried forward.

3. How it works

Fundamental lines and their required relations
  • Axis of the plate level ⟂ vertical axis.
  • Line of collimation ⟂ horizontal (trunnion) axis.
  • Horizontal axis ⟂ vertical axis.
  • Axis of the altitude level ∥ line of collimation.
  • Temporary adjustments at every set-up: centring over the station → levelling → focusing the eyepiece → focusing the objective (removes parallax).

Measuring a horizontal angle: the repetition method adds the same angle several times on the circle and divides — used for one angle of high precision. The reiteration (direction) method measures several angles round a station in turn and closes the horizon.

Traverse: a closed traverse returns to its starting point or ends on another known point; an open traverse does not (roads, canals) and has no check. Angles may be measured as included angles, deflection angles or direct bearings.

Checks and balancing
  • Sum of interior angles = (2n − 4) × 90°; sum of exterior angles = (2n + 4) × 90°; algebraic sum of deflection angles = 360°.
  • Bowditch (compass) rule: correction to the latitude (or departure) of a line = total error in latitude (or departure) × length of that line ÷ perimeter, with opposite sign. Assumes angles and lengths are measured with equal precision. Most commonly used.
  • Transit rule: correction to latitude = total error × latitude of that line ÷ arithmetic sum of all latitudes. Used when angles are more precise than lengths.
  • Consecutive coordinates = latitude and departure of each line. Independent coordinates = total latitude and total departure of each station from a common origin: NB = NA + LAB, EB = EA + DAB.

4. Everyday analogy

Walk round a field and come back to the tree you started from: the total distance you moved north must equal the total you moved south, and the same for east and west. If your notes do not balance, the small gap is the closing error.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Latitude uses cos, departure uses sin — when θ is measured from the north–south line.
  • Bowditch → proportional to length; Transit → proportional to the latitude or departure itself.
  • Changing face does not remove the error of a vertical axis that is not truly vertical.
  • Repetition suits one angle; reiteration suits many angles at one station.

6. Connections

Coordinates are the output of a total station (Topic 7) and of GPS (Topic 10). The closing-error idea is used again in levelling (Topic 5) and in the adjustment of triangulation (Topic 8).

Syllabus line: Measurement of elevations, spirit and trigonometric levelling.

Topic 6: Contours and Digital Elevation Modelling

Syllabus line: Contours, digital elevation modelling concept.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): no direct question in this shift
  • Not asked in this paper. Contour characteristics are a standard one-mark area in AEE and AE papers, and 'digital elevation modelling' is named in the syllabus.

1. Basic idea

A contour is an imaginary line joining points of equal elevation. Imagine a hill standing in a lake: each time the water level rises by one metre, the new shoreline is a contour. Drawn on a map, contours show the shape of the ground in three dimensions on a flat sheet.

2. Why it is needed

From a contour map an engineer can pick a road or canal alignment at a chosen gradient, find a reservoir's capacity, mark a catchment area, compute earthwork and check whether two points are intervisible — all without going back to the field.

3. How it works

Two key terms
  • Contour interval — the vertical distance between two consecutive contours. It is kept constant on a map.
  • Horizontal equivalent — the horizontal distance between two consecutive contours. It varies with the slope.
  • Gradient between two contours = contour interval ÷ horizontal equivalent.
  • The contour interval is chosen smaller for a larger map scale, flatter ground, more important work, and when more time and money are available.

Characteristics of contours

  • Every contour closes on itself, inside or outside the map.
  • Contours of different elevations never cross, except at an overhanging cliff or cave; they merge into one line at a vertical cliff.
  • Close spacing = steep slope; wide spacing = gentle slope; equal spacing = uniform slope; straight, parallel and equally spaced = a plane surface.
  • Contours cross ridge lines and valley lines at right angles.
  • A saddle (pass) lies between two hills, with higher ground on two opposite sides and lower ground on the other two.

Digital elevation modelling — the concept
  • A digital elevation model (DEM) stores ground elevation as numbers a computer can process, instead of as lines drawn on paper.
  • Raster (grid) DEM — a matrix of square cells, each holding one elevation. Simple; the cell size fixes the detail.
  • TIN (triangulated irregular network) — irregularly spaced points joined into non-overlapping triangles; more points where the ground is rough.
  • DSM (digital surface model) includes the tops of buildings and trees; DTM (digital terrain model) is the bare earth.
  • Sources: ground survey (total station, GPS), photogrammetry, LiDAR, radar interferometry (SRTM), satellite stereo images (CartoDEM from Cartosat).
  • Products: slope and aspect maps, contours, watershed and drainage delineation, cut-and-fill volumes, visibility analysis, flood mapping.

4. Everyday analogy

Slice a boiled potato into layers of equal thickness and trace each slice on paper, one inside the other: you have drawn its contour map. Thin, crowded outlines mark the steep side of the potato.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Valley: V-shaped, apex towards higher ground. Ridge: U-shaped, bend towards lower ground.
  • Contour interval is constant on one map; horizontal equivalent is not.
  • Contours merge at a vertical cliff; they cross only at an overhang.
  • DSM includes buildings and trees; DTM does not.

6. Connections

Contour gradient → highway and canal alignment (Transport and Irrigation modules). Area enclosed by contours → reservoir capacity (Hydrology). DEMs come from photogrammetry and remote sensing (Topics 9 and 10).

Topic 7: Indirect Distance Measurement — Tacheometry, EDM and Total Station

Syllabus line: Measurement of distances — indirect methods, optical and electronic devices.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question
  • Statement–conclusion on stadia tacheometry: the paper swapped the descriptions of the fixed-hair and movable-hair methods, so neither conclusion was correct (Q133).

1. Basic idea

Tacheometry finds horizontal distances and differences of level from instrument readings alone, without a chain. The telescope has two extra horizontal hairs (stadia hairs); the length of staff seen between them, the staff intercept, grows in proportion to the distance.

2. Why it is needed

On broken, hilly or marshy ground, and across rivers and ravines, chaining is slow or impossible. Tacheometry is fast and good enough for contouring and preliminary route surveys (accuracy about 1 in 1000). Electronic distance measurement has now taken over for precise work.

3. How it works

Tacheometric formulas
  • Horizontal line of sight: D = k s + C. With an anallactic lens the additive constant C becomes zero, so D = 100 s.
  • Inclined sight, staff vertical: D = k s cos²θ + C cos θ and vertical component V = (k s sin 2θ)/2 + C sin θ.
  • RL of staff station = RL of instrument axis ± V − central hair reading (+ for an angle of elevation, − for depression).
  • Subtense bar: D = (b/2) cot(α/2) ≈ b/α (α in radians, b = length of bar).
Electronic distance measurement (EDM)
  • Principle: the instrument sends a modulated electromagnetic wave to a reflector and measures the phase difference between the outgoing and returning wave. Distance = whole number of half wavelengths + the fraction from the phase difference.
  • Microwave instruments (Tellurometer) — long range, up to about 100 km; need an instrument at both ends.
  • Visible-light instruments (Geodimeter) — medium range, about 25 km at night.
  • Infrared instruments (Distomat) — short range, about 2–5 km; the kind built into total stations.
  • Corrections: atmospheric (temperature and pressure change the wave velocity), prism constant, slope and height.

Total station. It measures three things — horizontal angle, vertical angle and slope distance — and computes the rest: horizontal distance, difference of level, and the coordinates (northing, easting, elevation) of each point. Data are stored digitally and downloaded to a computer. It is used for traversing, topographic detail, setting out, and remote height and missing-line measurement. Reflectorless models measure to a wall or rock face without a prism.

4. Everyday analogy

Hold your thumb at arm's length against a distant building. The farther the building, the more floors your thumb covers. The stadia hairs are a fixed "thumb" inside the telescope, and the staff is the building with floors marked on it.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Fixed hair: interval fixed, intercept varies. Movable hair: intercept fixed, interval varies. The 2023 paper tested this by swapping them.
  • The anallactic lens makes the additive constant zero; it does not change the multiplying constant.
  • For an inclined sight D uses cos²θ, V uses sin 2θ / 2.
  • EDM measures the slope distance; the horizontal distance is computed.

6. Connections

Tacheometric radial lines are one indirect method of contouring (Topic 6). EDM made trilateration practical (Topic 8). Total-station coordinates use the latitude–departure idea of Topic 4.

Topic 8: Triangulation and Adjustment of Observations

Syllabus line: Establishment of control by triangulation; measurement and adjustment of observations.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): no direct question in this shift
  • Not asked in this paper. The syllabus names both triangulation and adjustment of observations, so the classification, the figures and the rules for most probable values must be known.

1. Basic idea

In triangulation the area is covered by a network of triangles. Only one side (the base line) is measured very accurately; all the angles are measured; every other side is then computed by trigonometry (sine rule). The corners become control points for all later surveys.

2. Why it is needed

Before EDM, measuring a long line precisely was far harder than measuring an angle. Triangulation carried accurate control across a whole country from a few measured base lines — the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India was done this way. Because no measurement is perfect, the observations must then be adjusted so that they agree with geometry.

3. How it works

Field terms: a well-conditioned triangle has no angle below 30° or above 120° (the best shape is isosceles with base angles of about 56°). Signals mark stations for sighting — opaque (poles, targets) or luminous (the heliotrope reflects sunlight; lamps at night). A satellite (eccentric) station is used when the instrument cannot be set over the true station, such as a church spire; the readings are then reduced to centre. Trilateration measures the sides with EDM instead of the angles.

Laws of accidental errors (normal distribution)
  • Small errors occur more often than large ones.
  • Positive and negative errors of the same size are equally likely.
  • Very large errors do not occur.
Most probable value and weights
  • The most probable value (MPV) of a quantity measured several times with equal care is the arithmetic mean.
  • With unequal reliability each observation gets a weight; MPV = Σ(w x) / Σw (the weighted mean). Weight is inversely proportional to the variance (square of the probable error).
  • Principle of least squares: the MPV is the value for which the sum of the squares of the residuals (weighted) is a minimum.
  • Probable error of a single observation = 0.6745 √[Σv² / (n − 1)]; of the mean = 0.6745 √[Σv² / n(n − 1)].
  • Weight of the mean of n observations of unit weight = n. If a quantity of weight w is multiplied by k, the weight becomes w/k². For a sum or difference, 1/w = 1/w₁ + 1/w₂.
Adjusting angles
  • Station adjustment: the angles closing the horizon at a station must add up to 360°.
  • Figure adjustment: the three angles of a plane triangle must add up to 180° (plus the spherical excess for large triangles, about 1″ per 196 km²).
  • Equal weights → distribute the error equally. Unequal weights → distribute the error in inverse proportion to the weights (the less reliable angle takes the larger correction).

4. Everyday analogy

Three friends measure the same table as 150.2, 150.4 and 150.6 cm. The fairest single answer is the mean, 150.4 cm. If one friend used a steel rule and is twice as reliable, his reading is counted twice — that is a weight of 2.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Triangulation: angles measured, one side measured. Trilateration: sides measured.
  • Braced quadrilaterals are the strongest figure; a single chain of triangles is the weakest.
  • Corrections go in inverse proportion to the weights, not in direct proportion.
  • Residual = observed value − most probable value; true error = observed value − true value (never exactly known).

6. Connections

Topic 9: Plane Tabling, Photogrammetry and Map Substitutes

Syllabus line: Map preparation by plane tabling and by photogrammetry; map substitutes.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • Scale of an aerial photograph: f = 200 mm, flying height 2700 m, ground elevation 200 m → 1 in 12,500 (Q137).
  • Flight planning, statement–conclusion: 200 mm × 200 mm photo at 1 : 10,000 covers 4 km² (not 9.6 km²), and 192 km² needs 200 photos (not 20) → neither conclusion correct (Q139).

1. Basic idea

Both methods produce a map without first writing long field books. On a plane table the map is drawn in the field while looking at the ground. In photogrammetry the ground is photographed (usually from the air) and the measurements are made on the photographs.

2. Why they are needed

Plane tabling is quick and cheap for small-scale detail work, and nothing is forgotten because the surveyor sees the ground while plotting. Photogrammetry maps large or inaccessible areas — forests, hills, flood plains — in a fraction of the time of ground survey.

3. How it works

Plane tabling

Accessories: drawing board on a tripod, alidade (plain or telescopic) for sighting and ruling, spirit level, trough compass for marking north, and plumbing fork (U-fork) with plumb bob for centring. Setting up means levelling, centring and orientation — keeping the board parallel to its position at the first station. Orientation by back-sighting is more reliable than by trough compass.

MethodWhat is doneUsed for
RadiationRays drawn from one station to all points; distances measured and scaled offSmall areas, all points visible from one station
IntersectionRays drawn to the same point from two stations at the ends of a measured base; the crossing fixes the pointDistant or inaccessible points (across a river)
TraversingTable moved from station to station, like a compass traverseNarrow strips — roads, rivers
ResectionFinding on the sheet the position of the station occupied by the table, by sighting known pointsFixing new instrument stations

Resection problems. Two-point problem: the station is found from two plotted points. Three-point problem: from three plotted points — solved by the mechanical (tracing paper) method, the graphical (Bessel's) method or by trial and error (Lehmann's rules, which shrink the triangle of error to a point). The solution fails when the station lies on the circle passing through the three points (the great circle).

Photogrammetry

Aerial photographs — key facts
  • Vertical photograph: camera axis vertical (tilt under about 3°). Oblique: axis deliberately tilted — high oblique shows the horizon, low oblique does not.
  • Principal point — foot of the perpendicular from the lens on the photograph. Nadir (plumb) point — point vertically below the lens. On a truly vertical photograph they coincide.
  • Relief displacement is radial from the nadir point: tops of tall objects are displaced outwards. Height of an object h = d H′ / r.
  • Overlap: longitudinal (forward) about 60 % between successive photographs of a strip, so that every point appears on two photographs and can be seen in 3-D; side (lateral) about 30 % between adjacent strips.
  • Flight planning: ground side covered by one photograph = photo size ÷ scale. Net new ground per photograph = (1 − Pl) L × (1 − Pw) W. Number of photographs = total area ÷ net area.
  • 2023 Q139 worked: 0.2 m × 10,000 = 2 km each way → 4 km² per photograph; net = (0.4 × 2) × (0.6 × 2) = 0.96 km²; 192 / 0.96 = 200 photographs.
  • Stereoscopy: an overlapping pair viewed under a stereoscope gives a 3-D model; heights are found from the difference in parallax measured with a parallax bar.

Map substitutes

When a proper line map is not available or would take too long, photographic products are used in its place. These are called map substitutes.

Map substituteWhat it is
MosaicSeveral overlapping aerial photographs joined into one continuous picture. Uncontrolled — simply matched by detail; controlled — photographs rectified and fitted to ground control points; semi-controlled — in between
PhotomapA mosaic or single photograph with grid lines, place names, a scale and a north arrow added
Orthophoto / orthophotomapA photograph from which tilt and relief displacement have been removed, so it has a uniform scale like a map
Photo indexA small-scale assembly of prints showing which photograph covers which area

4. Everyday analogy

Take a photograph of a street from a balcony: the nearby wall looks larger than the far end of the street. In an aerial photograph hilltops are the "nearby wall" — larger scale and pushed outward from the centre.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Use H − h, not H, in the scale formula; and keep f and H in the same unit.
  • In flight planning, first find the ground side of one photograph; do not multiply the two overlaps together directly.
  • Intersection needs a measured base line; radiation needs every distance measured.
  • Lehmann's rules belong to the trial-and-error solution of the three-point problem.

6. Connections

Stereo photographs are a main source of DEMs and contours (Topic 6). Satellite images extend the same idea from aircraft to space (Topic 10).

Topic 10: Remote Sensing, GPS and Field Astronomy

Syllabus line: Remote sensing concepts; concept of global positioning system; field astronomy.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • Match regions of the electromagnetic spectrum with wavelengths: visible 0.4–0.7 µm; near and mid infrared 0.7–3 µm; thermal infrared 3–14 µm; microwave 1 mm–1 m (Q135).
  • Spectral resolution indicates the wavelengths to which the sensor is sensitive (Q138).

1. Basic idea

Remote sensing is collecting information about an object without touching it — in practice, recording the electromagnetic energy reflected or emitted by the earth's surface from an aircraft or satellite. GPS gives the position of a receiver anywhere on earth from satellite signals. Field astronomy uses the sun and stars to find true north, latitude, longitude and time.

A. Remote sensing

2. Why it is needed

A satellite sees a whole district in one image and returns every few days. That makes it the fastest way to map land use, floods, reservoirs, crops, forests and urban growth, and to choose routes for roads and canals.

3. How it works

Stages: energy source (sun) → passage through the atmosphere → interaction with the target → recording by the sensor → transmission and processing → interpretation → application.

ResolutionMeaningExample
SpatialSize of the smallest object that can be distinguished — the ground size of one pixelLISS-IV: about 5.8 m; Cartosat: 2.5 m and finer
SpectralThe wavelengths to which the sensor is sensitive — number and width of its bandsMultispectral: a few broad bands; hyperspectral: hundreds of narrow bands
RadiometricSensitivity to small differences in energy — number of grey levels (bits)8-bit = 256 levels; 10-bit = 1024 levels
TemporalHow often the same area is imaged again (revisit time)A few days to a few weeks
More remote-sensing facts
  • Passive sensors record sunlight reflected or heat emitted by the earth (cameras, multispectral scanners). Active sensors send their own energy and record the return (radar, LiDAR) — they work at night and through cloud.
  • Spectral signature: each surface reflects differently in each band. Healthy vegetation absorbs red and reflects strongly in the near infrared; clear water absorbs near infrared and looks dark.
  • NDVI = (NIR − Red)/(NIR + Red) — a measure of green vegetation.
  • Standard false colour composite: near infrared shown as red, red as green, green as blue — healthy vegetation appears red.
  • Scattering: Rayleigh (particles much smaller than the wavelength; makes the sky blue), Mie (particles about the size of the wavelength), non-selective (larger particles; makes clouds white). Atmospheric windows are the bands that pass through the atmosphere with little loss.
  • Orbits: sun-synchronous near-polar orbits at about 700–900 km for earth observation (IRS, Resourcesat, Cartosat); geostationary orbit at about 36,000 km for weather and communication (INSAT).
  • Elements of visual interpretation: tone, texture, shape, size, pattern, shadow, site and association.

B. Global Positioning System

GPS — the concept
  • Three segments: space segment (the satellites), control segment (ground stations that track and correct them) and user segment (receivers).
  • Constellation: nominally 24 satellites in 6 orbital planes inclined at 55°, at an altitude of about 20,200 km, each completing an orbit in about 12 hours.
  • Principle: the receiver measures the travel time of the signal from each satellite, converts it into a distance (range), and fixes its position by trilateration.
  • At least 4 satellites are needed: three for the position (X, Y, Z) and one more to remove the error of the receiver's clock.
  • Signals are broadcast on the L-band (L1 and L2 carriers). Positions are given on the WGS-84 datum.
  • Errors: satellite clock and orbit (ephemeris) errors, ionospheric and tropospheric delay, multipath (reflected signals), receiver noise, and poor satellite geometry (high DOP).
  • DGPS: a reference receiver on a known point computes the errors and sends corrections to nearby receivers, improving accuracy from several metres to a metre or better. RTK gives centimetre accuracy.
  • Other systems: GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (Europe), BeiDou (China), and India's regional system NavIC (IRNSS); GAGAN is India's augmentation system for aircraft.

Rules to remember
  • Altitude of the celestial pole = latitude of the observer.
  • Astronomical triangle: formed by the pole, the zenith and the star; its sides are the co-latitude, the zenith distance (co-altitude) and the polar distance (co-declination).
  • A star is circumpolar (never sets) when its declination is greater than the co-latitude: δ > 90° − φ.
  • Upper culmination of a star north of the zenith: zenith distance = δ − φ. Lower culmination: altitude = φ + δ − 90°.
  • Time: 360° of longitude = 24 hours → 15° = 1 hour, 1° = 4 minutes. Places to the east have later local time. Indian Standard Time is the local mean time of longitude 82°30′ E = GMT + 5 h 30 min.
  • A sidereal day (23 h 56 min 4 s of mean solar time) is about 4 minutes shorter than a mean solar day.
  • Equation of time = apparent solar time − mean solar time; it varies through the year up to about 16 minutes and is zero four times a year.
  • Corrections to an observed altitude: refraction (subtract), parallax (add), semi-diameter (for the sun), dip (at sea).

4. Everyday analogy

Remote sensing is like a doctor reading an X-ray instead of opening the body. GPS is like finding where you are in a dark hall by listening to four friends who shout the exact time from known corners — the delay of each voice tells you how far each friend is.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Spatial = detail; spectral = wavelengths; radiometric = energy sensitivity; temporal = revisit frequency. The 2023 options were exactly these four.
  • GPS needs four satellites, not three, for a 3-D fix.
  • GPS satellites are at about 20,200 km — not geostationary (36,000 km).
  • A sidereal day is shorter than a solar day; east is ahead in time.

6. Connections

Satellite images are processed like aerial photographs (Topic 9) and yield DEMs (Topic 6). GPS coordinates replace triangulation control (Topic 8). The true meridian found by astronomy is the reference for bearings (Topic 3).
