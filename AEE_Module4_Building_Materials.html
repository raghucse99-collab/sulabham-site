Topic 1: Timber — Structure, Species, Moisture and Strength

Syllabus line: Timber: different types and species of structural timber, density–moisture relationship, strength in different directions.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • Statement with two conclusions on the fibre saturation point: above it, the properties of wood do not change with moisture (conclusion 1, true); the paper then claimed shrinkage is greatest in the longitudinal direction (false — it is the least). Key: only conclusion 1 (Q15).
  • Methods of finding the moisture content of wood under IS 11215: oven drying, distillation and electrical moisture meter are valid; the 'torsion balance moisture meter' is not a method (Q19).

1. Basic idea

Timber is wood that is fit for engineering use. A standing tree gives standing timber; a felled trunk is rough timber (a log); sawn to market sizes it is converted timber. Wood is made of long hollow cells (fibres) running along the trunk, like a tight bundle of drinking straws. Almost every property of timber follows from this one fact.

2. Why it matters

Timber is light, easily worked, strong for its weight and a good insulator, so it is used for doors, formwork, scaffolding, roof trusses, piles and sleepers. Its weakness is that it changes with moisture and is attacked by fungi and insects — which is what the exam asks about.

IS 883 places structural timbers in three groups by modulus of elasticity: Group A — E above 12.6 kN/mm²; Group B — 9.8 to 12.6 kN/mm²; Group C — 5.6 to 9.8 kN/mm².

Moisture in wood
  • Moisture content = (wet weight − oven-dry weight) ÷ oven-dry weight × 100. It is a percentage of the dry weight, so green timber can have more than 100 %.
  • Water is held in two ways: free water inside the cell cavities, and bound water soaked into the cell walls.
  • Fibre saturation point (FSP): the cell walls are fully saturated but the cavities hold no free water — about 25 to 30 %.
  • Above FSP only free water changes, so strength and size stay constant. Below FSP the walls dry: the wood shrinks and its strength rises.
  • Shrinkage: tangential (along the rings) is the largest, radial about half of it, longitudinal the least (almost nil).
  • Density rises with moisture, so it is quoted at a standard 12 % moisture. Denser timber is generally stronger.
  • Methods of finding moisture (IS 11215): oven-drying (standard), distillation (for oily or treated timber), electrical moisture meter (quick, in the field).

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a bundle of drinking straws tied together. Pull or push along the straws and the bundle is strong. Squeeze it from the side and the straws flatten; try to pull them apart sideways and they simply separate. That is why timber is strong along the grain and weak across it. A wet sponge works for moisture: squeezing out the loose water (free water) does not change its size, but once the sponge material itself dries (bound water) it shrinks and stiffens.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Softwood and hardwood are botanical terms, not a measure of hardness. Balsa is a hardwood.
  • Shrinkage order: tangential > radial > longitudinal. The 2023 paper reversed it.
  • Strength changes only below the FSP. Above it, wetter wood is heavier but not weaker.
  • Heartwood gives structural timber; sapwood is weaker and decays first.
  • Moisture content is on the oven-dry weight, not the wet weight.

6. Connections

Shrinkage below FSP is the cause of the seasoning defects in Topic 2. Anisotropy explains why plywood (Topic 2) crosses the grain of alternate layers. Moisture content on dry weight is defined the same way for soils (Soil Mechanics module).

Topic 2: Timber — Defects, Seasoning, Preservation and Plywood

Syllabus line: Timber: defects, preservations, and plywood.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question
  • Identify the defect from its description — radial splits wide at the circumference and narrowing towards the centre, caused by severe frost or fierce sun: star shake (Q14).

1. Basic idea

A defect is anything that lowers the strength, durability or appearance of timber. Defects come from five sources: the growing tree, drying (seasoning), sawing (conversion), fungi and insects. Seasoning is controlled drying of timber. Preservation is treatment with chemicals so that fungi and insects cannot live on it.

2. Why it matters

A freshly felled tree may hold more than half its weight as water. If it is used as it is, it shrinks, warps and rots in the structure. Seasoning and preservation turn a perishable material into a durable one.

Seasoning
  • Objects: reduce weight, shrinkage and warping; increase strength, stiffness and durability; make the timber fit for paint, glue and preservatives; make it safe from fungi.
  • Natural seasoning — air seasoning: timber stacked under a shed on a raised platform with gaps for air; cheap and gentle but slow (months), and moisture cannot be brought much below about 15–18 %. Water seasoning: logs kept in flowing water for 2–4 weeks to wash out sap, then air dried.
  • Artificial seasoning — kiln seasoning (temperature, humidity and air flow controlled; days instead of months; any moisture content), boiling, chemical (salt) seasoning, electrical seasoning.
  • Seasoned timber for general work has about 10 to 12 % moisture, close to the equilibrium moisture content of the place where it will be used.

Plywood and other wood products
  • Veneer — thin sheet (0.4 to 6 mm) peeled from a rotating log (rotary cutting) or sliced.
  • Plywood — an odd number of veneers (3, 5, 7 …) glued under pressure with the grain of each layer at right angles to the next. The outer layers are face plies, the middle is the core.
  • Why it is better than plain wood: nearly equal strength in both directions, very little shrinkage or swelling, does not split when nailed near the edge, available in large sheets, can be bent to shape.
  • Block board / batten board / lamin board — core of wooden strips (up to 25 mm, up to 80 mm and up to 7 mm wide) between veneers.
  • Fibre board — pressed wood or vegetable fibre; particle (chip) board — wood chips bonded with resin; hardboard — dense fibre board.

4. Everyday analogy

Seasoning is like drying red chillies or papad: dried slowly in shade they stay flat and whole; dried too fast in harsh sun the skin sets first and they crack and curl — that is case hardening and warping. Plywood is like a woven mat: single reeds bend easily one way, but woven at right angles they resist bending both ways.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Heart shake is widest at the centre; star shake is widest at the circumference. The 2023 question gave the description, not the name.
  • Dry rot needs dampness and no ventilation — the name refers to the dry powder it leaves, not to dry conditions.
  • Plywood always has an odd number of plies so that both faces have the same grain direction and the sheet stays balanced.
  • ASCU ratio is 1 : 3 : 4 — arsenic, copper, chromium, in that order.
  • Full-cell = Bethell; empty-cell = Lowry and Rueping.

6. Connections

Seasoning defects are the visible result of unequal shrinkage (Topic 1). Formwork for concrete (Topic 9) is made of plywood because it does not warp. Fungal decay needs moisture, air and warmth — the same three that corrode steel.

Topic 3: Bricks — Earth, Manufacture, Defects, IS Classification and Tests

Syllabus line: Bricks: types, Indian standard classification, absorption, saturation factor.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions
  • Match the proportions of brick ingredients. The official key took: lime less than 7 %, ferric oxide 10 %, silica 50–60 %, magnesia less than 1 % (Q10). Textbooks (Rangwala) give lime up to about 5 % and iron oxide 5–6 %; in a matching question, fix silica and magnesia first.
  • The deformation of the shape of bricks caused by rain water falling on hot bricks is called chuffs (Q12).

1. Basic idea

A brick is a block of clay that has been moulded, dried and burnt at about 900–1100 °C so that the clay particles fuse and become hard and permanent. Its small, uniform size lets one hand lift it, which is why brick masonry is the most common walling.

2. Why the ingredients matter

Good brick earth is a balance: alumina makes the clay plastic so that it can be moulded, and silica stops it from shrinking and cracking as it dries. Too much of either spoils the brick.

3. How it works

Harmful ingredients: lumps of lime, iron pyrites (decompose and split the brick), alkalis (cause efflorescence — white salt deposits), pebbles and stones, and organic matter (leaves the brick porous).

Manufacture in four stages
  • 1. Preparation of clay: unsoiling (remove top 20 cm) → digging → cleaning → weathering (exposed for weeks) → blending → tempering (kneading with water, in a pug mill).
  • 2. Moulding: hand moulding (ground-moulded or table-moulded) or machine moulding (plastic clay or dry clay). The mould is made 8–12 % larger to allow for shrinkage. The frog is the 10–20 mm deep depression on the top face: it forms a key for mortar and carries the maker's mark.
  • 3. Drying: to about 2 % moisture, so that the bricks do not crack in the kiln.
  • 4. Burning: in a clamp (temporary, cheap, poor control) or a kiln — intermittent kilns, or continuous kilns: Bull's trench kiln (most common in India), Hoffmann's kiln (roofed, works in all seasons) and the tunnel kiln.
  • Standard (modular) size: 190 × 90 × 90 mm; with mortar 200 × 100 × 100 mm, so 500 bricks per m³ of masonry. Traditional size is about 230 × 114 × 76 mm.

Indian Standard classification and tests (IS 1077, IS 3495)
  • IS 1077 classifies common burnt clay bricks by average compressive strength: classes 3.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 17.5, 20, 25, 30 and 35 (the number is the strength in N/mm²).
  • Water absorption after 24 hours in cold water: not more than 20 % for classes up to 12.5, and not more than 15 % for higher classes. Absorption = (wet − dry)/dry × 100.
  • Efflorescence: rated nil, slight (up to 10 % of the area), moderate (up to 50 %), heavy and serious. Not more than moderate up to class 12.5 and slight above.
  • Compressive strength test: frog filled flush with 1 : 3 cement mortar, brick soaked, load applied on the flat face at 14 N/mm² per minute.
  • Dimension tolerance: 20 bricks laid end to end must measure 3800 ± 80 mm in length and 1800 ± 40 mm in width and height.
  • Saturation coefficient (C/B ratio) = absorption after 24 hours in cold water ÷ absorption after 5 hours of boiling. It shows how much of the pore space fills easily. The lower it is, the more empty space is left for freezing water to expand, so the brick is more durable; values lie between about 0.4 and 0.95, and below about 0.8 is good.
  • Field tests: two bricks struck together give a clear metallic ring; a finger nail leaves no scratch; a brick dropped flat from 1 m does not break; a fractured face is uniform, with no lumps or holes.

4. Everyday analogy

Making bricks is like making chapati dough into something permanent. Too much fine flour (alumina) and the dough cracks as it dries; a little coarse flour (silica) holds the shape. Baking fixes it for ever — and sprinkling water on a hot tava makes it spit and distort, which is what rain does to hot bricks (chuffs).

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Alumina → plasticity; silica → prevents shrinkage. Questions swap these.
  • Chuffs (rain on hot bricks) and bloating (excess carbon and sulphur) are both shape defects with different causes.
  • IS 1077 classes are by strength; "first, second, third class" is the traditional classification by quality.
  • A lower saturation coefficient is better. Do not read it like a strength.
  • Efflorescence is caused by alkali salts, not by lime.

6. Connections

Brick strength and mortar strength together decide masonry strength (Topic 4). Water absorption is the reason bricks are soaked before laying: a dry brick sucks water out of the mortar (Topic 7). Fly-ash bricks use the pozzolanic reaction explained under cement (Topic 5).

Topic 4: Strength of Brick Masonry, Influence of Mortar — and Building Stones

Syllabus line: Bricks: strength in masonry, influence of mortar strength on masonry strength. Building stones are not named in the syllabus but were asked in 2023, so they are added here.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question
  • Match the property of stone with its test: hardness → Mohs scale; toughness → resistance to hammering; weather resistance → acid test; frost resistance → Brard's test (Q13).

1. Basic idea

Masonry is an assembly of units (bricks or stones) bonded with mortar. It is a composite, and its strength is not the strength of the brick. A wall of 10 N/mm² bricks may safely carry only a fraction of that.

2. Why masonry is weaker than its bricks

Mortar is softer than brick. Under vertical load the mortar in a joint tries to squeeze out sideways. Friction and bond make it drag the brick outwards with it, so the brick is put into sideways tension while it is being compressed. Brick is weak in tension, so the wall fails by vertical splitting of the bricks, long before the bricks would crush.

3. How it works

Rules to remember (IS 1905 — structural use of unreinforced masonry)
  • The basic compressive stress of masonry is read from a table against two things: the crushing strength of the unit and the type of mortar (H1, H2, M1, M2, M3, L1, L2).
  • Typical cement–sand mortars: H1 1 : 3 (about 10 N/mm²), H2 1 : 4 (7.5), M1 1 : 5 (5), M2 1 : 6 (3), M3 1 : 7 (1.5).
  • The basic stress is then multiplied by reduction factors for slenderness and eccentricity, and by area and shape factors.
  • Masonry strength is commonly only about 25 to 50 % of the brick strength.
  • A useful design rule: mortar should be no stronger than needed. A slightly weaker mortar accommodates small movements and confines cracking to the joints, which are easy to repair.
  • Bonds: English bond (alternate courses of headers and stretchers — strongest); Flemish bond (headers and stretchers alternate in each course — better appearance). A queen closer is a brick cut lengthwise in half, placed next to the quoin header to break the vertical joints.

Topic 5: Cement — Compounds, Hydration and Types

Syllabus line: Cement: compounds of different types.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 0 questions in this shift
  • No direct question on cement chemistry came in this paper. The compounds and types below are among the most repeated facts in civil engineering exams, so study them fully.

1. Basic idea

Cement is a fine grey powder that reacts chemically with water and hardens into a stone-like mass. The reaction is called hydration. Cement does not "dry" hard — it needs water to harden, and it hardens even under water, so it is a hydraulic binder. Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) was patented by Joseph Aspdin in 1824.

2. Why the compounds matter

Cement is not one chemical. It is a mixture of four compounds, each with its own speed, strength and heat. By changing their proportions the manufacturer makes rapid-hardening, low-heat or sulphate-resisting cement. If you know what each compound does, you can work out every "type of cement" question.

3. How it works

Manufacture in one line
  • Limestone (lime) + clay or shale (silica, alumina, iron oxide) → ground and blended (wet or dry process; the dry process is used today because it saves fuel) → burnt in a rotary kiln at about 1400–1500 °C → clinker → cooled and ground with 2–5 % gypsum → cement.
  • Gypsum is added to slow down the setting. Without it the cement would set at once (flash set).
  • Oxide composition of OPC: lime (CaO) 60–65 %, silica 17–25 %, alumina 3–8 %, iron oxide 0.5–6 %, magnesia up to about 4 %, sulphur trioxide 1–3 %, alkalis under 1 %.
  • Excess lime makes cement unsound (it expands later); too little lime reduces strength and makes it set quickly.

Setting and hardening are different. Setting is the change from a plastic paste to a solid (stiffening). Hardening is the gain of strength after setting. False set is early stiffening without much heat; remixing without adding water restores plasticity. Flash set is immediate stiffening with heat, caused by C₃A when gypsum is insufficient; it cannot be undone.

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a relay team. C₃A is the over-excited starter who would finish the race in a second and spoil it, so gypsum holds his hand. C₃S is the sprinter who gives the early lead (early strength). C₂S is the marathon runner who keeps adding distance for a year (later strength). C₄AF mostly carries the team colours (grey).

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Early strength → C₃S; later strength → C₂S; flash set and most heat → C₃A.
  • Low-heat cement has less C₃S and C₃A; rapid-hardening has more C₃S. Sulphate-resisting has less C₃A.
  • Gypsum retards setting; it does not add strength.
  • Rapid-hardening cement is not quick-setting cement: its setting times are the same as OPC; only the strength comes sooner.
  • Water for full hydration is 38 % (23 % bound + 15 % gel), not 23 % alone.

Topic 6: Cement — Setting Times, Soundness, Fineness and Strength Tests

Syllabus line: Cement: setting times, strength.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 0 questions in this shift

1. Basic idea

Before cement is used, a sample is tested to confirm five things: it is ground fine enough, it stays workable long enough, it hardens soon enough, it will not expand later, and it reaches its grade strength. The tests are given in IS 4031 and the limits in the code of each cement (IS 269 for OPC).

Each test answers a site question. Initial setting time: do I have enough time to mix, carry, place and compact? Final setting time: will it harden soon enough for the work to proceed? Soundness: will the hardened mass crack later by expanding? Fineness: will it hydrate fast enough? Strength: is it really 33, 43 or 53 grade?

Topic 7: Mortar — Ingredients, Proportions, Water Demand, Plastering; Lime

Syllabus line: Cement mortar: ingredients, proportions, water demand, mortars for plastering and masonry.

1. Basic idea

Mortar is a workable paste of a binder (cement or lime), fine aggregate (sand) and water. It beds and joins bricks or stones, and as plaster it covers them. Concrete is mortar plus coarse aggregate.

2. Why each ingredient is there

The binder glues. The sand gives bulk cheaply, and — more important — prevents the shrinkage cracks that a rich paste would develop. The water hydrates the cement and makes the mix workable. Sand takes no part in the chemistry; it is an inert filler.

Water demand and workability of mortar
  • Mortar needs more water than concrete of the same richness: it must spread under the trowel, and part of its water is sucked out by the bricks.
  • Water demand rises with finer sand, richer mix and higher temperature. Finer sand has more surface to be wetted.
  • Water retentivity is the ability of mortar to hold its water against the suction of the brick. Poor retention leaves too little water for hydration and a weak bond. Lime improves workability and water retention — the reason for cement–lime (gauged or composite) mortar such as 1 : 1 : 6.

  • Strength of mortar is tested on cubes (IS 2250); it must be kept wet (cured) for at least 7 days.
Sand
  • Sources: pit sand (sharp, angular — good for mortar), river sand (rounded, fine — good for plaster), sea sand (contains salts — causes efflorescence, avoided). Crushed-stone sand is now widely used.
  • Bulking of sand: a film of water round each grain pushes the grains apart, so damp sand occupies more volume than dry sand. Bulking is greatest, about 20–40 %, at 4–6 % moisture. Fine sand bulks more than coarse sand. Fully saturated (inundated) sand returns to its dry volume.
  • Bulking % = (bulked volume − dry volume) ÷ dry volume × 100. When sand is measured by volume, extra is taken to allow for bulking.
  • Fineness modulus of sand: fine 2.2–2.6, medium 2.6–2.9, coarse 2.9–3.2.
  • Silt content should not exceed about 8 % by volume (field test in a measuring cylinder); organic impurities are detected by the colour test with sodium hydroxide.
Plastering and pointing
  • Objects of plastering: a smooth, hygienic surface; protection from rain; a base for paint; hiding poor workmanship.
  • Coats: three-coat work — rendering coat (first, 9–10 mm), floating coat (second, 6–9 mm), finishing coat (2–3 mm). Two-coat work omits the floating coat. Each coat is kept rough to key the next.
  • Thickness: 12 mm on the fair (smooth) face of a brick wall, 15 mm on the rough face, 20 mm in two coats outside, 6 mm on concrete ceilings.
  • Defects: blistering (small swellings), crazing (hair cracks), cracking, efflorescence (white salts), flaking and peeling (loss of bond), popping (conical holes caused by particles that expand, such as unslaked lime), rust stains.
  • Pointing is finishing the joints only, raked out to 10–20 mm and filled with rich mortar. Types: flush, recessed, struck (weathered), tuck, V-pointing, beaded.

4. Everyday analogy

Cement paste alone is like pure jaggery syrup — sticky, costly, and it cracks as it dries. Add puffed rice (sand) and you get a firm, cheap laddu that holds its shape. Bulking of sand is like damp hair: slightly wet, it stands up and takes more room; soaked, it lies flat again.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Sand does not add strength to mortar; richer mortar (less sand) is stronger but shrinks and cracks more.

  • Dry volume of mortar is about 1.3 times the wet volume; forgetting this factor gives a wrong cement quantity.
  • Popping (conical holes) and blistering (swellings) are different plaster defects.

Topic 8: Concrete — Ingredients, Aggregates, Admixtures and the Water–Cement Ratio

Syllabus line: Concrete: importance of w/c ratio, strength, ingredients including admixtures.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question
  • Which aggregates are generally used for high-strength concrete? Key: angular aggregates — their rough faces and interlocking give a better bond with the cement paste (Q18).

1. Basic idea

Concrete is cement, fine aggregate (sand), coarse aggregate (stone) and water mixed in fixed proportions, which hardens into an artificial stone. Aggregates fill about 70–75 % of the volume; cement paste coats every particle and fills the gaps. M20 means a mix whose characteristic compressive strength is 20 N/mm², measured on 150 mm cubes at 28 days.

2. Why the water–cement ratio is the most important number

Cement needs water equal to only about 0.38 of its weight to hydrate fully. Any extra water is added just to make the mix workable. That extra water does not combine; it later dries out and leaves fine capillary pores. More pores means lower strength, more permeability and less durability. So for a fully compacted concrete, strength depends mainly on the water–cement ratio — the lower the ratio, the stronger the concrete. This is Abrams' law (Duff Abrams, 1918).

Topic 9: Fresh and Hardened Concrete — Workability, Curing, Strength Tests and NDT

Syllabus line: Concrete: workability, testing for strength, non-destructive testing.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 1 question

1. Basic idea

Concrete has two lives. Fresh concrete must be workable — easy to mix, carry, place, compact and finish without separating. Hardened concrete must be strong and durable. Tests exist for both. Non-destructive tests (NDT) judge the concrete in a finished structure without damaging it.

2. Why workability matters

Concrete that cannot be fully compacted keeps air voids, and each 1 % of voids costs about 5 % of strength. So the mix must be workable enough for the job: stiff for a road slab laid by machine, flowing for a congested column or a pile cast under water.

Topic 10: Concrete Mix Design (IS 10262) — and Reinforcing Steel

Syllabus line: Concrete: mix design methods. Steel is not named in the Building Materials syllabus line but two questions came from it in 2023, so it is added here.

Asked in APPSC AEE 2023, Paper III Civil, 22 Aug Shift 2 (verified with official key): 2 questions (both on steel)
  • As per IS 1786 : 2008, the yield strength of high-strength deformed bars is taken as the 0.2 % proof stress (Q16).
  • Two statements on carbon in steel: S1 said that ductility increases with carbon — false (it decreases). S2 said that the modulus of elasticity does not depend on carbon content — true (Q17).

1. Basic idea

Mix design is choosing the proportions of cement, water, fine and coarse aggregate (and admixture) so that the concrete has the required workability when fresh, the required strength and durability when hard, at the lowest cost. A nominal mix uses fixed volume proportions such as 1 : 1.5 : 3; a design mix is worked out for the actual materials and is required for M25 and above.

2. Why design for more than the grade

Cube results scatter round their average. If the average were only equal to the grade, half the results would fall below it. So the mix is designed for a higher target mean strength, chosen so that only 5 % of results fall below the grade.

3. How it works

Target mean strength
  • f′ck = fck + 1.65 s, where s is the standard deviation. (IS 10262 : 2019 also asks for fck + X and takes the larger; X is 5 for M10–M15, 5.5 for M20–M25, 6.5 for M30–M60.)
  • The factor 1.65 corresponds to 5 % of results being allowed below fck (1 in 20).
  • Assumed standard deviation (IS 456 / IS 10262) when no site data exist: 3.5 N/mm² for M10–M15; 4.0 for M20–M25; 5.0 for M30–M60.
  • Example: M25 → 25 + 1.65 × 4 = 31.6 N/mm². M30 → 30 + 1.65 × 5 = 38.25 N/mm².
  • Good quality control lowers s, lowers the target strength and so saves cement.
Step (IS 10262)What is done
1. Target strengthf′ck = fck + 1.65 s
2. Water–cement ratioChosen from the strength–w/c curve for the cement used; must not exceed the durability limit of IS 456
3. Water contentFrom a table, by maximum size of aggregate, for 50 mm slump: about 208 kg/m³ for 10 mm, 186 for 20 mm, 165 for 40 mm aggregate. Add about 3 % for every extra 25 mm of slump; reduce when a plasticiser is used
4. Cement contentWater ÷ w/c ratio. Check against the minimum for durability and the maximum of 450 kg/m³
5. Coarse aggregate fractionVolume of coarse aggregate per unit volume of total aggregate, from a table by zone of sand and size of aggregate (for 20 mm aggregate at w/c 0.5: Zone I 0.60, II 0.62, III 0.64, IV 0.66). Finer sand → more coarse aggregate
6. QuantitiesBy the absolute volume method: 1 m³ = volumes of cement + water + admixture + air + aggregates, each = mass ÷ (specific gravity × 1000)
7. Site correctionsAdjust for surface moisture and absorption of the aggregates
8. Trial mixesCast, test and adjust until workability and strength are satisfied

Other methods of mix design: ACI committee 211 method (USA), DOE method (Britain), Road Note No. 4 method, fineness modulus method, maximum density method, and the arbitrary (nominal) method. The IS method (IS 10262) is the one used in India.

Effect of carbon and the IS 1786 bars
  • More carbon → higher tensile strength, yield strength, hardness and brittleness; lower ductility, toughness and weldability.
  • The modulus of elasticity of all steels is practically the same, 2 × 10⁵ N/mm², whatever the carbon content.
  • IS 432 — mild steel plain bars, Fe 250. IS 1786 : 2008 — high-strength deformed bars: Fe 415, Fe 500, Fe 550, Fe 600, with ductile variants Fe 415D, Fe 500D, Fe 550D and Fe 415S, Fe 500S. IS 2062 — structural steel sections (E 250 and above).
  • Mild steel shows a clear yield point. High-strength bars do not, so their yield strength is defined as the 0.2 % proof stress: the stress at which a line drawn parallel to the initial straight part, from 0.2 % strain, meets the curve.
  • Minimum elongation: Fe 415 — 14.5 %; Fe 500 — 12 %; Fe 500D — 16 %; Fe 550 — 10 % (mild steel about 23 %). Higher grade, lower ductility.
  • TMT bars (thermo-mechanically treated): the hot bar is quenched with water so that the surface becomes hard martensite while the core stays soft and ductile — high strength with good ductility and weldability.
  • Ribs (deformations) on the bar improve the bond with concrete; IS 456 allows 60 % higher bond stress for deformed bars.

4. Everyday analogy

A tailor who must deliver trousers of at least 100 cm length does not aim at exactly 100, because his cutting varies a little; he aims at 102 so that almost none comes out short. That margin is 1.65 s. A careful tailor (small s) wastes less cloth. For carbon in steel, think of jaggery syrup: cook it longer and the sweet becomes harder and stronger to bite, but it snaps instead of bending.

5. Confusing areas and exam traps

  • Target strength is fck + 1.65 s, not fck + s. The 1.65 belongs to 5 % defectives.
  • Water content depends on the size of aggregate and slump; cement content then follows from the w/c ratio — not the other way round.
  • Finer sand (Zone IV) → larger proportion of coarse aggregate.
  • Carbon raises strength and hardness but lowers ductility; it does not change E. This was the 2023 trap.
  • Fe 500 means a yield (0.2 % proof) strength of 500 N/mm², not the ultimate strength.
