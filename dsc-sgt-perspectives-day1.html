Topic 1: Education in Ancient and Medieval India

1. Basic idea

Before schools and universities as we know them, India had four main traditions of education. In the Vedic tradition a student lived with the teacher in a gurukula (teacher's home). Buddhist education happened in monasteries called viharas, and some grew into large universities like Nalanda. Jain education taught right faith, knowledge and conduct. In the medieval period, Islamic education ran through maktabs (primary) and madrasas (higher).

2. Why it exists

Each tradition wanted to pass on its religion, values and practical knowledge to the next generation. There were no printed books, so learning depended on a close teacher–student bond, memory and discussion. The examiner asks these questions because modern ideas such as residential schools, the teacher as a role model and learning by discussion have roots here.

3. How it works

Tradition Where Start and end of study Method Language
Vedic Gurukula / ashrama Upanayana (initiation) → Samavartana (graduation, return home) Oral: Sravana → Manana → Nidhidhyasana Sanskrit
Buddhist Vihara (monastery), Mahavihara (university) Pabbajja / Pravrajya (first ordination, about age 8) → Upasampada (full ordination at 20) Discussion, debate, teaching in groups Pali (people's language)
Jain Basadis, upashrayas (temple halls) Lifelong discipline Ethical training: Tri-ratnas Prakrit
Islamic Maktab (primary, attached to a mosque) → Madrasa (higher) Bismillah ceremony at 4 years, 4 months, 4 days Memorising, reading, writing, arithmetic; later law, literature, sciences Arabic, Persian

Famous centres of learning

  • Takshashila (Taxila): one of the oldest; the grammarian Panini and Chanakya (Kautilya) are linked to it. Today it is in Pakistan.
  • Nalanda (Bihar): founded by Kumaragupta I (Gupta dynasty, 5th century CE). The Chinese traveller Xuanzang (Hiuen Tsang) studied here in the 7th century. Destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khilji (around 1193). A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2016.
  • Vikramashila (Bihar): founded by the Pala king Dharmapala (late 8th century).
  • Valabhi (Gujarat): a Buddhist centre under the Maitrakas.
  • Nagarjunakonda (Andhra Pradesh): Buddhist monastic centre under the Ikshvakus (3rd–4th century CE), linked with the philosopher Acharya Nagarjuna.

4. Everyday analogy

A Vedic gurukula is like a residential school today: the student stays with the teacher, does chores, and leaves after a "graduation day" (Samavartana). A Buddhist mahavihara like Nalanda is like a university campus with hostels. A maktab next to a mosque is like a village primary school next to the temple or panchayat office, and a madrasa is like a college in the town.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Upanayana: the "sacred thread" initiation that started Vedic study. Upa = near, nayana = to lead: leading the child near the teacher.
  2. Samavartana: the ceremony when study ended and the student returned home. Think of it as convocation.
  3. Sravana–Manana–Nidhidhyasana: listening to the teacher → reflecting and clearing doubts → deep meditation until the idea becomes your own.
  4. Pabbajja → Upasampada: Buddhist first ordination (novice, samanera) → full membership as a monk (bhikkhu) at 20.
  5. Ashtanga marga (Eightfold Path): the Buddhist path of right living. The 2025 paper matched "Buddhist education" with "Ashtanga margam".
  6. Tri-ratnas (Three Jewels): Jain ideals of right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. Remember: Tri-ratnas = Jain, not Buddhist.
  7. Maktab vs Madrasa: Maktab = primary (the alphabet, reading, prayers). Madrasa = higher learning. The Madrasa of Mahmud Gawan at Bidar (1472) is a famous example.
  8. Pre-Vedic period: the Indus (Harappan) cities show planning and a script, so some organised training existed. But the script is not deciphered, so we have no direct record of their education.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Nalanda vs Vikramashila founders: Nalanda = Kumaragupta I (Gupta). Vikramashila = Dharmapala (Pala). Examiners swap these.
  • Pabbajja vs Upasampada: Pabbajja is the first step, not the final one.
  • Tri-ratnas vs Triratna of Buddhism: Buddhism also has "Three Jewels" (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha), but in DSC questions the Tri-ratnas of right faith, knowledge and conduct are Jain.
  • Maktab vs Madrasa: maktab is lower, madrasa is higher. Reversing them is a common "incorrect statement".
  • Women in Vedic times: scholars like Gargi and Maitreyi show that women could study, though later this became rare.

7. Connections to other topics

  • History (GK): Guptas founded Nalanda; Palas founded Vikramashila; Ikshvakus ruled from Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda); Bahmani minister Mahmud Gawan built the Bidar madrasa.
  • Psychology: learning by listening and reflecting matches today's idea of meaningful (not rote) learning; the guru as model matches Bandura's observational learning.
  • Andhra Pradesh: Nagarjunakonda and Amaravati were major Buddhist centres; today Acharya Nagarjuna University is named after the philosopher.
  • NEP 2020: revives the idea of large multidisciplinary universities "like ancient Nalanda and Takshashila".

8. Memory technique

"U-S for Vedas, P-U for Buddha, 3 for Jains, M-M for Islam"

  • Vedic = Upanayana → Samavartana (Up and Sit: you go up to the teacher and sit back at home).
  • Buddhist = Pabbajja → Upasampada (P before U, like Primary before University).
  • Jain = 3 jewels (Tri-ratnas).
  • Islamic = Maktab → Madrasa (small M school first, big M school later).

Founders: "Gupta built Nalanda, Pala built Vikrama": G–N, P–V.

Topic 2: Education Reports before Independence (1813–1944)

1. Basic idea

Under British rule, education policy was shaped by a series of official documents: an Act (1813), a Minute (1835), a Despatch (1854), several Commissions and Committees, and plans by Indians such as Gokhale and Gandhi. Each one answered a question of its time: how much to spend, which language to teach in, who should run schools, and how to reach every child.

2. Why it exists

The East India Company first ignored education. Later it needed English-educated Indians to work as clerks and officials, and Indian reformers demanded education for the masses. So every few decades the government appointed a body to study the problem and recommend changes. The 2024 SCERT syllabus lists "education reports and committees from 1854 to 1944" for this reason.

3. How it works

  1. 1813Charter Act: Company to spend ₹1 lakh a year on education (first official responsibility).
  2. 1835Macaulay's Minute (2 Feb) and Bentinck's resolution: English becomes the medium of higher education.
  3. 1854Wood's Despatch: "Magna Carta of English education"; education departments, universities (1857).
  4. 1882Hunter Commission: first Indian Education Commission; primary education to local boards.
  5. 1902-04Indian Universities Commission (Raleigh) and Indian Universities Act 1904 (Curzon).
  6. 1911Gokhale's Bill for free and compulsory primary education: rejected.
  7. 1917-19Sadler (Calcutta University) Commission: intermediate colleges, Secondary Boards.
  8. 1929Hartog Committee: "wastage and stagnation" in primary education.
  9. 1937Wardha Scheme (Basic Education / Nai Talim): Gandhi; craft-centred, mother tongue, 7 years.
  10. 1944Sargent Plan: free, compulsory education for 6-14 within 40 years.

Key recommendations to remember

  • Wood's Despatch (1854), by Sir Charles Wood, President of the Board of Control: a Department of Public Instruction in each province; universities at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras on the London University model (set up in 1857); grants-in-aid to private schools; mother tongue at primary level and English at higher level; teacher training and women's education. It rejected the Downward Filtration Theory and wanted mass education.
  • Hunter Commission (1882), chaired by Sir William Wilson Hunter and appointed by Lord Ripon: the first Indian Education Commission. It gave primary education to local bodies (district and municipal boards), encouraged private schools with grants, and suggested two streams in secondary education (literary and practical).
  • Sadler Commission (1917–19), on Calcutta University, chaired by Michael Sadler: 12 years of schooling before a degree, separate intermediate colleges and Boards of Secondary and Intermediate Education.
  • Hartog Committee (1929), chaired by Philip Hartog: found huge wastage (children leaving school early) and stagnation (children failing and repeating the same class). It advised improving quality (consolidation) before expanding.
  • Wardha Scheme (1937), Gandhi's Basic Education (Nai Talim): free and compulsory education for 7 years (ages 7–14), in the mother tongue, built around a productive craft, and self-supporting. The Zakir Hussain Committee drew up the details.
  • Sargent Plan (1944), by John Sargent, Educational Adviser: the "Post-War Plan of Educational Development". It proposed free, compulsory education for ages 6–14 to be reached in 40 years, with pre-primary education for ages 3–6.

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a village that keeps finding problems in its school. First the panchayat sets aside money (1813). Then it argues about whether to teach in Telugu or English (1835). It writes a full development plan (1854). It hands the primary school to the gram panchayat (1882). It notices children dropping out and repeating classes (1929). And finally it makes a 40-year plan to get every child into school (1944).

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Charter Act 1813: the first time the Company accepted a duty to spend on education: ₹1 lakh a year.
  2. Orientalists vs Anglicists: Orientalists wanted Sanskrit and Persian learning; Anglicists wanted English. Macaulay's Minute (2 February 1835) sided with English, and Bentinck's resolution (March 1835) made it policy.
  3. Downward Filtration Theory: educate a small upper class in English, and knowledge will "filter down" to the masses. Macaulay's period followed it; Wood's Despatch rejected it.
  4. Grants-in-aid: government money given to private schools that meet standards. It started with Wood (1854) and was expanded by Hunter (1882).
  5. Wastage vs stagnation (Hartog): wastage = dropping out before completing; stagnation = staying in the same class by failing.
  6. Gokhale's Bill (1911): Gopal Krishna Gokhale's bill for free, compulsory primary education. It was rejected, but the idea came back in the Sargent Plan and finally in the RTE Act 2009.
  7. Indian Universities Act 1904 (Lord Curzon), after the Raleigh Commission (1902): it increased government control over universities.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Charles Wood ≠ William Hunter: Wood = Despatch, 1854. Hunter = Commission, 1882.
  • "Magna Carta": the title goes to Wood's Despatch, not Macaulay's Minute.
  • Sadler vs Sargent: Sadler (1917) = Calcutta University and intermediate colleges. Sargent (1944) = the 40-year plan for compulsory education.
  • Hartog was an auxiliary committee of the Simon Commission, and its focus was quality (wastage and stagnation), not expansion.
  • Wardha Scheme ages: 7 to 14 (7 years). The Sargent Plan's compulsory age is 6 to 14.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Modern History: Ripon (local self-government) → Hunter Commission gave schools to local boards. Curzon → Universities Act 1904 (also the partition of Bengal, 1905). Gandhi → Nai Talim.
  • Polity: Gokhale's dream of free, compulsory education became Article 45 (Directive Principle) and later Article 21A and the RTE Act 2009.
  • Psychology and methodology: Basic Education's "learning by doing" matches the project method and activity-based learning.

8. Memory technique

Chain story with years: "In 1813 the Company opened its purse (₹1 lakh). In 1835 Macaulay chose English. In 1854 Wood wrote the Magna Carta. In 1882 Hunter handed schools to the panchayat. In 1917 Sadler built intermediate colleges. In 1929 Hartog saw children wasting and stagnating. In 1937 Gandhi gave them a spinning wheel at Wardha. In 1944 Sargent gave them 40 years to reach every child."

Order code: C-M-W-H-S-H-W-S → "Company Makes Wood Hunt; Sadler Helps Wardha Schools".

Topic 3: Commissions and Policies after Independence (1948–1992)

1. Basic idea

After 1947, free India set up commissions to rebuild education level by level: universities first (Radhakrishnan, 1948), then secondary schools (Mudaliar, 1952), then the whole system (Kothari, 1964). Their reports became National Policies on Education (1968 and 1986), and the 1986 policy was revised in 1992 with a Programme of Action (POA).

2. Why it exists

The Constitution promised free and compulsory education for children (Article 45). The country needed scientists, teachers and skilled workers, and a common national structure instead of many different state systems. Each commission studied one level, and the national policies turned their ideas into government decisions and schemes.

3. How it works

  1. 1948-49University Education Commission: Dr S. Radhakrishnan; led to the UGC.
  2. 1952-53Secondary Education Commission: Dr A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar; multipurpose schools.
  3. 1964-66Kothari Commission: "Education and National Development"; 10+2+3, common school system.
  4. 1968National Policy on Education 1968: first national policy; three-language formula.
  5. 1977Ishwarbhai Patel Committee: SUPW (Socially Useful Productive Work).
  6. 1986National Policy on Education 1986: Navodaya Vidyalayas, Operation Blackboard (1987), DIETs.
  7. 1990Acharya Ramamurti Committee: review of NPE 1986.
  8. 1992Revised NPE and Programme of Action (POA), after the Janardhana Reddy Committee (1991).
  9. 1993Yash Pal Committee: "Learning without Burden".

4. Everyday analogy

Think of building a three-storey house after a flood. First you repair the top floor (universities, 1948), then the middle floor (secondary, 1952), then you call an engineer to redesign the whole house (Kothari, 1964). The engineer's plan becomes the official house plan (NPE 1968). Twenty years later you renovate (NPE 1986), check the work (Ramamurti, 1990) and write a step-by-step work schedule (POA 1992).

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Multipurpose schools (Mudaliar): one school offering several streams (academic, technical, agriculture, commerce) so that students can choose by interest.
  2. Common School System (Kothari): every child in a neighbourhood goes to the same public school, whatever the family's income or caste. This promotes equality.
  3. 10+2+3 (Kothari): 10 years of general school, 2 years of higher secondary, 3 years of degree. It is still the base of most degree courses, though NEP 2020 changed the school stages to 5+3+3+4.
  4. Three-language formula (Kothari; NPE 1968): the mother tongue or regional language, Hindi, and English (in Hindi states, a modern Indian language instead of Hindi).
  5. 6% of national income: Kothari's spending target for education, repeated in NPE 1968, 1986 and NEP 2020 (as 6% of GDP).
  6. Operation Blackboard (1987): minimum facilities for every primary school: at least two rooms, two teachers (one a woman, where possible), and basic teaching aids.
  7. DIET: District Institute of Education and Training, for training primary teachers at district level.
  8. Navodaya Vidyalayas: free residential schools for talented rural children, one per district.
  9. Kothari's opening line: "The destiny of India is now being shaped in her classrooms."

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Radhakrishnan = universities, Mudaliar = secondary. This is the most common swap in exams.
  • The Kothari Commission was officially called the "Education Commission" (sometimes "National Education Commission"). Its report is "Education and National Development" (1966).
  • NPE 1968 vs NPE 1986: 1968 is the first policy (Indira Gandhi); 1986 brought Navodaya, Operation Blackboard and DIETs (Rajiv Gandhi).
  • 1992 is not a new policy: it was a revision of NPE 1986, plus a Programme of Action.
  • SUPW comes from the Ishwarbhai Patel Committee (1977), not from Kothari, whose term was "work experience".
  • "Learning without Burden" (1993) comes from the Yash Pal Committee, about heavy school bags and rote learning.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Polity: the 42nd Amendment (1976) moved education from the State List to the Concurrent List. This is why both the Centre and the States make education policy.
  • NEP 2020: replaces NPE 1986; keeps the 6% target; replaces 10+2 with 5+3+3+4.
  • Day 2 (Contemporary concerns): the schemes born from these policies (Operation Blackboard, DPEP, SSA, Samagra Shiksha).
  • Methodology: Ishwarbhai Patel's SUPW and Kothari's work experience link to activity-based and project learning.

8. Memory technique

Level ladder: "U-S-A" in time order. University (Radhakrishnan, 1948) → Secondary (Mudaliar, 1952) → All levels (Kothari, 1964).

Number chain for policies: 68 → 86. The digits flip: NPE 1968 → NPE 1986. Then add six years for the revision and POA: 1986 + 6 = 1992.

Kothari's "10-2-3-6-3" code: 10+2+3 structure, 6% spending, 3-language formula.
