Topic 1: Growth, Development and Maturation

1. Basic idea

A child changes every day: taller, smarter, more social. Psychology separates this change into three ideas. Growth (పెరుగుదల) is the increase in body size, which you can measure. Development (వికాసం) is the change in the whole person: body, mind, emotions and behaviour. Maturation (పరిపక్వత) is the change that happens from inside, by nature, without any training.

2. Why it exists

A teacher must know what a child of a given age is ready for. Teaching before the child is ready wastes effort and causes frustration; teaching too little wastes the child's ability. Knowing the principles and stages of development lets the teacher match lessons to the child.

3. How it works

Growth Development
What changes Size of body: height, weight Whole person: body, mind, emotions, behaviour
Type Quantitative (measured in numbers) Qualitative and quantitative
When it stops After adolescence Continues through life ("womb to tomb")
Example Height goes from 110 cm to 120 cm Child starts sharing toys and solving puzzles
Relation A part of development Wider; growth is included in it

Development = Maturation + Learning. Maturation unfolds from inside; learning comes from practice and experience.

Principles of development (most asked)

  1. Continuity: development never stops; it goes step by step.
  2. Uniform sequence: every child passes through the same stages in the same order (crawl → stand → walk), though at different speeds.
  3. Cephalocaudal (శిరః పాదాభిముఖ): from head to toe. Head control comes first, then the trunk, then the legs.
  4. Proximodistal (సమీప దూరాభిముఖ): from the centre of the body outward. Shoulder before arm, arm before fingers.
  5. General to specific: a baby first moves the whole body to reach a toy; later only the hand.
  6. Individual differences: each child has their own rate.
  7. Heredity × environment: both work together; neither alone is enough.
  8. Predictable: we can roughly say when a child will walk or talk.
  9. Interrelated aspects: a sick child (physical) becomes irritable (emotional) and falls behind in studies (cognitive).

Factors influencing development (four groups in the syllabus)

Factor Examples
Biological Heredity (genes on 23 pairs of chromosomes), health, nutrition, glands (hormones), physical defects
Psychological Intelligence, motivation, self-concept, interests
Sociological Family, peer group, school, culture, socio-economic status, media
Emotional Love and security at home, fear, anxiety, emotional stability

Stages and their features

Stage Age Main features
Infancy 0–2 Fastest growth; learns through the senses (sensory development is highest); attachment to the mother
Early childhood 2–6 "Pre-gang age", "toy age"; many questions; language grows fast; egocentric
Later childhood 6–12 "Gang age"; school age; logical thinking with real objects; group loyalty; curiosity
Adolescence 12–18 "Period of stress and storm" (Stanley Hall); puberty; search for identity; strong emotions; hero worship

Dimensions of development: physical and motor (gross: running, jumping; fine: holding a pencil, buttoning); cognitive (thinking, memory, reasoning); emotional (K.M.B. Bridges: a newborn shows only general excitement, and emotions separate out by about age 2); social (from self-centred to cooperative); moral (sense of right and wrong); language (crying → cooing → babbling → one word → two words → sentences).

4. Everyday analogy

A child's development is like a mango tree. The tree getting taller and thicker is growth. The tree flowering and bearing fruit at the right season is development. The flowering happens on its own when the tree is ready (maturation), but water and manure (learning and environment) decide how good the fruit is. Plucking flowers early to "help" the tree only harms it, just as forcing writing on a 2-year-old does.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Growth vs development: growth = size (quantitative); development = quality and quantity, the whole person.
  2. Maturation vs learning: a baby walks without lessons (maturation); a child rides a cycle after practice (learning).
  3. Readiness: the state when maturation makes learning possible. Teaching works only after readiness.
  4. Cephalocaudal vs proximodistal: "cephalo" = head, "caudal" = tail → head to toe. "Proximo" = near, "distal" = far → centre to edges.
  5. Heredity vs environment: heredity = nature (genes from parents); environment = nurture (everything after conception). Twin studies compare them, because identical twins share the same genes.
  6. Gang age: in later childhood children form groups, love games with rules and want their friends' approval. This is why group work suits Classes 3–5.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Same sequence, different speed. "All children develop at the same rate" is a common wrong statement.
  • Proximodistal is the one asked most, and the 2025 answer was "centre of the body to the periphery". Don't pick "head to toe" for it.
  • Infancy is the stage of the most rapid growth and sensory learning, not early childhood.
  • "Stress and storm" is Stanley Hall's phrase for adolescence, not Erikson's.
  • Development stops? Growth stops; development continues lifelong.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Topic 2: Piaget's stages are the cognitive side of these age stages.
  • Unit 3 (Learning): readiness is Thorndike's law of readiness.
  • Methodology: the maxims "simple to complex" and "concrete to abstract" follow the principles of development.
  • Perspectives: NEP 2020's 5+3+3+4 stages match the stages of development.

8. Memory technique

"C-U-P-G-I-H-P-I" for the principles → "Children Use Pencils Gently In Happy Primary Institutions": Continuity, Uniform sequence, Proximodistal (and cephalocaudal), General to specific, Individual differences, Heredity × environment, Predictable, Interrelated.

Stages by "S-T-G-S": infancy = Senses, early childhood = Toys, later childhood = Gang, adolescence = Storm.

Topic 2: Theories of Development

1. Basic idea

Several psychologists explained how children develop, each from a different angle. Piaget: how thinking develops in stages. Vygotsky: how other people help a child learn. Kohlberg: how the sense of right and wrong develops. Erikson: how personality develops through a crisis at each stage. Chomsky: how language develops. Carl Rogers: how a child grows when accepted and valued.

2. Why it exists

Teachers face questions like "Why can't my Class 2 child understand that 5 + 3 and 3 + 5 are the same?" or "Why do Class 5 children care so much about fairness?" Each theory answers one kind of question, and DSC questions usually present a classroom situation and ask which theory or stage it shows.

3. How it works

Piaget: cognitive development. A child builds mental files called schemas. New information is fitted into an old schema (assimilation) or the schema is changed (accommodation). The balance between them is equilibration.

Stage Age Key words for the exam
Sensorimotor 0–2 Learns through senses and movement; object permanence
Pre-operational 2–7 Symbols and pretend play; egocentrism, animism, centration; no conservation
Concrete operational 7–11 Logic with real objects; conservation, reversibility, classification, seriation
Formal operational 11+ Abstract and hypothetical thinking; hypothetico-deductive reasoning

Vygotsky: social-cultural development. Learning happens first with others, then inside the child. The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) is the gap between what a child can do alone and what they can do with help. Scaffolding is help given inside the ZPD and slowly withdrawn. An MKO (More Knowledgeable Other) is anyone who knows more: teacher, parent or classmate. Children's self-talk is private speech.

Kohlberg: moral development (the Heinz dilemma; he studied the reason given, not the yes/no answer).

Level Stage Typical reasoning
1. Pre-conventional 1. Obedience and punishment "Don't, or you'll be punished."
2. Self-interest / instrumental exchange "Help her, she helped me."
2. Conventional 3. Good boy – good girl "Good students help others."
4. Authority and social-order maintaining (law and order) "It is the rule."
3. Post-conventional 5. Social contract "Laws should serve people."
6. Universal ethical principles "Human life is above everything."

Erikson: psychosocial development (8 stages, each a crisis between two opposites).

Age Crisis Strength
0–1 Trust vs Mistrust Hope
1–3 Autonomy vs Shame and doubt Will
3–6 Initiative vs Guilt Purpose
6–12 Industry vs Inferiority Competence
12–18 Identity vs Role confusion Fidelity
Young adult Intimacy vs Isolation Love
Middle adult Generativity vs Stagnation Care
Old age Integrity vs Despair Wisdom

Chomsky: language. Every child is born with a Language Acquisition Device (LAD), an inborn ability for language with a universal grammar. That is why any child can learn any language. Learning language only by imitation is Skinner's view, not Chomsky's.

Carl Rogers: self theory (humanistic). Every child has a self-concept and a drive to grow (self-actualisation). Children grow best when given unconditional positive regard (acceptance without conditions), empathy and genuineness. In school this means child-centred, non-directive teaching, with the teacher as facilitator.

Also useful: Freud gave the psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency for 6–12, a calm period, and genital). Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory says the child sits inside layers: family, school, community and culture.

4. Everyday analogy

Learning to ride a cycle shows all six theories. Piaget: the child must first be ready, and a 2-year-old can't. Vygotsky: an elder holds the seat (scaffolding) and slowly lets go. Kohlberg: the child stops at the signal "because the policeman is there" (stage 1) and later "because rules keep everyone safe" (stage 4). Erikson: a child who succeeds feels competent; one who is laughed at feels inferior. Chomsky: the child picks up the word "brake" without being drilled. Rogers: the child keeps trying when the family accepts the falls kindly.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Assimilation vs accommodation: a child who knows "dog" calls a goat "dog" (assimilation: fits the old file). When told "this is a goat", the child makes a new file (accommodation).
  2. Egocentrism vs animism vs centration: egocentrism = "everyone sees what I see"; animism = "the moon is alive and follows me"; centration = looking at only one feature (height of water).
  3. Conservation and reversibility: understanding that quantity stays the same when shape changes, because the change can be undone (pour the water back).
  4. ZPD and scaffolding in class: work a sum fully, give hints for the next one, then let the child do the third alone.
  5. Kohlberg stage 2 vs stage 3: stage 2 = "what do I get?" (exchange); stage 3 = "what will people think of me?" (approval).
  6. Erikson for SGTs: 6–12 is Industry vs Inferiority. Give tasks children can complete, and praise effort.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Object permanence belongs to the sensorimotor stage, not pre-operational.
  • Conservation appears in the concrete operational stage (7–11).
  • Private speech is Vygotsky's term; egocentric speech is Piaget's for the same self-talk.
  • Kohlberg's 2025 question: not copying "for fear of being debarred" is obedience and punishment (stage 1).
  • Chomsky ≠ imitation. The 2025 paper asked which is NOT Chomsky's idea, and the answer was "learning a language through imitation".
  • Rogers vs Maslow: both are humanistic; Rogers = self-concept and unconditional positive regard; Maslow = hierarchy of needs.
  • Carol Gilligan criticised Kohlberg for ignoring the "ethics of care".

7. Connections to other topics

  • Topic 1: Piaget's stages match the age stages; Erikson's 6–12 crisis is the "gang age".
  • Perspectives (NCF 2005): constructivism (Piaget) and social constructivism (Vygotsky) are the theory behind child-centred teaching.
  • Methodology: concrete materials for Classes 1–5 (concrete operational stage); group work and peer tutoring (ZPD).
  • Unit 3 (Learning): Bruner's scaffolding and discovery learning grew from these ideas.

8. Memory technique

Piaget: "S-P-C-F, 2-7-11": See and touch (0–2), Pretend (2–7), Concrete (7–11), Formal (11+).

Kohlberg: "Pain, Price, Praise, Police, Promise, Principle": 1 punishment, 2 exchange price, 3 praise and approval, 4 law (police), 5 social contract (promise), 6 universal principle.

Erikson's middle three: "I-I-I": Initiative (3–6), Industry (6–12), Identity (12–18).
