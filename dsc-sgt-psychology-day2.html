Topic 3: Individual Differences and Intelligence

1. Basic idea

No two children are the same, not even twins. Individual differences (వైయక్తిక భేదాలు) are the differences between people, and within one person, in abilities, interests and behaviour. Intelligence (ప్రజ్ఞ) is the ability to learn, solve new problems and adjust to new situations. Psychologists have built theories of what intelligence is, and tests (IQ) to measure it.

2. Why it exists

A class of 30 children has 30 different learners. If the teacher teaches everyone the same way at the same speed, the slow learners fall behind and the gifted get bored. Knowing about individual differences and intelligence helps the teacher plan grouping, remedial teaching and enrichment.

3. How it works

Two kinds of individual differences

  • Inter-individual: between two people (Ravi is better at Maths than Sita).
  • Intra-individual: within one person (Ravi is good at Maths but weak in drawing).
  • Areas: intelligence, aptitude, interest, attitude, personality, physical features, achievement.
  • Causes: heredity, environment, age, sex, family background, socio-economic status.

Theories of intelligence

Theory Given by Simple meaning
Uni-factor (monarchic) Binet, Terman Intelligence is one single ability
Two-factor Charles Spearman g = general ability used in all tasks; s = specific ability for one task
Multi-factor E.L. Thorndike Many separate abilities: abstract, social, mechanical
Primary Mental Abilities (group factor) L.L. Thurstone 7: verbal comprehension, word fluency, number, space, memory, perception, reasoning
Structure of Intellect (3-D model) J.P. Guilford Operations × Contents × Products = 180 abilities; gave convergent and divergent thinking
Multiple Intelligences (syllabus emphasis) Howard Gardner (1983) 8: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic
Triarchic Robert Sternberg Analytical, creative, practical
Fluid and crystallised R.B. Cattell Fluid = reasoning with new problems; crystallised = knowledge from experience

Gardner in class: a child who speaks fluently at an elocution contest → linguistic (the 2025 answer); a cricketer → bodily-kinesthetic; a class leader who settles quarrels → interpersonal; a child who knows their own strengths → intrapersonal; a child who knows birds and plants → naturalistic.

Measuring intelligence (IQ)

  • The first intelligence test was made by Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon (1905), France, who introduced Mental Age (MA).
  • William Stern (1912) gave the term IQ; Terman revised the test as the Stanford-Binet scale (1916).
  • IQ = (Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age) × 100. Example: MA 12, CA 10 → IQ 120. Always change years and months into months first.
IQ (Terman) Category
140 and above Genius / near genius
120–139 Very superior
110–119 Superior
90–109 Average
80–89 Dull
70–79 Borderline
Below 70 Intellectual disability

Types of tests: verbal vs non-verbal (performance); individual vs group. Examples: Wechsler scales; Bhatia's Battery of Performance Tests (Indian); Thorndike's CAVD (Completion, Arithmetic, Vocabulary, Directions), which measures intelligence. Non-verbal tests suit children who cannot read or who speak a different language.

Levels of intellectual disability

Level IQ (about) Old educational label What they can learn
Mild 50–70 Educable Basic reading, writing, arithmetic up to about Class 5–6; can live independently
Moderate 35–50 Trainable Self-care, simple work skills, with support
Severe 20–35 Custodial Basic self-care with close supervision
Profound Below 20 Custodial Needs lifelong care

Gifted children (IQ above about 130): need enrichment, extra challenge and acceleration. Slow learners (IQ about 70–89): need repetition, concrete examples and patience.

4. Everyday analogy

A class is like a vegetable market. Every vegetable is different (inter-individual), and even one vegetable has good and weak points (intra-individual: a mango is sweet but goes bad fast). A good cook does not cook them all the same way. A good teacher, likewise, plans different routes for different learners: slower steps for some, harder puzzles for others.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Spearman's g and s: g is like a car's engine that works for every trip; s is like a special tyre for one kind of road.
  2. Thurstone vs Guilford: Thurstone = 7 primary mental abilities; Guilford = 180 abilities in a 3-D cube. Small number → Thurstone; big number → Guilford.
  3. Gardner's 8: think "L-L-S-M-B-I-I-N": Linguistic, Logical, Spatial, Musical, Bodily, Interpersonal, Intrapersonal, Naturalistic.
  4. IQ with months: CA 8 years 4 months = 100 months; MA 10 years = 120 months → IQ 120.
  5. Educable vs trainable: educable (mild) learn school subjects slowly; trainable (moderate) learn self-care and skills.
  6. Classroom implementation: ability grouping, remedial teaching for slow learners, enrichment for the gifted, individual attention, varied methods and materials, and pacing for different speeds.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Thorndike appears twice: the multi-factor theory and the CAVD test (and also the laws of learning on Day 3).
  • Binet made the first test, but Stern gave the word IQ.
  • IQ 90–109 is average; 110–119 superior; 120–139 very superior.
  • Inter vs intra: "inter" = between people; "intra" = inside one person.
  • Trainable = moderate, not mild (mild = educable).

7. Connections to other topics

  • Day 1: heredity × environment explains individual differences.
  • Day 4 (Special needs): intellectual disability, gifted children and inclusive education.
  • Methodology: differentiated instruction, remedial teaching, multi-level activities.
  • Perspectives: equity in education means extra help based on individual needs.

8. Memory technique

Number chain for theories: "2-7-8-180-3". Spearman 2 factors; Thurstone 7; Gardner 8; Guilford 180; Sternberg 3 (triarchic).

IQ ladder from the top: "140 Genius, 120 Very, 110 Superior, 90 Average, 80 Dull, 70 Border".

Disability levels: "Mild–Educable, Moderate–Trainable" (M-E, M-T): say "ME first, then MT".

Topic 4: Emotional Intelligence, Creativity, Aptitude, Attitude, Interest and Habit

1. Basic idea

Intelligence is not the only thing that decides success. Emotional intelligence (EQ) is the ability to understand and manage your own feelings and other people's. Creativity is producing something new and useful. Aptitude is a natural potential to learn a skill; attitude is a learned readiness to respond for or against something; interest is a liking that pulls your attention; habit is an action repeated until it becomes automatic.

2. Why it exists

Two children with the same IQ can do very differently: one gives up, the other keeps trying; one has a liking for science, the other fears it. The syllabus asks how these factors influence intelligence and achievement, because a teacher can change attitudes, build interest and form good habits, while IQ changes little.

3. How it works

Emotional intelligence

  • The term was given by Salovey and Mayer (1990) and made popular by Daniel Goleman (1995).
  • Goleman's five components: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills.
Component Example in class
Self-awareness "I am getting angry."
Self-regulation Counting to ten before replying
Motivation Working for a goal without rewards
Empathy Understanding how a crying friend feels
Social skills Working well in a group, settling disputes

Creativity (సృజనాత్మకత)

  • Depends on divergent thinking (many answers); convergent thinking aims at one correct answer. Both terms come from Guilford.
  • Features: fluency (many ideas), flexibility (different kinds of ideas), originality (unusual ideas), elaboration (adding detail).
  • Stages (Graham Wallas): preparation → incubation → illumination → verification.
  • Tests: Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking; in India, Baqer Mehdi's test.
  • To encourage it: open-ended questions, accepting unusual answers, no fear of mistakes, freedom, brainstorming, art and project work.

Aptitude, attitude, interest, habit

Term Meaning Measured by / example
Aptitude (సహజ సామర్థ్యం) Potential to acquire a skill with training; it predicts future success DAT (Differential Aptitude Test)
Attitude (వైఖరి) Learned readiness to respond favourably or unfavourably. Mehran: "readiness to respond externally to social situations" (asked in 2025) Likert and Thurstone scales
Interest (అభిరుచి) A liking that draws attention to an activity Strong Interest Inventory; Kuder Preference Record
Habit (అలవాటు) An action repeated until it becomes automatic Formed by repetition and reward

How they influence intelligence and achievement: a positive attitude and strong interest raise attention and effort, so the child uses more of their intelligence. A matching aptitude makes learning in that area faster. Good habits (regular study, neat work) save time and effort, while bad habits waste ability.

4. Everyday analogy

Intelligence is like a bike's engine. Aptitude is how well that engine suits a certain road (hills or highway). Attitude is whether the rider wants to ride. Interest is the destination that pulls the rider forward. Habit is riding without thinking about each gear change. EQ is staying calm in traffic, and creativity is finding a new shortcut. A powerful engine with an unwilling rider still goes nowhere.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Aptitude vs attitude: they look similar. Aptitude = potential (ability); attitude = feeling towards something.
  2. Empathy vs sympathy: empathy = feeling with someone (understanding from their side); sympathy = feeling sorry for them. Goleman's component is empathy.
  3. Fluency vs originality: 15 ideas = fluency; ideas no one else gave = originality.
  4. Wallas order: prepare → let it rest (incubation) → sudden idea (illumination) → check it (verification).
  5. Habit formation: repetition + satisfaction (Thorndike's laws of exercise and effect) + starting early.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • EQ term: Salovey and Mayer; Goleman popularised it.
  • Goleman's five do NOT include sympathy or intelligence; they include empathy.
  • Creativity ≠ high IQ. Creativity needs a minimum level of intelligence, but a high IQ does not guarantee creativity.
  • Attitude scales are Likert and Thurstone. DAT measures aptitude.
  • Interest is not a fixed ability; it can be built by the teacher.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Day 4 (Personality): emotional intelligence and managing emotions also appear in the personality unit.
  • Day 3 (Learning): habit formation uses Thorndike's laws; interest links to motivation.
  • Methodology: brainstorming, project work and open-ended questions build creativity.
  • Perspectives: life skills (empathy, creative thinking, coping with emotions).

8. Memory technique

Goleman: "S-S-M-E-S" = Self-awareness, Self-regulation, Motivation, Empathy, Social skills → "Sita Sings Music Every Sunday".

Creativity features: "F-F-O-E" → "Five Fresh Original Examples".

Wallas: "P-I-I-V" → "Please Incubate Ideas Very well".

The four terms: "A-P-T = Potential; A-T-T = Tendency; I = Interest pulls; H = Habit runs on its own".
