Topic 5: Theories of Learning

1. Basic idea

Learning (అభ్యసనం) is a fairly permanent change in behaviour that comes from experience or practice. A change caused by tiredness, illness or drugs is not learning. Theories of learning explain how this change happens. Behaviourists (Thorndike, Pavlov, Skinner) say learning is building links between a stimulus and a response. Cognitive theorists (Kohler, Bruner) say learning is understanding. Social learning (Bandura) says we learn by watching others.

2. Why it exists

Every teaching decision rests on a learning theory, whether the teacher knows it or not: praising correct answers (Skinner), drilling tables (Thorndike), showing the whole picture first (Kohler), or modelling good behaviour (Bandura). DSC questions give a classroom situation and ask which theory or law explains it.

3. How it works

Psychologist Theory Experiment Key ideas Classroom use

B.F. Skinner Operant (instrumental) conditioning Rat (and pigeon) in a Skinner box Reinforcement (positive/negative), punishment, shaping, schedules Programmed instruction, teaching machines, immediate feedback
Wolfgang Kohler Insight learning (Gestalt) Chimpanzee Sultan, sticks and boxes Sudden "aha" from seeing the whole Show the whole first; problems that need understanding
Albert Bandura Social / observational learning Bobo doll Attention → retention → reproduction → motivation Teacher as model; children copy what they see (including violence on screens)
Jerome Bruner Discovery learning — Enactive → iconic → symbolic; spiral curriculum Let children find rules; concrete → picture → symbol

Thorndike's three primary laws

  1. Readiness: learning is satisfying when the learner is ready; forcing an unready learner annoys them.
  2. Exercise: practice strengthens a bond (use); lack of practice weakens it (disuse).
  3. Effect: a response followed by satisfaction is repeated; one followed by discomfort is dropped. This is the most important law.

Pavlov's terms

Term In the experiment
Unconditioned stimulus (UCS) Food
Unconditioned response (UCR) Saliva to food (natural)
Conditioned stimulus (CS) Bell (after pairing)
Conditioned response (CR) Saliva to the bell (learned)
  • Extinction: bell again and again without food → salivation stops.
  • Spontaneous recovery: after a rest, the bell again brings some saliva.
  • Generalisation: a similar sound also brings saliva.
  • Discrimination: the dog responds only to one particular bell.

Skinner's terms

  • Positive reinforcement: add something pleasant (praise, a star) → behaviour increases.
  • Negative reinforcement: remove something unpleasant (no homework) → behaviour increases. It is NOT punishment.
  • Punishment: behaviour decreases.
  • Shaping: rewarding small steps towards the final behaviour.
  • Schedules: continuous (every time) and partial: fixed ratio, variable ratio, fixed interval, variable interval.

4. Everyday analogy

Think of a child learning to use a mobile phone. Pressing buttons at random until the screen opens is Thorndike (trial and error). Feeling happy at the ringtone because it always means a call from mother is Pavlov. Using a game more because it gives coins is Skinner. Suddenly understanding how all the settings connect is Kohler. Copying how an elder brother swipes is Bandura. Exploring and working out the rules alone is Bruner.

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Classical vs operant: in classical conditioning the stimulus comes first and the response is automatic (saliva). In operant conditioning the learner acts first (presses the lever) and the consequence follows (food).
  2. Negative reinforcement vs punishment: negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant to increase behaviour; punishment decreases behaviour.
  3. Generalisation vs discrimination: generalisation = responding to similar stimuli; discrimination = responding only to the exact one.
  4. Insight: after failing, Sultan sat quietly and then suddenly joined two sticks (or piled boxes) to reach the banana. Gestalt = "whole".
  5. Bandura's four processes: attention (watch the model) → retention (remember) → reproduction (do it) → motivation (reward or praise).
  6. Bruner's three modes: enactive (doing with objects) → iconic (pictures) → symbolic (words and numbers). The spiral curriculum revisits a topic at higher levels each year.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • Experiments: cat = Thorndike; dog = Pavlov; rat/pigeon = Skinner; chimpanzee = Kohler; Bobo doll = Bandura. A "pigeon with Kohler" pair is wrong.
  • Programmed instruction comes from Skinner, not Thorndike.
  • The most important law of Thorndike is the law of effect.
  • Violent behaviour copied from the internet is social learning (Bandura), the 2025 answer.
  • Discovery learning is Bruner; insight is Kohler; both are cognitive, but they are not the same.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Day 1: Vygotsky's scaffolding and Bruner's discovery fit together; Bruner also used the word "scaffolding".
  • Topic 6: Thorndike's laws link to habit formation and transfer (identical elements).
  • Methodology: inductive method and discovery (Bruner); drill (Thorndike); reinforcement (Skinner).
  • Perspectives: NCF 2005's constructivism goes beyond behaviourism.

8. Memory technique

Animal chain: "Cat, Dog, Rat, Chimp, Doll" → Thorndike, Pavlov, Skinner, Kohler, Bandura → "The Pets Stay Kind By nature".

Thorndike's laws: "R-E-E" → "Ready, Exercise, Effect": first get ready, then exercise, then enjoy the effect.

Bandura: "A-R-R-M" → "Always Remember Role Models".

Topic 6: The Learning Process: Factors, Types, Dimensions, Curves, Transfer and Assessment

1. Basic idea

Topic 5 explained how learning happens. This topic looks at the learning process in the classroom: what helps or blocks it (factors), the steps a learner goes through (phases), the kinds of learning (types), the areas of learning (dimensions: cognitive, affective, psychomotor), how progress looks on a graph (learning curves), how one learning affects another (transfer), and how we check it (assessment).

2. Why it exists

A teacher needs to diagnose why learning is slow and plan for all three areas of growth: knowledge, feelings and skills. The SCERT syllabus lists all these under "Learning", and the 2025 paper asked about the psychomotor domain.

3. How it works

Factors affecting learning

Group Examples
Learner Health, intelligence, motivation, readiness, interest, attention
Teacher Knowledge, method, behaviour, use of teaching-learning material
Content Difficulty, meaningfulness, length, sequence
Environment Light, air, seating, noise, home support, peer group

Gagné's phases of learning (8): motivation → apprehending (attention) → acquisition → retention → recall → generalisation → performance → feedback.

Gagné's types of learning (8, simplest to most complex): (1) signal learning, (2) stimulus–response learning, (3) chaining (motor sequences), (4) verbal association, (5) multiple discrimination, (6) concept learning, (7) principle (rule) learning, (8) problem solving.

Dimensions of learning: Bloom's three domains

Domain Area Levels
Cognitive (Bloom, 1956) Thinking Knowledge → Comprehension → Application → Analysis → Synthesis → Evaluation
Revised cognitive (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) Thinking Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyse → Evaluate → Create
Affective (Krathwohl, 1964) Feelings, attitudes, values Receiving → Responding → Valuing → Organisation → Characterisation
Psychomotor (R.H. Dave, 1970) Physical skills Imitation → Manipulation → Precision → Articulation → Naturalisation

Learning curves

  • Convex (negatively accelerated): fast at first, then slow (easy tasks).
  • Concave (positively accelerated): slow at first, then fast (difficult tasks).
  • Mixed / S-shaped: a combination of both.
  • Plateau (పీఠభూమి): a flat stretch with no visible progress. Causes: fatigue, low interest, wrong method, or moving to a higher level of skill. Remedies: rest, motivation, a change of method.

Transfer of learning (అభ్యసన బదలాయింపు)

  • Positive: one learning helps another (cycling → motorbike).
  • Negative: one learning blocks another (driving on the left → driving on the right).
  • Zero: no effect.
  • Theories: mental (formal) discipline (the mind is a muscle); identical elements (Thorndike); generalisation (Judd); transposition (Gestalt).

Learning and assessment

Type When Purpose
Formative (assessment for learning) During teaching Find gaps and improve teaching
Summative (assessment of learning) End of a unit or term Grade and certify
Assessment as learning During learning, by the pupil Self-assessment and reflection
Diagnostic When a problem shows Find the exact difficulty → remedial teaching
Placement At the start Decide the right group or level

CCE (Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation): continuous = throughout the year; comprehensive = both scholastic and co-scholastic areas.

4. Everyday analogy

Learning to cook shows every part of this topic. Factors: a good teacher, a clean kitchen, interest in food. Phases: you want to cook, watch carefully, learn the steps, remember them, recall them next week, try a new dish, cook for guests, and hear their comments. Domains: knowing the recipe (cognitive), enjoying cooking (affective), cutting vegetables evenly (psychomotor). Plateau: your chapatis stop improving for a week. Transfer: making chapati helps with making paratha (positive).

5. Breaking the difficult parts

  1. Krathwohl's affective levels: receiving (paying attention) → responding (taking part; the 2025 trap) → valuing (accepting a value) → organisation (fitting values together) → characterisation (living by them).
  2. Dave's psychomotor levels: imitation (copying) → manipulation (doing from instructions) → precision (accuracy) → articulation (combining skills) → naturalisation (automatic, expert).
  3. Gagné's hierarchy: a higher type needs the lower ones first. You cannot solve problems (8) without rules (7) and concepts (6).
  4. Convex vs concave: easy task → quick start (convex); hard task → slow start (concave).
  5. Identical elements (Thorndike): transfer happens when the two tasks share common parts (Telugu grammar helps Sanskrit).
  6. Diagnostic → remedial: diagnose the exact difficulty first, then teach to remove it.

6. Exceptions and confusing areas

  • "Response/responding" is affective, not psychomotor, even though the word sounds like action (2025 paper).
  • "Valuing" is affective. Imitation, manipulation, precision, articulation and naturalisation are psychomotor.
  • The revised Bloom's taxonomy puts Create at the top; the original put Evaluation at the top.
  • A plateau is not the end of learning; progress usually resumes.
  • Formative vs summative: during vs end; improve vs grade.

7. Connections to other topics

  • Topic 5: Thorndike → identical elements; Skinner → immediate feedback in formative assessment.
  • Day 4: memory and forgetting are the retention and recall phases.
  • Methodology: writing lesson objectives in all three domains; CCE; remedial teaching.
  • Perspectives: RTE Section 29 (CCE) and NEP's competency-based assessment.

8. Memory technique
