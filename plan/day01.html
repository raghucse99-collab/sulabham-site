POLITY · Nature of the Indian Constitution and constitutional development

1. What is a Constitution? (రాజ్యాంగం)

A Constitution is the supreme law of a country. It (1) sets up the organs of government — legislature, executive, judiciary; (2) limits their power so that no one becomes all-powerful; and (3) gives citizens rights. Every other law must agree with it; a law that goes against it can be struck down by the courts. Think of it as the rule book of the game — players (governments) change, the rule book stays.

2. How it developed — British period (constitutional development)

Our Constitution did not appear suddenly; many of its features grew out of British Acts. Learn them as a chain: Company rule → Crown rule → Indians enter councils → elections → provincial autonomy → independence.

1773Regulating Act: Governor of Bengal → Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings); Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774)
1784Pitt's India Act: Board of Control — "double government" of Company + British Govt.
1833Charter Act: Governor-General of India (William Bentinck) — final step of centralisation
1853Charter Act: open competition for civil services; legislative work separated from executive
1858Govt of India Act: Crown takes over; Secretary of State for India; Viceroy (Canning)
1861Indian Councils Act: Indians nominated to councils; portfolio system; ordinance power
1892Indian Councils Act: indirect election begins; budget may be discussed
1909Morley–Minto: separate electorate for Muslims; S.P. Sinha first Indian in Viceroy's Executive Council
1919Montagu–Chelmsford: dyarchy in provinces; bicameral central legislature; direct elections

4. Nature / salient features — what kind of Constitution is it?

  • Written and lengthiest in the world — covers Centre, States, services, elections, emergency in one document.
  • Federal with a unitary bias (quasi-federal, as K.C. Wheare said): two levels of government, division of powers (7th Schedule), written & rigid Constitution, independent judiciary — but a strong Centre (single Constitution, single citizenship, Governor appointed by Centre, emergency powers, all-India services). Art. 1 calls India a "Union of States".
  • Parliamentary system (UK model): real power with the Council of Ministers responsible to the Lok Sabha; President is the nominal head.
  • Partly rigid, partly flexible: some parts change by simple majority, most need special majority, federal parts also need half the States (Art. 368).
  • Sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic — "socialist" and "secular" added by the 42nd Amendment, 1976.
  • Fundamental Rights + DPSP + Fundamental Duties (added 1976); independent integrated judiciary; universal adult franchise; single citizenship; emergency provisions.

ECONOMY · National income: concept and measurement

1. What is national income? (జాతీయ ఆదాయం)

National income is the money value of all final goods and services produced in a country in one year. It tells us how big the economy is and whether it is growing.

Why "final" only? To avoid double counting. Example: a farmer sells wheat for ₹20 → a miller makes flour worth ₹30 → a baker makes bread worth ₹50. Counting all three gives ₹100, but only ₹50 of new value was produced. So we count the final bread (₹50) — or add the value added at each stage: 20 + 10 + 20 = ₹50.

2. The key aggregates — learn the 3 switches

SwitchRuleRemember
Domestic → NationalGNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA = income earned by Indians abroad − income earned by foreigners in India)Domestic = inside borders; National = by nationals. India's NFIA is negative → GNP < GDP
Gross → NetNet = Gross − depreciation (wear and tear of machines = consumption of fixed capital)NDP = GDP − depreciation
Market price → Factor costFactor cost = Market price − indirect taxes + subsidiesTaxes raise the shop price; subsidies lower it

National Income (in the strict sense) = NNP at factor cost. Per capita income = National income ÷ population (a rough measure of average living standard).

India's present method: since 2015 the government reports GVA at basic prices by sector and GDP = GVA + product taxes − product subsidies. Base year has been 2011-12; MoSPI has been revising it to a newer base — always check the latest Economic Survey.

3. Nominal vs real GDP

Nominal GDP = at current year's prices (rises even if only prices rise). Real GDP = at constant prices of a base year (shows real growth in output). GDP deflator = Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP × 100 — a broad measure of inflation.
