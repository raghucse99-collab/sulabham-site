HISTORY · Indus Valley Civilization

Understand it first

What is it? About 4,500 years ago, people in the plains of the Indus and its neighbouring rivers began to live in planned towns with brick houses, drains, standard weights and measures, and long-distance trade. This is India's first urban (city) civilization. 'Urban' means that not everyone is a farmer: some are traders, craftsmen and officials. That needs surplus food, so farming must be productive first. Why does it matter for the exam? It is the starting point of Indian history, and APPSC asks about its sites, town planning, crafts, trade, seals and decline every year.

Map of the major Indus Valley sites. Find Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Lothal, Dholavira, Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Discovery, names and time

  • Discovery: 1921, Daya Ram Sahni found Harappa (Montgomery district, now Pakistan); 1922, R.D. Banerji found Mohenjo-daro (Sindh). John Marshall, Director-General of the ASI, announced it in 1924. After Independence, Ropar (Punjab) was the first Harappan site excavated in India.
  • Names: Harappan Civilization (after the first site found) and Indus Valley Civilization (because many sites lie on the Indus). Most sites actually lie along the dry Ghaggar–Hakra river, so scholars also use 'Indus–Sarasvati' or 'Sindhu–Sarasvati'; the exam usually says Indus Valley or Harappan.
  • Time (approximate): Early Harappan c. 3300–2600 BCE; Mature Harappan c. 2600–1900 BCE; Late Harappan c. 1900–1300 BCE. The roots go back to farming villages such as Mehrgarh (Balochistan, Pakistan) c. 7000 BCE, where wheat, barley and cotton were already used. It is a Bronze Age culture: copper and bronze, no iron.
  • Extent: about 1.25 million sq km, larger than Egypt and Mesopotamia put together. Limits to remember: Sutkagen-dor (west, near the Iran border), Alamgirpur (east, Uttar Pradesh), Manda (north, Jammu), Daimabad (south, Maharashtra).

2. The big idea: a planned town

Ruins of Mohenjo-daro: straight streets and brick walls of a planned town. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

Imagine a town built by a plan, not by chance. Streets met at right angles (a grid). Houses were built of standard-size burnt bricks (the ratio 1 : 2 : 4) around a courtyard; most had a bathroom and a well, and the doors opened to side lanes, not the main street. Waste water ran into covered drains with inspection holes along the street. The town had two parts: a raised citadel (west) for public buildings, and a lower town (east) where most people lived. Such care shows a strong civic authority. (Contrast: most later Indian towns grew without such planning.)

3. Farming, food and animals

Rivers flooded every year and left fertile silt, so farming gave a surplus without big canals. Crops: wheat and barley (main), peas, sesame, mustard, dates; cotton (among the earliest in the world); rice appears at Lothal and Rangpur (Gujarat) as husk. Animals: humped bull (zebu), buffalo, sheep, goat, pig, dog; camel bones and elephants are known; the horse is not clearly present. Ploughed field at Kalibangan proves ploughing. Stored grain was kept in granaries (Harappa, Lothal).

4. Crafts and art: pottery, seals, figurines, bronzes, beads

Harappans were skilled craftsmen. These are the items the exam asks about most:

  • Pottery: made on the potter's wheel and baked in kilns. Most is plain red ware; the finer ware has a red slip with black painted designs: pipal leaves, fish scales, intersecting circles, wavy lines, chequers, trees, birds and animals. Special types: perforated jars (for straining or fermenting), pointed-base storage jars, goblets, S-shaped jars. Polychrome pottery is rare. Some pots carry signs scratched after firing (graffiti).
  • Seals: about 2,000 found, mostly of steatite (soft stone), square, with an animal and a few signs. The commonest animal is the 'unicorn' (one-horned bull); others are the humped bull, elephant, tiger, rhinoceros. Used to stamp trade goods and as identity marks; the Pashupati seal shows a horned figure in yoga posture with animals around.
  • Terracotta (baked clay) figurines: mostly female figures, called mother goddess; also toy bullock carts, whistles, rattles, animal models and dice.
  • Bronze and stone: the bronze Dancing Girl of Mohenjo-daro (about 10 cm tall) was made by the lost-wax (cire perdue) method: a wax model is covered with clay, the wax melts out, and molten bronze fills the mould. The steatite 'Priest-King' bust from Mohenjo-daro wears a patterned shawl.
  • Beads and jewellery: carnelian (etched beads), agate, gold, copper, shell and faience (glazed paste) beads; shell bangles; ivory. Bead workshops: Chanhudaro and Lothal.

5. Trade, weights and script

  • Internal trade: copper from Khetri (Rajasthan), shell and carnelian from Gujarat, and other goods moved by bullock carts and boats.
  • Foreign trade: with Mesopotamia (called Meluhha in its texts), also Dilmun (Bahrain) and Magan (Oman). Lapis lazuli came from Afghanistan (Shortughai). Harappan seals and beads have been found in Mesopotamian cities such as Ur. Lothal was the port; Sutkagen-dor and Balakot were coastal outposts.
  • Weights and measures: cubical chert weights in a ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 : 8 up to 64, then 160, 320 and so on (binary first, then decimal); a bronze/ivory scale was used. Such standards show state control of trade.
  • Script: pictographic, about 400 signs, mostly written right to left; found on seals, pots and the Dholavira signboard. It is still undeciphered, so we cannot read Harappan names, laws or history.

6. Religion, burial and society

  • Religion: no certain temples. Evidence of mother-goddess worship, a horned deity (Pashupati), tree (pipal) and animal worship, phallus-like stones (linga), and fire altars at Kalibangan and Lothal.
  • Burial: the dead were usually buried in an extended position with pots and ornaments; cremation and a few double burials (Lothal) are also found. No huge royal tombs.
  • Society and rule: no palace or royal tomb found, so who ruled is debated (priests, merchants, or a council). Different house sizes show some social difference; the Citadel suggests a ruling group.

7. Why did it decline? (a causes list, not one answer)

There is no single agreed cause. Scholars point to: change of climate and drying of the Ghaggar–Hakra river, floods and river shifting, falling trade with Mesopotamia, and breakdown of city control. The old 'Aryan invasion' idea has no evidence of a city-wide massacre and is now largely rejected. After about 1900 BCE people moved east and south into smaller villages (Late Harappan): the cities faded, but the culture did not vanish overnight.

Exam trap

Great Bath: Mohenjo-daro; granary: Harappa; dockyard: Lothal; water system: Dholavira; ploughed field: Kalibangan; largest in India: Rakhigarhi; first post-1947 dig: Ropar. Seals are of steatite, not iron or gold. No iron, no certain horse, script undeciphered. Do not write 'Mohenjo-daro in India': it is in Pakistan.

POLITY · Nature of the Indian Constitution and constitutional development

Understand it first

What is a Constitution? It is the supreme rule book of a country. It does three jobs: it creates the organs of government (the legislature that makes laws, the executive that runs them, the judiciary that decides disputes); it limits their power so nobody becomes all-powerful; and it gives citizens rights. Every ordinary law must agree with it. Think of a cricket match: players change every season, but the rules stay, and the umpire can reject any play that breaks them. Why it matters: the Group 2 syllabus begins with this topic, and the rest of Polity (rights, Parliament, courts) all builds on it.

1. How it developed: the story in five steps

Our Constitution did not fall from the sky. Many of its ideas were tried first under British rule. Learn the story, then the years: Company runs India → Crown takes over → Indians enter the councils → elections begin → provinces get self-rule → independence.

2. How the Constitution was made: the Constituent Assembly

A Constituent Assembly is a body elected to write a constitution. Ours was formed under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946): members were elected indirectly by the provincial assemblies (princely states sent nominees). It had 389 planned seats (296 British India + 93 princely states); after Partition about 299 remained.

3. What kind of Constitution is it? (its nature)

  • Written and the longest in the world, because it covers the Centre, States, services, elections and emergency in one document.
  • Federal with a unitary bias (K.C. Wheare: 'quasi-federal'). Two levels of government and a division of powers (Seventh Schedule), but a strong Centre: single Constitution, single citizenship, Governor appointed by the Centre, emergency powers. Article 1 calls India a 'Union of States'.
  • Parliamentary system (British model): real power with the Council of Ministers responsible to the Lok Sabha.
  • Flexible and rigid together: some parts change by a simple majority, others need a special majority, and a few also need half the States.
Example

Example: Why a 'federal with unitary bias' label? If a State Government fails, the Centre can impose President's Rule (Art 356). A pure federation like the USA has no such power. That one rule shows the 'unitary bias'.

Exam trap

Adopted on 26 Nov 1949 but enforced on 26 Jan 1950 (the day was chosen because Purna Swaraj was declared on 26 Jan 1930). The temporary President was Sachchidananda Sinha, not Rajendra Prasad. 'Union of States', not 'Federation', is the wording of Article 1.

REASONING · Number series

Understand it first

What is it? A number series is a list that follows a hidden rule. Your job is to find the rule and give the next number. The exam gives about 1–2 such questions and each takes under 40 seconds once you know the five common rules. Method: (1) look at the gaps between numbers; (2) if gaps are not equal, look at the gaps of the gaps; (3) test multiplication; (4) test squares/cubes; (5) test two interleaved series.

RuleExampleAnswer and why
Add a constant2, 5, 8, 11, 14, ?17: each term +3
Multiply by a constant3, 6, 12, 24, 48, ?96: each term ×2
Squares / cubes1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ? and 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, ?36 (6²) and 216 (6³)
Gaps grow2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?42: gaps 4, 6, 8, 10, so next gap 12
Multiply then add3, 7, 15, 31, 63, ?127: each term ×2 + 1
Two series mixed5, 10, 8, 16, 14, 28, ?26: alternately ×2 and −2
Prime numbers2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, ?17: next prime
Example

Worked example: 7, 10, 16, 28, 52, ? Gaps: 3, 6, 12, 24. The gaps double. Next gap = 48, so the answer is 52 + 48 = 100.
