HISTORY · The Gupta Empire: administration, economy, art, literature

Understand it first

What is it? After the Mauryas and a gap of about 500 years, the Guptas (c. 320–550 CE) again united north India from Magadha (Pataliputra). This age is often called the 'Golden Age' because of great progress in literature, science and art, but remember that the pillars of that glory (land grants, rigid castes, falling trade) also began the feudal pattern of the next centuries. Learn it again as Sources → Rulers → Politics → Society → Economy → Religion → Art, literature, science → Decline.

Map of the Gupta Empire at its height. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Sources

SourceWhat it tells
Allahabad Pillar inscription (Prayaga Prashasti)In Sanskrit, composed by Harishena, court poet of Samudragupta. Lists his conquests. The same Ashokan pillar also carries Ashoka's edict.
Fa-Hien (Faxian)Chinese Buddhist monk who visited India (c. 399–414 CE) in the reign of Chandragupta II; describes mild laws, no passports, the chandalas living outside towns, and prosperity.
Other inscriptionsMehrauli iron pillar (Chandra = Chandragupta II); Eran (first sati, 510 CE); Junagadh (Skandagupta repaired the Sudarshana lake); Bhitari (Skandagupta and Huns); Mathura.
Coins and textsGold coins called dinars (also suvarna), silver rupaka, copper coins. Types: lyrist (Samudragupta plays the veena), archer, Ashvamedha, tiger-slayer. Texts: Kalidasa's works, Devichandraguptam and Mudrarakshasa (Vishakhadatta), Puranas, Narada and Brihaspati smritis.
A gold coin (dinar) of Samudragupta: Gupta rulers issued a large number of gold coins. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

2. The rulers, in order

RulerYears (CE)Key facts
Sri Gupta, Ghatotkachac. 240–319Founders of the dynasty (small rulers, titled Maharaja).
Chandragupta Ic. 319/320–335First important king, title Maharajadhiraja. The Gupta era starts from his accession (319–320 CE). Married Kumaradevi, a Lichchhavi princess, which brought political strength (shown on the Chandragupta–Kumaradevi coins).
Samudraguptac. 335–375Called the 'Napoleon of India' (V.A. Smith). The Allahabad Prashasti: in Aryavarta he uprooted 9 kings; in Dakshinapatha he captured and then released 12 kings (a policy of 'grahana-moksha-anugraha'); forest tribes and frontier states paid tribute. Performed the Ashvamedha; a musician (lyrist coins); a poet, titled Kaviraja. Sri Lankan king Meghavarna got his permission to build a monastery at Bodh Gaya.
Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya)c. 375–415Defeated the Shakas of Western India (Rudrasimha III), making Ujjain a second capital. Gave his daughter Prabhavati Gupta to the Vakataka king Rudrasena II. Navaratnas (nine gems) at his court, including Kalidasa. Fa-Hien visited. Title Vikramaditya.
Kumaragupta Ic. 415–455Founded the Nalanda Mahavihara (later a great university). Performed the Ashvamedha. First Huna pressure.
Skandaguptac. 455–467Repelled the Huns (Hephthalites); repaired the Sudarshana lake (Junagadh inscription). After him the empire weakened: Budhagupta, later kings; the Huns (Toramana, Mihirakula) took north-west India. The line ended c. 550 CE.
Example

Andhra link (all regions equal): the Allahabad Prashasti names Samudragupta's southern opponents, identified with the Andhra–Kalinga coast and Krishna–Godavari region: Hastivarman of Vengi (Salankayana, near Eluru), Ugrasena of Palakka (Nellore area), Kubera of Devarashtra, Damana of Erandapalla, Nilaraja of Avamukta, Mantaraja of Kurala, Vishnugopa of Kanchi (Pallava). Some identifications are debated, but the names Vengi and Palakka are a favourite exam point.

3. Politics · Society · Economy · Religion of the Guptas

AreaGupta period
PoliticsKing's titles: Maharajadhiraja, Parama Bhattaraka. Council of ministers; Sandhivigrahika (war and peace), Mahabaladhikrita (army), Kumaramatya (high officers), Mahadandanayaka. Bhukti (province, ruled by Uparika) → Vishaya (district, Vishayapati) → Vithi → Grama (village, Gramika). A less centralised state than the Mauryan: feudatories (Samantas) and land grants with administrative rights; punishments were milder; separate civil and criminal law (Narada and Brihaspati smritis). Taxes: bhaga, bhoga, kara, udranga, forced labour vishti.
SocietyCaste became more rigid: many sub-castes; chandalas (untouchables) lived outside towns and beat clappers (Fa-Hien); the Kayastha (scribes) rose as a new group. Status of women fell: child marriage, the first evidence of sati in the Eran inscription (510 CE, Goparaja's wife). Shudras could listen to epics and Puranas; slavery continued.
EconomyLargest number of gold coins (dinars) in ancient India, but gold purity fell later. Land grants to Brahmins and temples (agrahara, brahmadeya, devadana) with rights to revenue: a start of feudalism. Roman trade declined, towns shrank, though guilds (shrenis), silk and ivory continued; ports Tamralipti, Broach. Agriculture and irrigation (Sudarshana lake).
ReligionBrahmanism revived: Vedic rites (Ashvamedha), Vaishnavism favoured (kings called Parama Bhagavata, emblem Garuda, Vishnu's Dashavatara), Shaivism and Shaktism grew. Structural temples began. Buddhism (Mahayana, Nalanda) and Jainism continued (Valabhi council c. 5th–6th century).

4. Art, architecture, literature and science

Bodhisattva Padmapani, Ajanta Cave 1 (c. 5th century CE): the finest example of Indian wall painting of the age. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
FieldDetails
TemplesFirst structural temples (flat roof, then early shikhara): Dashavatara temple, Deogarh (stone, with Shesha-shayi Vishnu panel); Bhitargaon (brick, Kanpur); Tigawa; Udayagiri caves (Vidisha: Varaha panel); Sanchi temple 17.
Sculpture, paintingSarnath Buddha (calm, plain robe, halo) and Mathura school; Ajanta paintings (caves 16, 17, 1: Jataka stories, Padmapani); Bagh caves. Metal work: Mehrauli iron pillar and the bronze Buddha of Sultanganj.
LiteratureKalidasa: *Abhijnana Shakuntalam*, *Meghaduta*, *Raghuvamsha*, *Kumarasambhava*, *Ritusamhara*, *Malavikagnimitra*, *Vikramorvasiya*. Shudraka: *Mrichchhakatika*. Vishakhadatta: *Mudrarakshasa*. Amarasimha: *Amarakosha*. Vishnu Sharma: *Panchatantra*. Vatsyayana: *Kamasutra*. Languages: Sanskrit as court language, Prakrit for common speech in plays.
ScienceAryabhata: *Aryabhatiya* (499 CE), the place-value system and decimal, value of pi about 3.1416, earth rotates on its axis, causes of eclipses. Varahamihira: *Brihat Samhita*, *Panchasiddhantika*. Vagbhata: *Ashtanga Hridaya* (medicine). Charaka and Sushruta samhitas were compiled earlier. Nalanda was a centre of learning.

5. Decline

Causes: (1) Hun invasions (Toramana, Mihirakula); (2) weak later rulers and quarrels; (3) growth of powerful feudatories (Samantas) and land grants that drained revenue; (4) decline of trade and towns, less money; (5) rise of regional powers: Vakatakas, Maukharis, Later Guptas, Yashodharman of Malwa, Pushyabhutis.

Exam trap

Allahabad Pillar: Harishena (Samudragupta); Mehrauli iron pillar: Chandra; Fa-Hien under Chandragupta II; Nalanda: Kumaragupta I; Huns repelled: Skandagupta; sati at Eran (510 CE); Aryabhatiya (499 CE); Brihat Samhita: Varahamihira (not Aryabhata); Mudrarakshasa: Vishakhadatta. Gupta rule was less centralised than Mauryan. Gupta gold coins are called dinars; Mauryan silver coins were punch-marked panas.

POLITY · Fundamental Rights II: Articles 20–22, 23–24, 25–30, 32 and writs

Understand it first

What is it? Day 4 covered equality and the six freedoms (Arts 14–19). Today the rest of Part III: protection in criminal trials and life and liberty (Arts 20–22), no exploitation (23–24), freedom of religion (25–28), minorities (29–30) and the remedy: the right to go to court (Art 32). Think of Art 32 as the 'lock' that protects all the other rights.

1. Right to Freedom (continued): Articles 20, 21, 21A, 22

ArticleWhat it saysKey points and cases
Art 20Protection in respect of conviction for offences: (1) no ex-post-facto law (no punishment for an act that was not an offence when done, and no higher penalty than the law then in force); (2) no double jeopardy (not prosecuted and punished twice for the same offence); (3) no self-incrimination (nobody forced to be a witness against himself).Available to all persons (citizens and foreigners), only in criminal proceedings. Cannot be suspended even during an emergency (Art 359, after the 44th Amendment).
Art 21Protection of life and personal liberty: no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.A.K. Gopalan (1950): narrow reading. Maneka Gandhi (1978): the procedure must be just, fair and reasonable; Arts 14, 19 and 21 are connected, so Art 21 became a source of many rights: privacy (K.S. Puttaswamy, 2017), livelihood (Olga Tellis, 1985), speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979), clean environment, dignity, shelter, health, free legal aid, right to die with dignity (passive euthanasia: Common Cause 2018). Applies to all persons; cannot be suspended in emergency.
Art 21ARight to education: free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14.Added by the 86th Amendment (2002); the RTE Act 2009 came into force on 1 April 2010.
Art 22Protection against arrest and detention. Ordinary arrest: must be told the grounds, right to consult and be defended by a lawyer, and must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours (excluding travel time).These protections do not apply to enemy aliens and to preventive detention (detaining to prevent a future crime). Preventive detention up to 3 months without the opinion of an Advisory Board (the 44th Amendment's change to 2 months is not yet in force). Laws: PD Act 1950, MISA 1971, COFEPOSA 1974, NSA 1980, UAPA. D.K. Basu (1997): arrest guidelines.

2. Right against Exploitation: Articles 23 and 24

ArticleMeaningNotes
Art 23Prohibits traffic in human beings, begar (unpaid forced labour) and other similar forced labour.Available even against private persons. Exception: the State may impose compulsory service for public purposes (e.g., military or national service). Laws: Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956, Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976. PUDR v. Union of India (1982, Asiad workers): paying less than minimum wages is forced labour.
Art 24No child below 14 to work in any factory, mine or other hazardous employment.Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986, amended in 2016 (ban on all work below 14, with exceptions for family work; adolescents 14–18 barred from hazardous work). M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996): Sivakasi match factories.

3. Right to Freedom of Religion: Articles 25–28

ArticleMeaningNotes
Art 25Freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, to all persons.Subject to public order, morality, health; the State can regulate secular activity linked to religion and provide social reform (e.g., temple entry for all Hindu classes). Wearing and carrying the kirpan is part of the Sikh religion. Shirur Mutt (1954): 'essential religious practices' test.
Art 26Every religious denomination may establish and maintain institutions, manage its own affairs in religion, own and administer property.Right of a group (denomination), not an individual.
Art 27No person can be forced to pay taxes whose proceeds go to promote or maintain any particular religion.Fees are allowed; taxes are not.
Art 28No religious instruction in institutions wholly maintained by the State; in aided or recognised institutions it is voluntary.Bijoe Emmanuel (1986): students may refuse to sing the national anthem on religious grounds, but must not disrespect it. S.R. Bommai (1994): secularism is part of the basic structure.

4. Cultural and Educational Rights: Articles 29–30

ArticleMeaningNotes
Art 29Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it. No citizen can be denied admission to a State-run or State-aided institution only on grounds of religion, race, caste or language.Protects both minorities and majority sections (only for citizens).
Art 30All minorities, based on religion or language, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.State cannot discriminate in giving aid on this ground. If the State acquires such property, it must pay compensation (44th Amendment). T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002): minority status is decided State-wise.

5. Right to Constitutional Remedies: Article 32 and the writs

Art 32 gives every person the right to move the Supreme Court directly to enforce Fundamental Rights. Dr. Ambedkar called it the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution. The Court can issue directions, orders or writs. Art 32 itself is a Fundamental Right; it can be suspended only during an emergency (Art 359). High Courts have the same power under Art 226, which is wider: they can issue writs for FRs and for any other legal right. Writs are an idea taken from English law.

The five writs (Articles 32 and 226)
WritMeaningWhen used
Habeas Corpus'You may have the body'Court orders the person who has detained someone to produce him in court so that the legality of detention can be tested. Can be used against public authorities and private persons. Protects personal liberty.
Mandamus'We command'Orders a public official or body to do a public legal duty they have refused to do. Not issued against private individuals (generally), the President or Governors, or for a duty that is only discretionary.
Prohibition'To forbid'A higher court forbids a lower court or tribunal from going beyond its jurisdiction while a case is still pending. Used against judicial and quasi-judicial bodies only.
Certiorari'To be certified'A higher court quashes an order already passed by a lower court or tribunal for want of jurisdiction or an error of law. (Prohibition is before the decision; certiorari is after.)
Quo Warranto'By what authority'Court asks a person by what authority he holds a public office and removes him if he holds it illegally. Only for a substantive public office created by law (not private or ministerial posts).

Other articles of Part III: Art 33 lets Parliament restrict FRs of the armed forces and police; Art 34 limits FRs while martial law is in force; Art 35 gives Parliament (not States) the power to make laws for certain FRs. ADM Jabalpur (1976) held that habeas corpus could not be used in the Emergency, but it was overruled in Puttaswamy (2017).

Example

Example: Ravi is arrested and kept in a police station for 3 days without being shown to a magistrate. This breaks Art 22(2) (24 hours). His family can ask the High Court or the Supreme Court for a habeas corpus (Art 226 or 32) to get him produced. If a man holds the post of a public official illegally, a quo warranto petition challenges his authority; if an order of a tribunal exceeded its powers, certiorari can cancel it.

Exam trap

Arts 20 and 21 cannot be suspended in an emergency. Art 20 is for criminal matters only. Art 22 protections do not apply to preventive detention. Art 21A: 86th Amendment, ages 6–14. Habeas corpus: against private persons too; mandamus: not against private persons, President or Governors. Prohibition = before, certiorari = after. Quo warranto: public office only. Art 32 = Supreme Court; Art 226 = High Court (wider). Art 29 can be used by any section of citizens; Art 30 by minorities.

REASONING · Shapes and their sub-sections (figures and counting)

Understand it first

What is it? A figure (a triangle with many lines, a grid of squares, a polygon) is given, and you must count how many triangles, squares or rectangles it contains, of every size. The secret: never count by eye. Count systematically: name the points with letters, count the smallest pieces first, then pieces made of 2, 3 … small pieces, and use a formula to check.

Three standard counting figures
CaseFormulaExample
Triangles with lines from the apex to n divisions of the basen(n+1)/2n = 5: 5×6/2 = 15
Line segments on a line with n pointsn(n−1)/26 points: 6×5/2 = 15
Diagonals of an n-sided polygonn(n−3)/2Octagon: 8×5/2 = 20; hexagon: 9
Rectangles (including squares) in an m×n gridm(m+1)×n(n+1)/43×3: 3×4×3×4/4 = 36; 3×4: 60
Squares in an n×n grid1² + 2² + … + n² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/63×3: 14; 4×4: 30; chessboard 8×8: 204
Triangle of side n cut by lines parallel to its sides (n = 3)Up-pointing + down-pointing6 + 3 + 1 = 10 up; 3 down; total 13 (n = 4: 27)
Example

Steps for any figure: (1) letter the corners; (2) count single small figures; (3) count figures made of 2 parts, then 3 parts and so on; (4) add; (5) check with a formula if one fits. In a triangle with lines from the apex, every triangle is fixed by choosing two points on the base: with 6 points that is 6×5/2 = 15 pairs.

Exam trap

Do not forget bigger figures made of several small pieces and, in squares, the tilted squares if the figure shows diagonals. In a grid, rectangles include squares; if the question says 'rectangles that are not squares', subtract the squares (3×3 grid: 36 − 14 = 22).
