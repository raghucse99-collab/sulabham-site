HISTORY · Harshavardhana and his achievements; Ancient art and architecture

Understand it first

What is it? Harshavardhana (606–647 CE) was the last great Hindu king of north India before the Rajput age and the Turkish invasions. After the Guptas fell (Day 5), north India split into small states. Harsha joined many of them again, but his empire was a loose union of friendly and subordinate kings that broke up when he died. We know him unusually well because we have both a court biography and a foreigner's eyewitness account. In the second half of today we also learn ancient Indian art and architecture from the Indus cities to the Guptas, because exam questions often ask 'which monument belongs to which period'.

2. His rise and conquests

  • Family: the Pushyabhuti (Vardhana) dynasty of Thanesar (Haryana). Father Prabhakaravardhana (Maharajadhiraja), mother Yashomati, elder brother Rajyavardhana, sister Rajyashri.
  • 606 CE: Rajyashri's husband, the Maukhari king Grahavarman of Kanauj, was killed by the Malwa king Devagupta; Rajyavardhana avenged but was murdered by Shashanka of Gauda (Bengal). Harsha, aged 16, became king and started the Harsha era (606 CE).
  • He rescued Rajyashri (who was about to commit sati) and ruled both Thanesar and Kanauj, moving his capital to Kanauj. Titles: Shiladitya, Rajaputra.
  • Allies and conquests: friendship with Bhaskaravarman of Kamarupa (Assam) against Shashanka; he won parts of Punjab, Bihar, Bengal and Orissa, and Valabhi (Gujarat) accepted him (Dhruvasena II married Harsha's daughter); his sway reached from Punjab to Orissa (Ganjam).
  • Defeat: around 618–619 CE Pulakeshin II (Chalukya of Badami) stopped him on the Narmada; Harsha could not go south. The Aihole inscription records it. Harsha's southern boundary was the Narmada.
  • 647 CE: died without an heir; minister Arunashva (Arjuna) seized the throne and the empire broke up. (Kanauj later became the prize of the Tripartite Struggle.)

3. Politics · Society · Economy · Religion of Harsha's age

AreaHarsha's period
PoliticsKing himself active and travelling (the camp, skandhavara); officers: Mahasandhivigrahadhikrita (war and peace), Senapati, Mahapratihara (palace), Dutaka. Divisions Bhukti → Vishaya → Pathaka → Grama. Feudal tendency: ministers and officials were paid by land grants (Xuanzang), not cash, and feudatories (Samantas) kept their own armies. Revenue: one-sixth of produce; few taxes; forced labour was light. Revenue was divided into four parts: king's expenses, scholars, officials, religious gifts. Harsha toured to see the people.
SocietyFour varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) plus many mixed castes; Shudras mostly cultivators (Xuanzang); outcastes (butchers, scavengers) lived outside towns; some women (Rajyashri) took part in public life, but the practice of sati and purdah-like seclusion were known; Xuanzang praised honesty but also noted harsh punishments and the trial by ordeal.
EconomyAgriculture main; fewer coins and less trade than Gupta age, towns declined (Xuanzang saw many ruined towns, though Kanauj was rich); more land grants to Brahmins, monasteries and officials; Nalanda supported by the revenue of 100 villages.
ReligionHarsha first worshipped Shiva and the Sun (his family were Shaiva and Surya devotees), later became a Mahayana Buddhist under Xuanzang's influence, but supported all faiths. Kanauj assembly (643 CE) in Xuanzang's honour; Prayag Mahamoksha Parishad every five years where he gave away all his wealth (except the royal clothes). He built stupas, monasteries and rest houses; Nalanda was at its peak.
LiteratureCourt poets Banabhatta, Mayura (Surya Shataka), Divakara; Harsha's plays; scholars of Nalanda: Shilabhadra, Dharmapala.
Exam trap

Harsha era 606 CE; capital Thanesar → Kanauj; defeated by Pulakeshin II on the Narmada (Aihole, Ravikirti); Banabhatta = Harshacharita; Xuanzang = Si-yu-ki, studied at Nalanda; Kanauj assembly 643, Prayag every 5 years; officials paid in land; Harsha the last Hindu emperor of north India (traditional phrase); Shashanka (Gauda) was a Shaiva who persecuted Buddhists (Bodhi tree cut).

PART B · Ancient Indian art and architecture (up to the Guptas)

Understand it first

Why learn it as a story? Art follows patronage: who paid, and for what faith. Cities and trade built the Indus art; the Mauryan state built polished stone pillars; Buddhism spread stupas and caves; the Kushans and Satavahanas gave us regional schools; the Guptas gave the first stone and brick temples. The stupa: what it is and its parts

A stupa is a solid dome that holds the relics of the Buddha or a monk (it began as a burial mound). The people walk around it clockwise for worship. It has no inside room. Chaitya is the hall for prayer, vihara is the monastery.

POLITY · Directive Principles and their relationship with Fundamental Rights

Understand it first

What is it? Fundamental Rights (Day 4–5) tell the State what it must not do to you. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) tell the State what it should do for you: build a welfare state with social and economic justice. They are in Part IV (Articles 36–51), borrowed from the Irish Constitution, which took the idea from Spain. Granville Austin called FRs and DPSPs the 'conscience of the Constitution' and Dr. Ambedkar called DPSP a 'novel feature'. Why needed? Political freedom is empty without food, work and education.

1. Nature

  • Art 36: 'State' has the same meaning as in Part III. Art 37: they are not enforceable by any court, yet are fundamental in the governance of the country, and it is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.
  • They are positive directions to the legislature and executive; they give moral and political (not legal) force; the people judge governments by them at elections.
  • They aim at social and economic democracy (FRs give political democracy). Courts use them to interpret laws and to test whether a restriction on a right is reasonable.

4. The conflict and how the courts settled it

Year / case or lawHolding
State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)FRs prevail over DPSP; DPSP must run as a subsidiary to FRs. Parliament can still amend FRs to implement DPSP (First Amendment).
Golaknath (1967)Parliament cannot amend FRs at all (later overruled).
25th Amendment (1971) – Art 31CLaws made to carry out Art 39(b) and (c) cannot be challenged for violating Arts 14, 19; courts cannot question them if they say they give effect to these.
Kesavananda Bharati (1973)Basic structure doctrine; the first part of Art 31C upheld, the part barring judicial review struck down.
42nd Amendment (1976)Extended Art 31C to all DPSPs (they would prevail over Arts 14, 19, 31).
Minerva Mills (1980)Struck down that extension; harmony and balance between FRs and DPSPs is part of the basic structure. FRs and DPSPs are the 'two wheels of a chariot'; neither is absolute.
Unni Krishnan (1993) and laterCourt read DPSPs into FRs: right to education (Art 21), then Art 21A (2002); environment, health and legal aid through Art 21.
Example

DPSPs turned into laws: abolition of zamindari, Minimum Wages Act, Equal Remuneration Act 1976 (Art 39d), 73rd and 74th Amendments (Art 40), Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 (39A), Environment Protection Act 1986 (48A), RTE Act 2009, MGNREGA 2005 and National Food Security Act 2013 (41, 47), Maternity Benefit Act (42).

Exam trap

Part IV = Arts 36–51; Irish source; Art 37 non-justiciable. Art 40 panchayats (Gandhian), Art 44 UCC (liberal-intellectual), 39A and 43A: 42nd Amendment, 43B: 97th, 45 changed by 86th, 38(2): 44th. Cases: Champakam (FR over DPSP), Kesavananda (31C part), Minerva Mills (harmony, 42nd Amendment extension struck).

REASONING · Statement and assumptions

Understand it first

What is it? A statement is given, with some assumptions. An assumption is something the speaker takes for granted without saying it: an idea that must be true for the statement to make sense. You must decide which assumptions are implicit (hidden in the statement). Test (negation test): deny the assumption; if the statement then loses its meaning or becomes pointless, the assumption is implicit.

RuleMeaningExample
1. Not a fact already statedRepeating the statement is not an assumption.'Buses are late' → 'Buses are late' ✗
2. Not a conclusionWhat follows from the statement is not what lies behind it.'Many will come' as a result ✗
3. Extreme words are usually wrongall, only, always, never, none, best, every go beyond the statement.'Only this works' ✗
4. No outside knowledgeUse only what the statement needs; do not bring your own facts.—
5. It must support the statementIt should be a necessary base: people believe the action works, can be done, and has some purpose.'Use app X' → people can use apps ✓
Example

Worked example: Statement: *'Apply online for your certificate; do not visit the office.'* Assumption I: *Most applicants can use the internet.* Deny it: if nobody can go online, the advice is pointless, so I is implicit. Assumption II: *Offices will be closed.* Not needed for the advice, so not implicit. Answer: Only I. Typical patterns: advice or order (assumes it can be followed and will help), advertisement (assumes people want and believe it), a decision or plan (assumes it is possible and useful), a comparison (assumes the other is worse or different).

Exam trap

Assumption ≠ conclusion ≠ fact in the statement. Statements with 'only', 'all', 'always' in the assumption are usually wrong. Do not stop at the first assumption: check each one separately. Answer options: only I, only II, either, neither, both.
