HISTORY · The Cholas: administrative system

Understand it first

What is it? The Cholas ruled much of South India from about 850 to 1279 CE. At their peak (Rajaraja I and Rajendra I) they ruled the Tamil country, parts of Andhra and Karnataka, Sri Lanka, and sent a fleet across the sea to South-East Asia. Why study them? They gave India its best-documented village self-government (Uttaramerur), a clear revenue and land system, a powerful navy, and the great Dravida temples. Learn them as Sources → Rulers → Administration → Politics · Society · Economy · Religion → Art and literature → Decline.

The Chola Empire at its greatest extent (c. 1030 CE, under Rajendra I). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

3. The administrative system

The king was the head of everything, hereditary, the Yuvaraja was often named while the king lived. He was advised by a council of officers (perundanam = high officers, sirutanam = lower officers) and royal orders (olai) were written by the olai-nayakam and approved by the Oluvai (secretary). He toured and heard petitions. The state was a mix of centralised control of revenue and army and local autonomy in villages.

Chola territorial administration
UnitDetails
MandalamProvince under a royal prince or viceroy; about 8 to 9 mandalams in the empire.
Valanadu → Nadu → Kurram / KottamMandalams were divided into valanadus, these into nadus (each with a council of local landowners, the Nattar) and then into groups of villages (kurram).
Ur, Sabha, NagaramUr = general village assembly (all taxpaying landholders); Sabha / Mahasabha = assembly of Brahmin (brahmadeya) villages; Nagaram = assembly of merchants in trading towns.

Village self-government: Uttaramerur

The Uttaramerur inscriptions (Parantaka I, 919 and 921 CE) record how a Sabha was chosen. The village was divided into 30 wards (kudumbu). Qualifications: age 35 to 70; owner of tax-paying land (more than about a quarter veli) and a house on own site; knowledge of the Vedas/mantras; honest. Disqualified: those who had served on a committee in the previous three years, those who had not submitted accounts, those guilty of sins/crimes, and close relatives of a candidate. Method: names of eligible people were written on palm leaves and put in a pot; a young boy drew one ticket per ward (kudavolai = pot-ticket system, a form of lottery). Members served one year (360 days). The members formed committees (variyams): Samvatsara variyam (annual), Eri variyam (tanks), Totta variyam (gardens), Pon variyam (gold), etc. Members were called variyaperumakkal.

4. Politics · Society · Economy · Religion of the Cholas

AreaChola period
PoliticsStrong monarchy with local self-government; vassals (Samanta); marriage alliances (Vengi); an overseas navy; the king worshipped as the earthly form of the temple deity.
SocietyCaste was strong: Brahmins and landowners (Vellalas) on top; the artisan and trader groups formed Valangai (right-hand) and Idangai (left-hand) divisions, who often quarrelled; devadasis attached to temples; slaves existed; Brahmadeya villages were tax-free.
EconomyAgriculture on the Kaveri delta with tanks and canals (Rajendra built the Cholagangam tank at Gangaikonda Cholapuram); guilds: Manigramam, Ayyavole (Ainnurruvar), Nanadesi, Valanjiyar; trade with China, Sri Lanka, South-East Asia; Nagapattinam, Kaveripattinam ports; textiles of Kanchi; temples were the largest landlords, banks and employers.
ReligionShaivism (Nayanmars; Tirumurai compiled by Nambiyandar Nambi in Rajaraja's time; Chola kings were mostly Shaiva) and Vaishnavism (Alvars; Ramanuja, Vishishtadvaita); Buddhism at Nagapattinam (Chudamani Vihara) and Jainism continued; the temple was the centre of religion, learning, art and economy.
Art, architecture, literatureDravida style: tall vimana over the sanctum, later gopuram; Brihadisvara (Thanjavur), Gangaikonda Cholapuram, Airavateshvara (Darasuram), Nageshwara (Kumbakonam): UNESCO 'Great Living Chola Temples'. Chola bronzes by the lost-wax method, above all Nataraja. Literature: Kamban (Ramavataram), Sekkizhar, Jayamkondar, Buddhamitra (Virasoliyam), Tolamozhi Devar (Sulamani).

POLITY · Fundamental Duties; Amendment of the Constitution

Understand it first

What is it? Rights come with responsibilities. Fundamental Duties remind citizens what they owe to the nation. The amendment procedure shows how a living Constitution changes while its core stays safe. It is neither as rigid as the American one nor as flexible as the British one: a mix.

1. Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

  • Source: the USSR Constitution. Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee, during the Emergency; 10 duties at first, the 11th added by the 86th Amendment (2002).
  • They apply to citizens only (not foreigners) and are non-justiciable: no court can enforce them and there is no penalty in the Constitution itself, but Parliament can make laws (for example the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971). The Verma Committee (1999) listed laws that enforce some duties. Courts use them to uphold laws and to read limits on rights.
No.The duty of every citizen
(a)To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals, institutions, the National Flag and National Anthem
(b)To cherish and follow the noble ideals of the freedom struggle
(c)To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India
(d)To defend the country and render national service when called
(e)To promote harmony and brotherhood and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women
(f)To value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture
(g)To protect and improve the natural environment (forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife) and have compassion for living creatures
(h)To develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform
(i)To safeguard public property and to abjure violence
(j)To strive towards excellence in individual and collective activity
(k)Parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to the child aged 6–14 (added by the 86th Amendment 2002)

2. Amendment of the Constitution (Part XX, Article 368)

Three ways to amend
  • Who starts: only Parliament (a Bill in either House; not a State legislature). A Bill needs a special majority in each House separately (a majority of the total membership and two-thirds of those present and voting). There is no joint sitting to break a deadlock. The President must give assent (made compulsory by the 24th Amendment, 1971). A State legislature can only ratify, and only in the third category.
  • Ratification by half of the State legislatures (a simple majority in each) is needed for: election of the President (Arts 54, 55), the executive power of the Union and States, the Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers and the 7th Schedule, States' representation in Parliament, and Art 368 itself. Example: the 101st Amendment (GST, 2016) was ratified by States.

REASONING · Statement and argument; statement and conclusion

Understand it first

Two question types. (1) Statement and argument: a question like 'Should X be done?' is given with arguments (Yes / No); decide which are strong and which weak. (2) Statement and conclusion: from the given statement decide which conclusions definitely follow. In both, use only what is given, not your own opinion or outside facts.

TypeStrong / followsWeak / does not follow
ArgumentImportant to the question, general (applies to many), direct link, based on facts or public benefitPersonal or one case ('my uncle…'), vague, emotional, copying others ('others do it'), unproved, side issue
ConclusionFollows directly and definitely from the statementNeeds outside facts, uses extreme words (all, only, never) not in the statement, or is only a guess
Example

Either-or rule: if neither conclusion follows alone, and the two contradict each other (for example 'all are raw' and 'some are not raw'), then either I or II follows. If one can be true only when the other is false, they form the pair. Example (argument): 'Should plastic bags be banned?' Argument I: 'Yes, they choke drains and harm animals' is strong (important, general, direct). Argument II: 'No, my neighbour still uses them' is weak (personal).

Exam trap

Do not use your own opinion. Words like 'all', 'only', 'never' make a conclusion too strong. A 'Yes' argument is not strong just because it sounds nice: it must be directly about the question. Options: only I, only II, either, neither, both.
