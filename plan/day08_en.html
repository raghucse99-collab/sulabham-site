HISTORY · Delhi Sultanate: administration, society, economy

Understand it first

What is it? From 1206 to 1526 Turkish and Afghan Sultans ruled from Delhi, five dynasties one after another. For the first time a Muslim state governed much of north India, then reached the Deccan. It changed administration (iqta, cash salaries, market control), economy (coins, towns, crafts), society (a new ruling class) and religion (Sufism, Bhakti), and it ended with the Mughals after the First Battle of Panipat (1526). Learn it as Sources → Dynasties and rulers → Administration → Politics · Society · Economy · Religion → Architecture and literature → Decline.

2. Dynasties and rulers

Dynasty / rulerKey facts
Mamluk (Slave) dynasty, 1206–1290Qutbuddin Aibak (1206–10): first Sultan; built Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque (Delhi) and Adhai-din-ka-Jhonpra (Ajmer); began the Qutb Minar; died in a polo accident (Lahore).

The Qutb Minar, Delhi: begun by Aibak and completed by Iltutmish; about 73 m high; a symbol of early Sultanate architecture. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
Example

Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316): conquests. Gujarat (1299) by Ulugh Khan and Nusrat Khan, Ranthambore (1301), Chittor (1303), Malwa (1305); in the south Malik Kafur took Devagiri (1307), Warangal (1310) (the Kakatiya king Prataparudra paid tribute), Dwarasamudra (1311) (Hoysala) and Madurai (1311) (Pandya). He defeated Mongol raids (1299, 1303). Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–51): shifted the capital to Daulatabad (1327), the Doab tax increase, token currency (1329–30) of copper/brass, an agriculture department (Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi), failed Khurasan plan; revolts and the rise of Vijayanagara (1336) and the Bahmani kingdom (1347). Firuz Shah (1351–88): many canals, Firuzabad, towns Hissar, Jaunpur, Diwan-i-Khairat (charity), Diwan-i-Bandagan (slaves), moved Ashokan pillars to Delhi, jizya on Brahmins, made iqtas hereditary.

Example

Andhra link (all regions equal): the Kakatiya capital Warangal was attacked in 1310 (Malik Kafur) and 1318, and fell in 1323 (Ulugh Khan, the future Muhammad bin Tughlaq); the Musunuri Nayaks (Prolaya Nayaka, Kapaya Nayaka) freed Warangal about 1336 and the Vijayanagara empire grew as a Hindu answer in the south.

3. Administration

LevelDetails
SultanHead of state, army, law, justice; absolute in practice; read the Khutba and issued coins in the name of the Caliph (nominal overlord). Succession had no fixed rule: the strongest noble often won.
Central ministries (Diwans)Diwan-i-Wizarat (finance and revenue; head Wazir); Diwan-i-Arz (army; Ariz-i-Mumalik); Diwan-i-Insha (state correspondence); Diwan-i-Risalat (appeals, religion, scholars; Sadr-us-Sudur for religion and charity, Qazi-ul-Quzat for justice); Barid-i-Mumalik (intelligence and post). Special: Diwan-i-Mustakhraj (arrears, Alauddin), Diwan-i-Kohi (agriculture, Muhammad bin Tughlaq), Diwan-i-Khairat, Diwan-i-Bandagan (Firuz).
Provinces and localIqta = a grant of land revenue instead of salary to an officer (muqti / iqtadar), who kept an army and sent the surplus to the Sultan; begun by Iltutmish, reformed by Balban and Alauddin (cash salaries), made hereditary by Firuz. Provinces (iqtas / wilayats) → Shiq (Shiqdar) → Pargana (Amil, Qanungo, Patwari) → Village (Muqaddam, Khut, Chaudhari).
ArmyCentral army (Hashm-i-Qalb) plus iqtadars' contingents; Alauddin created a large standing army paid in cash (234 tankas a year per soldier) with dagh (branding of horses) and huliya (descriptive roll) to stop fraud.
JusticeSharia for Muslims (Qazi, Mufti), customary law for Hindus (village panchayats); Amir-i-Dad to try cases; the Sultan heard appeals.

Revenue and the market reforms of Alauddin

  • Revenue: measurement of land (paimaish) and a tax of half (50%) of the produce (kharaj) in the Doab (royal land, khalisa); he broke the power of khuts and muqaddams (rural chiefs) who had collected the tax; extra taxes ghari (house) and chari (grazing).
  • Market control: to feed a big army on a low salary he fixed prices of grain, cloth, horses, slaves and cattle. Officers: Shahna-i-Mandi (market superintendent), Diwan-i-Riyasat (head of the markets), spies (barids, munhiyans) to report; strict weights and measures; the grain was stored in government granaries; punishment for cheating traders. This held in Delhi; it ended after his death.
A window of the Alai Darwaza (1311) at the Qutb complex: built by Alauddin Khalji, the first building with a true dome and true arch with voussoirs in India. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

4. Politics · Society · Economy · Religion of the Sultanate

AreaDelhi Sultanate
PoliticsMilitary monarchy based on the army and nobles. Theories of kingship: Balban's divine kingship (Zil-i-Ilahi); Alauddin separated religion from politics (state before ulema's advice); Firuz Shah stressed a Muslim state with ulema. A nobility of Turks, later Khaljis, Afghans, Indian Muslims; frequent revolts and murders; the Sultanate faced Mongol threats on the North-West.
SocietyA new ruling class (Turks, Afghans, Persians) over a mostly Hindu population, who were zimmis (protected non-Muslims) paying jizya (Firuz extended it to Brahmins); ulema and Sufis had influence; slavery was common (Firuz kept about 1,80,000 slaves); Indian Muslim converts mixed with local life; women: Razia ruled, but purdah spread in the ruling class and sati and child marriage continued among Hindus; caste remained.
EconomyCash economy grew: silver tanka (about 175 grains) and copper jital; urbanisation: Delhi, Lahore, Multan, Daulatabad, Lakhnauti, Cambay (ports), Jaunpur; crafts: weaving, paper-making, metalwork, the spinning wheel (charkha), the carding bow and the Persian wheel (araghatta) spread; trade: Multani and Gujarati merchants, banjaras (grain carriers); Firuz's canals raised farming; the Doab was the granary; the iqta moved revenue to the capital; Alauddin's price control.
ReligionSufism: Chishti order (Moinuddin Chishti, Ajmer; Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki; Baba Farid; Nizamuddin Auliya; Nasiruddin Chiragh-i-Dehli) and Suhrawardi (Bahauddin Zakariya, Multan). Bhakti: Ramananda, Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, later Chaitanya; they preached equality and one God and used local languages. The Sufi–Bhakti exchange created a mixed culture; the Urdu/Hindavi language grew.
Architecture and literatureIndo-Islamic blend of arch, dome and Persian style with Indian craft: Quwwat-ul-Islam, Qutb Minar (73 m, 5 storeys), Alai Darwaza, Tomb of Iltutmish, Tughlaqabad (sloping walls), Firuz Shah Kotla, Lodi Gardens tombs (octagonal, double dome). Literature in Persian: Barani, Isami, Amir Khusrau (also in Hindavi; tradition credits him with the sitar and tabla).

5. Decline

Causes: a vast empire held by force, no fixed succession, nobles' revolts, Muhammad bin Tughlaq's failed experiments, new kingdoms (Vijayanagara, Bahmani, Bengal, Jaunpur, Gujarat), Timur's invasion (1398) which broke the treasury and army, weak Sayyids and Lodis, and finally Babur's victory at Panipat (1526).

Exam trap

Iltutmish = iqta, tanka, jital, Delhi capital, Caliph 1229; Razia = only woman; Balban = Sijda, Paibos, Zil-i-Ilahi, destroyed the Forty; Alauddin = market control, cash salaries, dagh-huliya, Alai Darwaza, 50% revenue; Muhammad bin Tughlaq = Daulatabad 1327, token currency, Doab tax, Ibn Battuta; Firuz = canals, Firuzabad, jizya on Brahmins, Diwan-i-Khairat, hereditary iqta; Timur 1398; Panipat 1526. Warangal fell in 1323.

POLITY · Basic Structure of the Constitution

Understand it first

What is it? Article 368 lets Parliament amend the Constitution. If this power had no limit, Parliament could, by amendments, remove democracy, rights or even the Constitution itself. The Supreme Court answered: Parliament may amend, but it cannot destroy or change the 'basic structure' (basic features) of the Constitution. The words 'basic structure' are not in the Constitution: it is a judge-made doctrine, drawn out case by case. The idea was first suggested by the German scholar Dietrich Conrad (1965, a lecture at Banaras Hindu University).

1. How the doctrine developed

Case / changeWhat happened
Shankari Prasad (1951) → Sajjan Singh (1965)Parliament's power to amend is wide, including FRs.
Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)11 judges (6:5): Parliament cannot amend FRs.
24th and 25th Amendments (1971)Parliament restored its power to amend FRs; Art 31C added.
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (24 April 1973)The largest bench (13 judges), 7:6, under Chief Justice S.M. Sikri: Golaknath overruled; Parliament can amend any part including FRs but cannot alter the basic structure. (Swami Kesavananda challenged Kerala's land reform laws; Nani Palkhivala argued for him.)
Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)Struck down the 39th Amendment clause that put the Prime Minister's election beyond court review: free and fair elections and rule of law are basic structure.
42nd Amendment (1976)Tried to remove all limits on amending power and court review.
Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)Struck down that clause: limited amending power, judicial review and the balance between FRs and DPSPs are basic structure.
Waman Rao (1981)Only amendments after 24 April 1973 are tested for basic structure.
S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)Secularism and federalism are basic structure; President's Rule (Art 356) can be reviewed by courts.
L. Chandra Kumar (1997)Judicial review by High Courts and Supreme Court (Arts 226, 32) cannot be taken away, even for tribunals.
I.R. Coelho (2007)Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 1973 can be reviewed on the basis of basic structure and fundamental rights.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)Struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act (a commission to appoint judges) as against the independence of the judiciary; the collegium system continued.

2. What counts as basic structure

Features recognised by courts

There is no fixed list; the list is open and grows case by case. In Kesavananda the judges named: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form, secular character, separation of powers, federal character, and individual freedom and the Preamble's aims; later cases added judicial review, independence of the judiciary, free and fair elections, rule of law, welfare state, equality, harmony of FRs and DPSPs and limited power to amend.

Example

Three points to remember: (1) It is not written in the Constitution; courts find it case by case. (2) It is used mainly to test constitutional amendments (an ordinary law is tested against Part III and the division of powers). (3) It protects the identity of the Constitution, but it does not freeze the Constitution: Parliament can still amend the parts that are not basic.

Exam trap

Kesavananda Bharati (1973): 13 judges, 7:6, basic structure; Golaknath overruled. Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): free and fair elections. Minerva Mills (1980): limited amending power, FR–DPSP balance. Bommai (1994): secularism, federalism. Coelho (2007): Ninth Schedule. NJAC case (2015): independence of judiciary. Source of the idea: Germany (Dietrich Conrad). Not defined in the Constitution.

REASONING · Courses of action; deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning

Understand it first

What is it? Part A: a statement describes a problem, and courses of action are steps to deal with it. You decide which course should be followed. Part B: three ways of reasoning, which APPSC reasoning questions test through examples.

Part A · Courses of action

A good course of action is…A poor one is…
Practical and possible for the authorityImpossible or too costly
Directly related to the problemUnrelated or only for show
Balanced and fair; a step to improve or investigateExtreme or harsh (ban everything, punish all, close down)
A new step, not already in the statementA comment or only a delay ('wait and see')
Example

Example: Statement: 'Many people complain that the Gram Sachivalayam office is closed during working hours.' Course I: 'Officials should explain their absence and attendance should be monitored.' It is direct, practical and fair, so it follows. Course II: 'The office should be closed permanently.' It is extreme, so it does not follow.

Part B · Deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning

Three kinds of reasoning
KindDirectionStrength of conclusionClue words / example
DeductiveGeneral rule → specific caseCertain if the premises are true (valid argument)'All A are B; x is A; so x is B'. All birds have feathers; a sparrow is a bird; so it has feathers.
InductiveSpecific cases → general ruleProbable, never certain; more cases make it stronger'Every crow I have seen is black, so all crows are black.' (generalisation, pattern, sample)
AbductiveObservation → best explanationA likely guess, to be tested later (Charles Peirce)'The road is wet, so it probably rained.' (doctor's diagnosis, detective's guess)
Exam trap

Deductive: top-down, certain. Inductive: bottom-up, probable. Abductive: best explanation, a guess. A valid deductive argument can still have a false conclusion if a premise is false; a sound one has valid logic and true premises. For courses of action: practical, direct, not extreme, not just a comment.
