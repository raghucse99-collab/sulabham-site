HISTORY · Medieval art, architecture, language and literature

Understand it first

What is it? Rulers, nobles and merchants of the medieval age (c. 1200–1750) paid for buildings, paintings, music and books. This patronage shows us what each age valued. Medieval culture is a meeting of traditions: Turko-Persian ideas met Indian ones and created a new Indo-Islamic style, while in the south the Vijayanagara kings carried on and grew the Dravidian temple style. Learn it as Architecture → Painting → Music and dance → Language and literature.

Medieval architecture
The Qutb Minar, Delhi (begun by Qutbuddin Aibak, finished by Iltutmish; about 72.5 m, five storeys). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. What is Indo-Islamic architecture?

FeatureMeaning
Arch, dome, vault (arcuate)Weight is carried by curved arches; the older Indian style was trabeate (beam and pillar). Voussoirs are the wedge stones of an arch.
MinaretA tall tower beside a mosque from which the call to prayer (azan) is given.
Lime mortarMortar of lime and sand made large domes and arches strong.
Calligraphy, arabesque, geometryWalls carry Quranic verses and floral and geometric patterns instead of images of living beings. Jali = pierced stone screen; later pietra dura = inlay of coloured stones.
Indian contributionIndian craftsmen, lotus, bell and chain motifs, brackets and chhatris joined the foreign forms.

4. Vijayanagara and temple architecture

The stone chariot at the Vittala Temple, Hampi (Vijayanagara, 16th century). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Vijayanagara style (capital Hampi, 1336–1565): tall gopurams (gateway towers), kalyana mandapas (wedding halls), hundred-pillared halls, carved musical pillars, the stone chariot. Main sites: Virupaksha Temple, Vittala Temple, Hazara Rama Temple, Lotus Mahal, Elephant stables, Krishna temple (built by Krishnadevaraya). Domingo Paes and Nuniz praised the city.
  • Andhra region: Lepakshi (Veerabhadra temple, 16th c., built under Virupanna and Viranna; giant Nandi, hanging pillar, murals), Tadipatri (Chintalarayaswami and Bugga Ramalingeswara temples), Srikalahasti (gopuram added by Krishnadevaraya), Tirumala (gopuram and mandapas), Ahobilam, Srisailam, Kalyanadurgam and Penukonda: spread across Rayalaseema, Coastal Andhra and Telangana.
  • Other medieval styles: Chola (Brihadisvara temple, Thanjavur, Rajaraja I, c. 1010); Hoysala (Belur, Halebidu: star-shaped base, soapstone carving); Rajput (Vijay Stambh, Chittor, Rana Kumbha; forts of Mehrangarh, Amber, Kumbhalgarh); Sikh (Harmandir Sahib, Amritsar).

Mughal architecture was covered on Day 9 (Humayun's Tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, Taj Mahal, Red Fort); remember its steps: Humayun's Tomb → Fatehpur Sikri → Itimad-ud-Daula → Taj Mahal.

6. Music and dance

  • Amir Khusrau (Delhi, Alauddin Khalji's time): poet and musician, traditionally credited with mixing Persian and Indian music, qawwali, khayal, and the sitar and tabla; he wrote in Hindavi. Sarangadeva's *Sangita Ratnakara* (13th c.) is a key text.
  • Hindustani: Tansen (Akbar's court, Dhrupad, ragas like Miyan ki Malhar), Man Singh Tomar of Gwalior (patron of Dhrupad), Baiju Bawra. Karnatak: Purandaradasa (15th–16th c., 'father of Karnatak music'), Annamacharya and the Vijayanagara court; later the Trinity (Tyagaraja, Shyama Shastri, Muthuswami Dikshitar) in the 18th–19th c.
  • Dance: Kathak (north, temple storytelling that moved to Mughal courts), Kuchipudi (Andhra, developed by Siddhendra Yogi, 15th c., Kuchipudi village in Krishna district), Bhagavata Mela (Tamil Nadu), Yakshagana (Karnataka). Bhakti and Sufi devotional music (kirtan, qawwali, abhangs) spread.

7. Language and literature

Persian became the language of the court and administration; Arabic for religion and learning; Sanskrit continued for scholarship; and regional languages grew through Bhakti poets who spoke to the people. Urdu (camp language of Persian, Arabic, Turkish and Hindi words) and Hindavi/Hindi developed. Translations (Mahabharata as Razmnama, Ramayana, Atharva Veda, Panchatantra as Anwar-i-Suhaili) were made under Akbar.

POLITY · Parliamentary executive: President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers

Understand it first

What is it? In a parliamentary system (the Westminster model) the head of State (President) is a nominal head, and the real power lies with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, who are chosen from the legislature and must keep its majority support. In a presidential system (USA) the President is both head of State and real executive and is not answerable to the legislature in the same way. India chose the parliamentary system for responsibility and for harmony between legislature and executive.

The Union executive
Rashtrapati Bhavan, New Delhi: the official residence of the President of India. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. The President

PointDetails
PositionArt 52: there shall be a President; Art 53: executive power of the Union is vested in the President, who uses it directly or through officers under the Constitution.
Qualifications (Art 58)Citizen of India, 35 years old, qualified to be a Lok Sabha member, holds no office of profit. Nomination needs 50 proposers and 50 seconders.
Election (Art 54–55)By an electoral college: elected members of both Houses of Parliament and elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies (including Delhi and Puducherry). Nominated members and members of Legislative Councils do not vote. Method: proportional representation by single transferable vote, secret ballot. Votes are weighted so that States and the Union carry equal weight.
Term (Art 56)5 years, may be re-elected; resigns to the Vice-President; continues until the successor takes office. A vacancy must be filled by election within 6 months; the Vice-President acts meanwhile (Art 65).
Impeachment (Art 61)Only for violation of the Constitution; either House can start; 14 days' notice signed by at least 1/4 of its members; passed by 2/3 of the total membership; the other House investigates, and also passes by 2/3. Nominated members can vote in this process.

Powers of the President

TypePowers
ExecutiveAppoints the PM, ministers, Governors, judges, Attorney General, CAG, Chief Election Commissioner; Supreme Commander of the armed forces.
LegislativeSummons and prorogues Parliament, dissolves the Lok Sabha, addresses it, nominates 12 to the Rajya Sabha and 2 Anglo-Indians earlier; ordinances (Art 123, when Parliament is not in session); gives assent to Bills.
FinancialMoney Bills need his prior recommendation; places the Budget before Parliament; controls the Contingency Fund; appoints the Finance Commission every 5 years.
JudicialPardon (Art 72: pardon, reprieve, respite, remission, commutation, including death sentences); seeks the Supreme Court's advice (Art 143).
EmergencyNational Emergency (Art 352), President's Rule (Art 356), Financial Emergency (Art 360).
Veto (Art 111)Absolute (withhold assent; only on private members' and government Bills after a Cabinet resigns), suspensive (return a Bill once, Parliament may pass it again), pocket (take no decision, no time limit). No veto on Money Bills returned, and a Constitution amendment Bill must be signed.

2. The Vice-President (Art 63–71)

Elected by an electoral college of all members of both Houses of Parliament (including nominated members, but not State legislators) by single transferable vote; 35 years; 5-year term; ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (no vote except to break a tie); acts as President during a vacancy (up to 6 months).

3. The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers

ProvisionDetails
Art 74A Council of Ministers headed by the PM shall aid and advise the President, who shall act on that advice (42nd Amendment, 1976); the 44th Amendment (1978) lets the President ask the Council to reconsider once, but he must act on the advice given after reconsideration. Courts cannot ask what advice was given.
Art 75(1)The PM is appointed by the President; other ministers are appointed on the PM's advice. By convention the President appoints the leader of the majority party or coalition in the Lok Sabha. The PM may be a member of either House.
Art 75(1A), (1B)91st Amendment (2003): the Council cannot exceed 15% of the Lok Sabha's strength (in a State, 15% of the Assembly, minimum 12); a member disqualified for defection cannot be a minister.
Art 75(2)Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President (individual responsibility).
Art 75(3)The Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha: if it loses confidence, all must resign. A minister who disagrees must resign or support.
Art 75(5)A minister who is not a member of Parliament must become one within 6 months, or he ceases to be a minister.
Art 77, 78Business is conducted in the President's name; the PM must inform the President of all decisions on administration and legislation and give any information he asks for (Art 78).

  • The Prime Minister: 'keystone of the Cabinet arch' (Nehru's words for the office), 'first among equals'. If the PM resigns or dies, the whole Council goes.
  • Important cases: Ram Jawaya Kapur (1955): the President is a constitutional head and real power lies with the Council; Shamsher Singh (1974): the President and Governors act on ministers' advice except in a few special cases; U.N. MATHS · Averages

Understand it first

What is it? An average (arithmetic mean) is the one number that could replace every item and leave the total unchanged. If 5 students score 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, the total is 300; if each had scored the average, 60, the total would still be 300. So: Average = Sum ÷ Number of items, and therefore Sum = Average × Number. Nearly every averages problem is just moving between sum and average.

Key formulas of averages

1. Shortcuts and properties

SituationRuleExample
First n natural numbersAverage = (n + 1) ÷ 2; sum = n(n+1)/2First 20: average 10.5
First n even / odd numbersEven: average n + 1; odd: average nFirst 10 odd numbers: 10
Any AP / consecutive numbersAverage = (first + last) ÷ 2 = the middle term7, 14, … 98: (7 + 98) ÷ 2 = 52.5
Add 1 to every itemAverage rises by the same amount; multiply every item and the average is multiplied tooAverage 20; each +5: new 25
Add a new item (n items, average A, the new average A′)New item = A′ × (n + 1) − A × nClass of 30, average 14; with teacher average 15: teacher = 15×31 − 14×30 = 45
Remove an itemRemoved item = old sum − new sum5 numbers, average 27; after removal average 25: 135 − 100 = 35
Replace an itemChange in average × n = new item − old item
Combined (weighted) average(n₁a₁ + n₂a₂) ÷ (n₁ + n₂)40 students at 60, 60 students at 70: (2,400 + 4,200) ÷ 100 = 66
Average speedTotal distance ÷ total time. For equal distances at a and b: 2ab ÷ (a + b), not (a + b) ÷ 2. For equal times: (a + b) ÷ 2.40 km/h and 60 km/h over equal distances: 2×40×60 ÷ 100 = 48 km/h
Example

Why not 50? A car goes 120 km at 40 km/h (3 hours) and 120 km at 60 km/h (2 hours). Total 240 km in 5 hours = 48 km/h, not 50. The car spends more time at the slower speed, so the average sits nearer the slower one.

2. Mean, median and mode

  • Mean = sum ÷ number. Median = the middle value when the data are arranged in order (for an even number of items, the average of the two middle values). Mode = the value that occurs most often.
  • For a moderately skewed distribution: Mode = 3 × Median − 2 × Mean. Example: Mean 20, Median 18 gives Mode = 54 − 40 = 14.
  • Weighted average (marks with different weights, or a mixture): multiply each value by its weight, add, and divide by the total weight. Deviation method: pick a guess average, add the differences from it, divide by n and add back, which saves effort when numbers are close together, e.g. 48, 52, 50, 49, 51 (guess 50: differences −2, +2, 0, −1, +1 sum 0, so the average is 50).
Exam trap

Sum = Average × n; average of 1 to n = (n + 1)/2; of an AP = (first + last)/2; new item = A′(n + 1) − An; equal distances: 2ab/(a + b); Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean; combined average = weighted by the number of items.
