HISTORY · Shivaji and the rise of the Maratha Empire

Understand it first

What is it? In the 17th century, while the Mughal Empire was at its widest, a new regional power rose in the hills and forts of the Western Ghats (Maharashtra). Shivaji turned a small jagir into an independent Swaraj (self-rule) with a disciplined army, a navy, a sound revenue system and a council of ministers. After him the Peshwas made the Marathas the strongest power in India until 1761, and the British finally ended Maratha rule in 1818. Learn it as Background → Shivaji's rise → Administration → Politics · Society · Economy · Religion → Successors and Peshwas → Fall.

Rise and fall of the Marathas
The Maha Darwaja (main gate) of Raigad Fort, Shivaji's capital where he was crowned in 1674. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Background and early life

  • Land and people: the Deccan plateau and Western Ghats gave hill forts, narrow passes and the hardy Mavala hill soldiers. The Bhakti saints (Jnaneshwar, Eknath, Tukaram, Ramdas) built a Marathi pride and unity across castes. The Deccan Sultanates of Ahmednagar and Bijapur employed Maratha chiefs, so the Marathas learnt warfare and administration; Malik Ambar (Ahmednagar) taught guerrilla war and a land-revenue system that Shivaji later improved.
  • Shivaji was born at Shivneri fort (near Junnar) in 1630 (traditional date; some sources give 1627), son of Shahaji Bhonsle (a Bijapur noble with a jagir at Pune) and Jijabai. His guardian Dadoji Konddev trained him in administration and war; Ramdas and Tukaram shaped his ideals.

3. Administration

Ashta Pradhan
  • Ashta Pradhan (council of eight ministers): Peshwa (prime minister), Amatya (finance), Mantri (royal records), Sachiv (correspondence), Sumant (foreign affairs), Senapati (army), Pandit Rao (religion and charity) and Nyayadhish (justice). They were advisers, not hereditary; all but the Pandit Rao and Nyayadhish led armies. The king held the final power.
  • Territory: Swaraj (his own core, directly governed: Pune, Konkan), Mughlai (neighbouring lands paying Chauth and Sardeshmukhi) and the Karnataka conquests.
  • Revenue: land was measured (rod, kathi) and classed by fertility (survey under Annaji Datto); the State took about one-third, later two-fifths of the produce, collected directly from the cultivators, so the deshmukhs and jagirdar middlemen lost power; chauth (one-fourth of the revenue of neighbouring lands, as a payment for protection from his raids) and sardeshmukhi (an extra 10%, as hereditary head of the Deshmukhs). Coins: hon (gold), shivrai (copper).
  • Army: a loyal, paid standing army; infantry (Mavalas), cavalry of bargirs (horses and arms supplied by the State) and shiledars (own horses), strict discipline, no women or plunder from the poor; a navy with bases at Kolaba and Sindhudurg; about 300 hill forts, each under three officers of different communities (havaldar, sabnis, sarnobat) who checked one another; a good spy system (Bahirji Naik).

4. Politics · Society · Economy · Religion

AreaMarathas
PoliticsSwaraj under a Chhatrapati with an Ashta Pradhan; guerrilla (ganimi kava) war; forts and mobility; later the Peshwas (Chitpavan Brahmin ministers) held real power at Pune while the Chhatrapati at Satara became a figurehead; a confederacy of Peshwa, Scindia (Gwalior), Holkar (Indore), Gaekwad (Baroda) and Bhonsle (Nagpur).
SocietyStrong peasant (Kunbi/Maratha) and Mavala base; deshmukhs and watandars controlled by the State; Brahmin administrators; shared Marathi culture and Bhakti (Varkari); Shivaji ordered honour for women and no harm to mosques and the Quran.
EconomyRaiyatwari-like direct collection; chauth and sardeshmukhi from neighbours; plunder of Surat; ports and a navy for trade and defence; forts as storehouses; the Peshwas' bankers and hundis later made Pune a financial centre.
Religion and languageShivaji was a devout Hindu (Bhavani, Tukaram, Ramdas) but tolerant; he made Marathi and Sanskrit the court languages and had Raghunath Pandit prepare the Rajyavyavahar Kosh to replace Persian words; he supported temples and gave grants to mosques and dargahs too.

5. After Shivaji: successors and Peshwas

RulerKey facts
Sambhaji (1680–89)Fought the Mughals; captured and executed by Aurangzeb (1689); his son Shahu was held by the Mughals.

Baji Rao I (1720–40)Greatest Peshwa, never lost a battle; defeated the Nizam at Palkhed (1728); raided Delhi (1737); Bhopal (1738) against the Nizam; took Bassein (1739) from the Portuguese; built Shaniwar Wada at Pune; spread Maratha power into Malwa, Gujarat and Bundelkhand.
Balaji Baji Rao (Nana Saheb, 1740–61)Maratha power reached the Punjab and Attock; the Peshwa became the real head after Shahu's death (1749); lost the Third Battle of Panipat (14 January 1761) to Ahmad Shah Abdali: Sadashivrao Bhau and Vishwasrao died.
Madhavrao I (1761–72), Mahadji Scindia, Nana PhadnavisMadhavrao recovered Maratha power after Panipat; Mahadji Scindia restored the Marathas at Delhi (1771, took Shah Alam II under protection); Nana Phadnavis was the able minister.

6. Fall: the Anglo-Maratha Wars

WarFacts
First (1775–82)Began when the British backed Raghunath Rao (Raghoba) against the Peshwa's ministers; Treaty of Salbai (1782) (mediated by Mahadji Scindia) restored the position and gave 20 years of peace.
Second (1803–05)Baji Rao II signed the Treaty of Bassein (1802) accepting Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance; Scindia and Bhonsle resisted and lost (Assaye, Argaon); treaties of Deogaon and Surji-Anjangaon (1803), Rajghat (Holkar, 1805).
Third (1817–19)Peshwa Baji Rao II rose against the British, lost at Khadki and Koregaon (Bhima Koregaon, 1 Jan 1818), surrendered in 1818, was pensioned at Bithur; the Peshwaship was abolished and the Maratha power ended.

Why they fell: loss at Panipat (1761), rivalry among the confederacy chiefs, the Peshwas' neglect of navy and modern artillery, and British diplomacy that split the chiefs.

Exam trap

Shivaji: Torna 1646, Javli 1656, Afzal Khan (Pratapgad) 1659, Shaista Khan 1663, Surat 1664 & 1670, Purandar 1665 (23 forts), Agra 1666, coronation 1674, died 1680; Ashta Pradhan (8); Chauth = 1/4, Sardeshmukhi = 10%; 3 officers per fort; Sambhaji executed 1689; Shahu 1707; Balaji Vishwanath 1719; Baji Rao I: Palkhed 1728; Panipat III 1761; Salbai 1782; Bassein 1802; end 1818.

POLITY · State executive and legislature: Governor, Chief Minister, Legislature

Understand it first

What is it? Every State copies the Union's model: a Governor (nominal head, like the President), a Chief Minister and Council of Ministers (real power, like the PM and Council) and a State Legislature (like Parliament). The Constitution's Part VI (Articles 152–237) deals with the States. Learn it as Governor → Chief Minister and Council → Legislature → President's Rule.

The State executive and legislature

1. The Governor

PointDetails
Appointment (Art 153–156)Each State has a Governor (one person can serve two or more States, 7th Amendment 1956); appointed by the President by warrant; holds office during the President's pleasure; normal term 5 years.
Qualifications (Art 157–158)Citizen, 35 years, not a member of Parliament or a State Legislature (if so, he vacates the seat), holds no office of profit. Oath is taken before the Chief Justice of the High Court (Art 159).
Executive powersAppoints the Chief Minister and, on his advice, the other ministers; the Advocate General (Art 165), the State Public Service Commission chairman and members; he is the Chancellor of State universities.
Legislative powersSummons and prorogues the House, dissolves the Assembly, addresses it, nominates 1/6 of the Legislative Council members; assent to Bills (Art 200): assent, withhold, return (not Money Bills) or reserve for the President (Art 201); ordinances (Art 213) when the House is not in session.
Ordinance (Art 213)Has the force of law; must be placed before the Assembly when it meets and expires six weeks after reassembly (or earlier if disapproved); Governor needs the President's instructions in some cases.
Financial and judicialMoney Bills need his prior recommendation; places the State Budget and controls the Contingency Fund of the State; Art 161: may grant pardon, reprieve, remission, or suspend or commute a sentence, but cannot pardon a death sentence (the President can) nor a court-martial sentence.
Discretion (Art 163)Normally acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, but has discretion in: reserving a Bill for the President, inviting a leader to form a Ministry when no party has a majority, dismissal of a ministry that has lost the House, and reporting to the President under Art 356. Bommai (1994) said the proof of majority must be on the floor of the House.

2. The Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers

  • Art 164: the CM is appointed by the Governor, who invites the leader of the majority in the Assembly; other ministers are appointed on the CM's advice; they hold office during the Governor's pleasure and are collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. A minister who is not a member must become one within 6 months.
  • 91st Amendment (2003): the Council cannot exceed 15% of the Assembly's strength and cannot have fewer than 12 ministers (including the CM). Example: Andhra Pradesh has 175 members, so the maximum is 15% of 175 = 26.25, that is 26.
  • Art 167: the CM must communicate to the Governor all decisions of the Council on administration and legislation and give any information he asks for. The CM is the link between the Governor and the Council, heads the Council, and chairs the Cabinet; the Chief Secretary heads the permanent civil service in the State.

4. President's Rule in a State

  • Art 355: the Union must protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance. Art 356: if the President is satisfied, on the Governor's report or otherwise, that the government of the State cannot be carried on according to the Constitution (failure of constitutional machinery), he can take over the State's executive; the Assembly is suspended or dissolved; Parliament makes laws for the State.
  • Approval: by both Houses within 2 months (simple majority); lasts 6 months, extendable by 6 months each time up to a maximum of 3 years; beyond 1 year only if a National Emergency is in force and the Election Commission certifies that elections cannot be held (44th Amendment). S.R. Bommai (1994): the proclamation is subject to judicial review; the Assembly cannot be dissolved before Parliament approves it; secularism is part of the basic structure.

MATHS · Percentage

Understand it first

What is it? Percent means 'per hundred' (Latin *per centum*). x% is simply x/100. A percentage lets us compare quantities on a common scale of 100: 18 out of 25 and 36 out of 50 look different, but both are 72%. Percentages are used in marks, profit, discount, interest, population, elections and budgets, so this topic supports many later topics.

Example

Why a rise and an equal fall do not cancel: take ₹100, add 20% = ₹120; now take 20% of ₹120 (₹24) = ₹96. The fall is on a bigger base, so the result is 4% lower. Always ask: percent of what? The base (the original) is the number after 'of' or 'than'.

Exam trap

x% = x/100; change ÷ original × 100; successive: x + y + xy/100; rise and equal fall: −x²/100 %; reverse: 100x/(100 + x); area: 2x + x²/100; growth: P(1 + r/100)ⁿ; 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/6 = 16.67%, 1/7 = 14.29%, 1/9 = 11.11%.
