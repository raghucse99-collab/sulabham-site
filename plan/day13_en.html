HISTORY · Advent of Europeans and the rise of British power

Understand it first

What is it? From 1498 European trading companies came to India by sea, first to buy spices and cotton cloth. Over about 260 years one of them, the English East India Company, defeated its European rivals and Indian rulers and turned from a trader into a ruler. The reasons are why they came, how they competed, and why the British won. Learn it as Why Europe came → Portuguese → Dutch → English → Danes and French → Carnatic Wars → Bengal (Plassey, Buxar) → Why the British won.

European companies in India

1. Why did Europe come by sea?

  • Spices and cloth (pepper, cloves, cinnamon, Indian cotton) were very costly in Europe, and the old trade went through Arab and Venetian middlemen. The Ottoman capture of Constantinople (1453) made the land route harder and dearer.
  • Renaissance, science and new ships (compass, astrolabe, the caravel, maps) and royal backing (Prince Henry the Navigator of Portugal) made long voyages possible. Mercantilism (the idea that a country grows rich by selling more than it buys, and by colonies) pushed governments to back trade companies.

5. The Danes and the French

  • Danes: company founded 1616; Tranquebar (Tharangambadi), 1620; Serampore (1755); both sold to the British in 1845; known more for missionary work (Serampore Mission).
  • French: Compagnie des Indes Orientales (1664), founded under Colbert (Louis XIV's minister) as a State-run company; Surat (1668), Masulipatnam (1669); Pondicherry (1673) founded by Francois Martin; Chandernagore (1690); Mahe, Karaikal and Yanam (Andhra, 1723). Dupleix (governor 1742–54) used Indian soldiers trained in European style (sepoys) and interfered in Indian successions. French territories joined India in 1954 (de facto) and 1962 (de jure).

Why the French lost: weaker navy; State control and little support from the French government; Dupleix's recall; the English held Bengal's wealth after 1757; poorer leadership against Clive and Eyre Coote; the Seven Years' War in Europe went against France.

7. Bengal: Plassey and Buxar

From trader to ruler
EventFacts
BackgroundAlivardi Khan died (1756); his grandson Siraj-ud-Daulah became Nawab. He was angry that the English fortified Calcutta without permission, misused dastaks and sheltered a runaway (Krishna Das). He took Fort William (June 1756); the Black Hole incident (20 June 1756; Holwell's account of 146 prisoners and 123 deaths is disputed).
Plassey (23 June 1757)Clive and Admiral Watson retook Calcutta (January 1757), then plotted with Mir Jafar (the Nawab's commander), Rai Durlabh, banker Jagat Seth and Omichand. At Plassey, Mir Jafar's troops stood aside; the Nawab was beaten in a few hours and later killed; Mir Jafar became Nawab and paid huge sums; the Company got 24 Parganas. Mir Qasim (1760–63)Made Nawab for ceding Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong; moved to Munger, built a modern army and abolished internal duties (which hurt Company traders); clash with the Company.
Buxar (22 October 1764)Hector Munro defeated the combined armies of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh and the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. More decisive than Plassey: it was a fair fight won by arms, making the Company the leading power in north India.
Treaty of Allahabad (1765)Clive got from Shah Alam II the Diwani (right to collect revenue) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa for ₹26 lakh a year to the emperor; Shuja paid ₹50 lakh; Kora and Allahabad went to the emperor. Dual government (1765–72): the Company had revenue (diwani) and the Nawab had administration (nizamat) but no power to run it: 'power without responsibility' for the Company, ended by Warren Hastings (1772).

Ceded districts (Kadapa, Kurnool, Anantapur, Bellary): given to the Company by the Nizam in 1800 under the Subsidiary Alliance. Hyderabad (Telangana) stayed a princely State under the Nizam until 1948.

POLITY · Judiciary: Supreme Court, High Courts; judicial review and judicial activism

Understand it first

What is it? The judiciary is the organ that settles disputes, protects the rights of citizens and tests whether laws and government actions follow the Constitution. India has a single, integrated system with the Supreme Court at the top. Its two powerful tools are judicial review (striking down laws that break the Constitution) and, in recent decades, judicial activism (the courts actively filling gaps to protect rights).

The integrated judiciary

1. The Supreme Court

PointDetails
Composition (Art 124)Chief Justice of India and not more than 33 other judges (34 in all, raised by the 2019 Act); seat at Delhi (Art 130); ad hoc judges (Art 127) and retired judges (Art 128) can be called.
QualificationsCitizen; 5 years as a High Court judge, or 10 years as a High Court advocate, or a distinguished jurist in the President's opinion.
AppointmentBy the President after consulting judges: the collegium system (the CJI and four senior-most judges). First Judges case (S.P. Gupta, 1981): executive's view prevails; Second (1993): CJI's view (with collegium) is binding; Third (1998): collegium of five. The 99th Amendment and NJAC (2014) were struck down in 2015 (Fourth Judges case) as against the basic structure and judicial independence.
Tenure and removalRetire at 65. Removed by the President after an address by each House (special majority: majority of total membership and 2/3 of members present and voting) for proved misbehaviour or incapacity (Art 124(4); Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968). No judge has been removed: V. Ramaswami (1993) motion failed in the Lok Sabha.
IndependenceSalaries charged on the Consolidated Fund (not voted); no discussion of conduct in Parliament (Art 121); contempt power (Art 129); no practice after retirement before any court; separation from the executive (Art 50).

Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

TypeDetails
Original (Art 131)Exclusive: disputes between Centre and States, or between States; (not for disputes under pre-Constitution treaties).
Writ (Art 32)Right to Constitutional Remedies: habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari, quo warranto to enforce Fundamental Rights; Dr Ambedkar called it the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution.
Appellate (Art 132–136)Constitutional, civil and criminal appeals from High Courts; Art 136: Special Leave Petition (SLP) to appeal from any court or tribunal (except armed forces).

Chandra Kumar, 1997: tribunals' decisions can be tested in High Courts).

3. Judicial review

  • Meaning: the power of the courts to examine whether a law or executive action is consistent with the Constitution, and to declare it void if it is not. Basis: the written, supreme Constitution and federalism; Art 13 (laws against Fundamental Rights are void), Arts 32 and 226, 131–136. (In the USA it began with Marbury v Madison, 1803.)
  • Landmark cases: A.K. Gopalan (1950): narrow reading of Art 21; Golaknath (1967): Parliament cannot amend Fundamental Rights; Kesavananda Bharati (1973): Parliament may amend but not the basic structure; Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975); Minerva Mills (1980): judicial review and the balance of Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are basic structure; L. Chandra Kumar (1997): judicial review is basic structure; I.R. Coelho (2007): laws in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be reviewed.

4. Judicial activism and PIL

  • Judicial activism: the courts go beyond deciding disputes and take a proactive role to protect rights and fill gaps left by the legislature and executive. Its tool is the Public Interest Litigation (PIL): any public-spirited person can approach the court for those who cannot, relaxing the rule of locus standi (who may sue); even a letter or postcard can be treated as a petition (epistolary jurisdiction). It began in the late 1970s under Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer.
  • Key cases: Hussainara Khatoon (1979): rights of undertrial prisoners, speedy trial; Maneka Gandhi (1978): Art 21's procedure must be fair, just and reasonable; Olga Tellis (1985): right to livelihood; M.C. Mehta: environment, polluter pays; Vishaka (1997): guidelines against sexual harassment at work; NALSA (2014): recognised transgender persons; Puttaswamy (2017): right to privacy is a Fundamental Right; Shayara Bano (2017): set aside instant triple talaq.
  • Criticism: judicial overreach (courts entering the domain of the legislature and executive, against the separation of powers), slow justice, a huge backlog of cases, and misuse of PIL for 'publicity interest'. The remedy is judicial restraint: courts must respect their limits, and the Constitution is the final guide.
Exam trap

MATHS · Simple and compound interest

Understand it first

What is it? Interest is the price of using someone else's money. The money lent is the principal (P), the yearly percentage charged is the rate (R), the period is the time (T) and the total repaid is the amount (A = P + Interest). In simple interest the interest is calculated only on the original principal, so it grows in a straight line. In compound interest the interest of each period is added to the principal, so you pay interest on interest, and it grows faster and faster, like a snowball.

Simple and compound interest

1. Simple interest

  • SI = P × R × T / 100, and A = P + SI. Also P = (SI × 100) / (R × T), R = (SI × 100) / (P × T) and T = (SI × 100) / (P × R). T must be in years: 6 months = 1/2 year, 9 months = 3/4 year, 73 days = 73/365 = 0.2 year.
  • Example: ₹8,000 at 7.5% for 4 years: SI = 8,000 × 7.5 × 4 / 100 = ₹2,400; A = ₹10,400.
  • Doubling and trebling: at SI a sum becomes n times in T years at a rate R = 100(n − 1)/T. If it doubles in 8 years, R = 12.5%, and it will become 4 times in 24 years (SI = 3P needs 3 × 8 years).
  • Two parts of a sum lent at different rates: if the total interest is known, use allegation (weighted rates). Borrowing at one rate and lending at another: gain = P × (difference of rates) × T / 100.

2. Compound interest

CompoundingAmount ANote
YearlyA = P (1 + R/100)ᵀCI = A − P
Half-yearlyA = P (1 + R/200)²ᵀRate halves, periods double
QuarterlyA = P (1 + R/400)⁴ᵀRate ÷ 4, periods × 4
Different rates R₁, R₂, R₃ in successive yearsA = P (1 + R₁/100)(1 + R₂/100)(1 + R₃/100)
Fractional years, e.g. 2½ yearsA = P (1 + R/100)² × (1 + (R/2)/100)Compound for the whole years, then simple interest for the part year
Example

CI − SI: for 2 years, the difference is P × (R/100)²; for 3 years, P × (R/100)² × (3 + R/100). Example: ₹5,000 at 8% for 2 years: difference = 5,000 × 0.08² = ₹32. CI example: ₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years: A = 10,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = ₹12,100, so CI = ₹2,100 (while SI would be ₹2,000: the extra ₹100 is the interest on the first year's interest). Half-yearly: ₹16,000 at 20% for 1 year: rate 10% per half-year, 2 periods: A = 16,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = ₹19,360. Rule of 72: at compound interest an amount doubles in about 72 ÷ R years.

Exam trap

SI = PRT/100; A = P + SI; CI: A = P(1 + R/100)ᵀ; half-yearly: R/2, 2T; CI − SI (2 yrs) = P(R/100)²; (3 yrs) = P(R/100)²(3 + R/100); SI doubling: T = 100/R; CI doubling ≈ 72/R; CI is always more than SI for T > 1.
