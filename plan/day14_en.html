HISTORY · Consolidation of British rule: policies and administration

Understand it first

What is it? After Plassey and Buxar the Company was a ruler, but a company of traders could not run a country. Between 1773 and 1858 the British built a system of control: laws from Parliament (to control the Company), Governors-General (to win and organise territory), land revenue systems (to collect money), and four pillars of administration: the civil service, army, police and judiciary. Learn it as Acts → Governors-General → Land revenue → Four pillars → Andhra link → Impact.

Acts of Parliament

1. Acts that controlled the Company

ActMain points
Regulating Act, 1773First step by Parliament, after the Company's misrule and bankruptcy. Governor of Bengal became Governor-General (Warren Hastings first) with a council of four; Bombay and Madras came under Bengal; Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774), Chief Justice Elijah Impey; servants banned from private trade and gifts.
Pitt's India Act, 1784Board of Control (6 commissioners) for political affairs while the Court of Directors kept commerce and patronage: dual control. Governor-General's council cut to three. Company's lands called 'British possessions in India'.
Charter Act, 1793Company's trade monopoly renewed for 20 years; Governor-General got power over the other presidencies.
Charter Act, 1813Monopoly ended except in tea and the China trade; Indian trade open to all British merchants; ₹1 lakh a year for education; Christian missionaries allowed; first claim of the Crown's sovereignty over British India.
Charter Act, 1833Trade monopoly ended completely; Company became a purely administrative body; Governor-General of Bengal became Governor-General of India (Bentinck first); all legislative power in the Governor-General's council; a Law Member added (Macaulay); Indians not to be barred from jobs by religion, birth or colour (promise in principle).
Charter Act, 1853Separate Legislative Council for law-making; open competitive examination for the Civil Service (patronage ended); Law Member became a full member; last Charter Act. 1858: the Crown took over (a later topic).

3. Land revenue systems

Understand it first

Why this matters: land revenue was the Company's biggest income (about half of it). Rates were very high and the systems changed who owned land, so they shaped rural life, debt and famine. The three systems differ in who paid the tax.

Three land revenue systems
SystemDetails
Permanent Settlement (Zamindari), 1793Cornwallis, in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa (and, from 1802, the Northern Circars and parts of Madras); about 19% of British India. Zamindars became owners and paid a fixed sum for ever (10/11 to the Company, 1/11 kept). Sunset Law: if rent was not paid by sunset on the due date, the estate was auctioned. Effects: the Company's income became certain and a loyal class grew; but no incentive for improvement, cultivators lost rights, absentee landlords, and the Company lost the rising value of land.
Ryotwari, 1820Captain Alexander Read began it in Baramahal (1792) and Thomas Munro extended it as Collector of the Ceded districts (1800) and as Governor of Madras (1820–27); in Madras and Bombay (about 51% of British India). The ryot (cultivator) is the owner and pays directly to the State; revenue about 1/3 to 1/2 of produce, revised every 20–30 years. Effects: no middleman, but rates very high, heavy debt to moneylenders, and the State acted like a landlord.
Mahalwari, 1822 and 1833Holt Mackenzie (1822), refined by Robert Bird (1833); in the North-Western Provinces, Central India and Punjab (about 30%). The mahal (village or group of villages) is the unit; the village community or its headmen (lambardars) pay jointly; revenue fixed for 20–30 years.

4. The four pillars of administration

PillarWhat was done
Civil serviceCornwallis gave high salaries and ended private trade; Fort William College (1800) and Haileybury College (1806) trained recruits; 1853 open competitive exam in London; Macaulay Committee (1854); Indians kept out of higher posts. ICS name from 1858–61; first Indian: Satyendranath Tagore (1863). Called the 'steel frame'.
ArmyMostly Indian sepoys under British officers; the Bengal, Madras and Bombay armies; high posts only for the British; the Company spent most revenue on the army. Sepoy discontent later led to 1857.
PoliceCornwallis set up the district police under darogas and removed the zamindars' police; in the Madras Presidency, Munro replaced the Palegars' village watch; Thuggee and Dacoity Department under Sleeman; the Police Act 1861 (after 1857) built the modern police.
JudiciarySupreme Court, Calcutta (1774); Cornwallis Code (1793): Collector no longer a judge, rule of law, government servants answerable to courts; Sadr Diwani and Nizamat Adalats; Macaulay's Law Commission gave the Indian Penal Code (drafted 1837, in force 1860); Indian High Courts Act 1861 set up High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras (1862). Faults: slow and costly justice, racial bias (the Ilbert Bill case later).

5. The Andhra link

  • Madras Presidency: the Telugu country was split among the Northern Circars (Visakhapatnam, Godavari, Krishna, Guntur and Srikakulam areas), the Ceded districts (Kadapa, Kurnool, Anantapur, Bellary) and Nellore; Telangana stayed with the Nizam. Each region was ruled differently, which is why zamindari and ryotwari areas existed side by side in Andhra.
  • Northern Circars: Permanent Settlement (1802) made zamindars (e.g. of Vizianagaram, Pithapuram, Venkatagiri, Bobbili) owners. Ceded districts, Nellore and others: Thomas Munro introduced Ryotwari from 1800, and ended the Palegar system (Palegars lost their forts and police rights).
  • Revolt: Uyyalawada Narasimha Reddy (1846–47), a former Palegar whose pension was cut, rose against the Company; he attacked the Koilkuntla treasury and was hanged on 22 February 1847, one of the earliest revolts before 1857.
  • Irrigation: Sir Arthur Cotton built the Dowleswaram (Godavari) Anicut (completed 1852) and the Krishna Anicut at Vijayawada (1855), turning the delta districts into the 'granary of the South' and bringing the delta prosperity (while the Rayalaseema regions stayed dry and poor).

6. Impact of consolidation

AreaPoints
PoliticsRule by law and a central bureaucracy; Indian princes reduced to dependents (Subsidiary Alliance, Lapse); separation of revenue and justice; Parliament control over the Company.
SocietySati abolished (1829), Widow Remarriage Act (1856), English education, but the rulers kept a racial distance and Indians were shut out of high posts.

POLITY · Distribution of legislative and executive powers between the Union and States

Understand it first

What is it? India is a Union of States: both the Centre and the States have their own governments, so the Constitution must say who may make laws and who may run the administration on each subject, to prevent clashes. It does this by dividing subjects into three lists (Seventh Schedule) and by linking executive power to legislative power: the government can act only where its legislature can legislate. The Constitution gives the Centre extra strength, so India is called 'federal in form, unitary in spirit' (a quasi-federal system); but the Supreme Court (S.R. Bommai, 1994) treats federalism as part of the basic structure.

The three lists

1. Legislative powers: Arts 245–255

ProvisionDetails
Art 245Territorial limits: Parliament makes laws for all or part of India; a State legislature for its State. Only Parliament can make extra-territorial laws (e.g. for Indians abroad).
Art 246Subject-matter: Parliament has exclusive power on the Union List; both can legislate on the Concurrent List (Union law prevails); States have exclusive power on the State List (subject to Parliament's overriding powers). Union List gets priority over the other two.
Seventh ScheduleUnion List: 98 subjects (originally 97); State List: 59 (originally 66); Concurrent List: 52 (originally 47). The 42nd Amendment (1976) shifted five subjects from State to Concurrent: education, forests, protection of wild animals and birds, weights and measures, administration of justice. (Borrowed from the Government of India Act 1935 and Canada's strong Centre, with the Concurrent List from Australia.)
Residuary powers (Art 248)Subjects in no list (e.g. a new technology) belong to Parliament (Entry 97 of the Union List), unlike the USA where they go to the States. Canada is the model.
Art 246A (GST)Inserted by the 101st Amendment (2016): both Parliament and States can make laws on Goods and Services Tax; the GST Council (Art 279A) decides.

2. When the Centre may legislate on State subjects

ArticleSituation
Art 249National interest: if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by two-thirds of members present and voting that Parliament should legislate on a State List subject; valid for one year, extendable by a year each time.
Art 250National Emergency: Parliament can legislate on the State List while the Emergency lasts (laws lapse six months after it ends).
Art 252Consent of two or more States: their legislatures pass resolutions asking Parliament to legislate; the law applies to those States (others may adopt it later). Example: Prize Competitions Act.
Art 253International treaties: Parliament can legislate on any subject to implement a treaty or international agreement, even a State List subject.
Art 356President's Rule: when a State's constitutional machinery fails, Parliament exercises the State's legislative powers.
Art 254 and Art 200–201On the Concurrent List, if a State law conflicts (is repugnant) with a Union law, the Union law prevails (254(1)). Exception: a State law reserved for the President and assented to by him prevails in that State (254(2)), though Parliament can later override it. Governor may reserve State Bills for the President (Art 200).

3. Doctrines used by the courts

DoctrineMeaning
Pith and substanceTo decide which list a law belongs to, look at its true nature; a law mainly on a State subject is valid even if it incidentally touches a Union subject (State of Bombay v F.N. Balsara, 1951).
Colourable legislation'What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly': a legislature cannot do indirectly what it lacks the power to do directly (K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v State of Orissa, 1953).
Harmonious constructionIf two entries overlap, read them together to give effect to both; if impossible, the Union List prevails.
Territorial nexusA State law may apply outside the State if there is a real connection (nexus) between the State and the subject (State of Bombay v R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala, 1957).

4. Executive powers

ProvisionDetails
Art 73 (Union)Union executive power extends to matters on which Parliament can legislate and to rights under treaties. Concurrent List: it extends only if the Constitution or a Parliament law gives it; otherwise the States run those laws.
Art 162 (State)State executive power extends to matters on which the State legislature can legislate, and on the Concurrent List it is subject to Union law.
Art 256, 257States must comply with Union laws; the Union can give directions to States: to keep national and military communications safe, to protect railways, and (Art 257A was deleted). If a State disobeys, Art 365 lets the President say constitutional machinery has failed, leading to Art 356.
Art 258, 258AThe President may, with a State's consent, entrust Union functions to it or its officers; a Governor may, with the Centre's consent, entrust State functions to the Union.

5. Why the Centre is strong, and the checks

  • Centre's strengths: longer Union List, residuary powers, Union law prevails on the Concurrent List, Parliament's power over the State List (Arts 249, 250, 252, 253, 356), Governor appointed by the President, single citizenship, all-India services and a single integrated judiciary.
  • Checks and balance: the Supreme Court settles Union–State disputes (Art 131); S.R. Bommai (1994) made federalism a basic feature and made Art 356 reviewable; State of West Bengal v Union of India (1963) held India is not a true federation (Union stronger); the Inter-State Council (Art 263; set up 1990); the Sarkaria Commission (1983–88) and Punchhi Commission (2007–10) studied Centre–State relations.
Exam trap

Art 245 territory; Art 246 three lists; Seventh Schedule: Union 98, State 59, Concurrent 52; 42nd Amendment: education, forests, wild animals, weights and measures, justice to Concurrent; Art 248 residuary to Union; Art 246A GST (101st Amendment 2016); Art 249 Rajya Sabha 2/3, one year; 250 Emergency; 252 consent of States; 253 treaties; 356 President's Rule; Art 254 repugnancy (State law reserved for the President prevails); Pith and substance (Balsara 1951); Colourable (Gajapati 1953); Art 73 and 162; Art 256–258; Art 263 Inter-State Council; Bommai 1994.

MATHS · Time and work

Understand it first

What is it? These problems ask how long a job takes when one or more people (or machines or pipes) work on it. The one idea behind all of them: work done = rate × time. If a person finishes a job in n days, in one day the person does 1/n of the job: that is the rate. More workers means less time, and efficient workers do more in the same time. Treat the whole job as 1 (or as a convenient number of units), then add rates when people work together.

Time and work: key ideas

1. Rate and working together

  • Rate = 1/days. If A takes a days and B takes b days, then together in one day they do 1/a + 1/b = (a + b)/(a × b), so the time together = (a × b)/(a + b) days. Example: A = 10 days, B = 15 days: together 150/25 = 6 days.
  • LCM method (fastest): take the LCM of the days as the total work in units. A (10 days) and B (15 days): total = 30 units; A does 3 units a day, B does 2; together 5 a day, so 30/5 = 6 days.
  • Finding one person's time: if A + B finish in x days and A alone in a days, B's rate = 1/x − 1/a. Efficiency: if A is twice as efficient as B, A takes half the time: rates 2 : 1, times 1 : 2.
  • Leaving midway and alternate days: work in stages: units done in the first stage, then remaining units at the new rate. Alternate days: find the work in two days (one day each), then count cycles. Example: A (10 days) and B (15 days) on alternate days: two days = 3 + 2 = 5 of 30 units, so 12 days for 30 units.

2. Pipes and cisterns

  • It is the same idea: an inlet pipe fills (a + rate), an outlet pipe empties (a − rate). Net rate = inlets − outlets. If the net rate is negative, the tank empties.
  • Example: A fills in 20 minutes, B fills in 30, C empties in 15. Take 60 units: A = +3, B = +2, C = −4 per minute; net +1 a minute, so 60 minutes.

3. Wages and the man-days formula

  • Wages are shared in the ratio of work done (rate × days), not by days alone. Example: A (6 days), B (8 days) and C together finish in 3 days. Take 24 units: A = 4, B = 3 per day, C = 24/3 − 4 − 3 = 1. Wages ratio 4 : 3 : 1; on ₹3,200, C gets ₹400.
  • Man-days formula: M₁ × D₁ × H₁ / W₁ = M₂ × D₂ × H₂ / W₂ (M = men, D = days, H = hours a day, W = work). Example: 24 men finish a job in 20 days; after 8 days, 8 men leave. Total = 24 × 20 = 480 man-days; done = 24 × 8 = 192; left = 288; 16 men need 288/16 = 18 more days.
Exam trap

Rate = 1/days; together = ab/(a + b); LCM = total units; net rate for pipes = inlets − outlets; wages ∝ work done; M₁D₁H₁/W₁ = M₂D₂H₂/W₂; more men → fewer days; if A is k times as efficient as B, A takes 1/k of the time.
