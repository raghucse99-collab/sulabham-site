HISTORY · Changes in administration, economy, society and culture under the British

Understand it first

What is it? After the Revolt of 1857 the British Crown took India from the Company (1858) and ruled it for 89 more years. This topic asks what changed under that rule: in how India was governed, in how its economy worked, and in how society and culture were shaped. The key idea: the changes were made for British benefit first; some (railways, English education, modern law) also had unintended, lasting effects on India. Learn it as Administration → Economy → Education and press → Society and culture → Andhra link → Impact.

How India was governed after 1857

1. Changes in administration

MeasureDetails
Government of India Act, 1858Crown rule replaced the Company; the Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished; the Governor-General became Viceroy (Lord Canning, first Viceroy); a Secretary of State for India (a British Cabinet minister) was helped by a Council of 15; India was ruled from London.
Queen's Proclamation, 1 November 1858Read at Allahabad by Canning: no more annexation of Indian states, religious tolerance, equal and impartial service for all subjects, respect for treaties and customs; it was the Crown's promise, often broken. (Sometimes called the 'Magna Carta of Indian liberties'.)
Indian Councils Acts1861: Viceroy's Council became a body with portfolios (Canning's portfolio system); the Legislative Council got 6–12 additional members (nominated, a few Indians like the Raja of Benares), powers restored to Madras and Bombay; Viceroy could issue ordinances. 1892: more additional members, power to discuss the budget and ask questions, indirect election in principle.
ArmyAfter 1857 (Peel Commission, 1859): more European troops (about 1 to 2 in the Bengal army, 2 to 5 in Madras and Bombay), artillery in European hands only, recruits from the 'martial races' (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims) and soldiers mixed by caste and region to prevent unity.
Princely StatesPolicy of no annexation; rulers became loyal 'props' of the Empire (about 560 states, one-third of the area) under paramountcy and a British Resident.
Local government and financeLord Mayo (1870): financial decentralisation gave provinces fixed grants for some services. Lord Ripon (1880–84): Resolution of 1882 on local self-government (elected local boards), hence the 'father of local self-government'; also repealed the Vernacular Press Act (1882), passed the first Factory Act (1881), set up the Hunter Education Commission (1882), and faced the Ilbert Bill (1883) controversy (Indian judges trying Europeans). Lord Lytton (1876–80) passed the Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act (1878).

2. Changes in the economy

The colonial economy
Understand it first

Why this matters: before the British, India was a leading exporter of cloth and spices. Under colonial rule it became a supplier of raw materials (cotton, jute, indigo, tea) and a market for British factory goods. This is called a colonial economy: the economy served the ruler's interest.

FeatureDetails
Drain of wealthA part of India's wealth went to Britain with no return: 'Home Charges' (interest on debt, pensions, salaries, costs of the India Office), profits and savings of Europeans. Dadabhai Naoroji first stated it in 'England's Debt to India' (1867) and in 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India' (1901); R.C. Dutt in 'Economic History of India' (1901–03); also M.G. Ranade and G. Subramania Iyer.
De-industrialisationCheap machine-made cloth from Britain with low duties and high duties on Indian goods ruined weavers and artisans (e.g. Dhaka muslin); fewer jobs pushed people back onto the land; ruralisation.
Commercialisation of agricultureFarmers grew cash crops (indigo, cotton, jute, opium, tea, sugarcane, tobacco) for export because taxes had to be paid in money; less land for food crops; moneylender debt grew; indigo growers in Bengal rose in the Indigo Revolt (1859–60) (Dinabandhu Mitra's play 'Nil Darpan').
FaminesHeavy land revenue, export of grain and poor relief made famines deadly: Great Famine 1876–78 (millions died; worst in Madras Presidency, Mysore, Hyderabad and the Bombay Deccan; Lytton was Viceroy); Famine Commission 1880 under Richard Strachey led to the Famine Code (1883); later Lyall (1898) and MacDonnell (1901) commissions; famines of 1896–97, 1899–1900.
Railways, post, telegraphFirst line Bombay–Thane (1853), then Calcutta–Raniganj (1854) and Madras (Royapuram–Wallajah Road, 1856). Built by British companies with a guaranteed 5% return paid from Indian revenue; most steel, engines and coal came from Britain; routes linked ports, raw-material areas and army posts. Good effects: a national market, movement of people and ideas, relief of famine areas. Bad effects: it did not build Indian industry.
Modern industryMostly Indian-owned, small at first: cotton mill, Bombay (1854, Cowasji Davar), jute mill, Rishra (1855), coal at Raniganj, later Tata Iron and Steel, Jamshedpur (1907); no heavy industry and a 'free trade' policy that gave no protection.

3. Education and the press

StepDetails
Before 1835Orientalists (Warren Hastings, William Jones: Calcutta Madrasa 1781, Asiatic Society 1784, Sanskrit College Benares 1791) backed Indian classical learning; Charter Act 1813: ₹1 lakh; the Orientalist–Anglicist controversy.
Macaulay's Minute, 1835Anglicists won: English as medium for higher education to create a class 'Indian in blood and colour, English in taste, opinions, morals and intellect'; Bentinck's Resolution (March 1835); the 'downward filtration theory' (educate the few, and learning would filter down).
Wood's Despatch, 1854'Magna Carta of English education in India': an education department in each province, universities (Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, 1857), primary schools in the vernacular, middle schools Anglo-vernacular, English colleges, grants-in-aid, teacher training, women's and vocational education.
After 1857Hunter Commission (1882): stress on primary and secondary education, private enterprise; Indian Universities Act 1904 (Curzon) and the Raleigh Commission (1902): tighter government control. Result: a small educated middle class (lawyers, teachers, clerks) that later led nationalism, but literacy stayed very low, especially for women.
PressJames Hickey's Bengal Gazette (1780) was the first newspaper; Ram Mohan Roy's Sambad Kaumudi (1821); the press became a voice of nationalism and was controlled by laws: Licensing Regulations 1823 (Adam), Metcalfe freed the press (1835), Vernacular Press Act 1878, Newspaper (Incitement to Offences) Act 1908.

4. Changes in society and culture

  • Law and rights: a uniform legal code (Indian Penal Code 1860, Code of Criminal Procedure), courts and the idea of equality before law (though Europeans got special treatment); social laws against sati (1829), for widow remarriage (1856), against female infanticide (1870) and the Age of Consent Act (1891).
  • New classes and ideas: an English-educated middle class, a new urban class of clerks, lawyers and traders; ideas of liberty, equality and nationalism from the West; missionaries ran schools and hospitals; socio-religious reform movements (a separate topic) arose in reply.
  • Knowledge and institutions: Census (first 1872, regular from 1881), Archaeological Survey of India (1861, Alexander Cunningham), Geological Survey (1851), Survey of India, medical colleges (Calcutta and Madras, 1835), printing presses; Indo-Saracenic architecture and 'Company painting'.
  • Colonial attitude: British rulers kept a racial distance (clubs, railway compartments, the Ilbert Bill dispute), ruled with a 'divide and rule' policy and described India as backward; Indians answered with reform and, later, a national movement.

5. The Andhra link

  • Famines: the Great Famine of 1876–78 (Telugu 'Dhatu' famine, from the Telugu year Dhatu) hit the Madras Presidency's Telugu districts, from the Ceded districts and Nellore to the Circars, and Hyderabad State; earlier the Guntur famine of 1832–33 was also called a terrible disaster. They led to famine relief works and later the Famine Code.
  • Irrigation and crops: Cotton's anicuts on the Godavari (1852) and Krishna (1855) raised paddy and trade in the delta, with Cocanada (Kakinada) growing as a port; cotton, tobacco, indigo and groundnut became cash crops in different regions, and Masulipatnam, once the great cloth port, declined.
  • Education and culture: Robert Noble started a school at Masulipatnam (1843), later Noble College; Madras University (1857) served the Telugu country; C.P. Brown (1798–1884, Madras Civil Service, worked in Kadapa and Madras) wrote the Telugu–English dictionary (1852), collected and published Vemana's verses and old Telugu texts; Colin Mackenzie (first Surveyor General of India) gathered the Mackenzie Manuscripts and local histories (kaifiyats) from across the Telugu land with the help of Kavali Venkata Borayya and his brothers.

POLITY · Legislative, administrative and financial relations between the Union and States

Understand it first

What is it? Day 14 covered who makes laws (legislative relations, Arts 245–255) and the basic executive rule. Here we complete the picture: administrative relations (how the Centre and States work together or control each other) and financial relations (who collects which taxes and who gets the money). Both exist because a State cannot run on its own tax income and many problems (rivers, crime, trade) cross State borders. The Constitution keeps the Centre financially strong and then shares money through the Finance Commission.

1. Administrative relations (Part XI, Arts 256–263)

ProvisionDetails
Arts 256, 257States must comply with Union laws; the Centre can give directions on national and military communications, railways and such matters; failure can lead to Art 356 (via Art 365).
Art 258, 258ADelegation of functions by consent: Centre ⇄ States.
Art 261Full faith and credit to the public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State.
Art 262 (water disputes)Parliament can make law to adjudicate disputes over inter-State rivers, and can bar the Supreme Court and other courts from hearing them. Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 sets up tribunals: e.g. Krishna (1969, Bachawat; second 2004, Brijesh Kumar), Godavari (1969), Cauvery (1990).
Art 263 (Inter-State Council)The President can set up a council to investigate and discuss common interests; the Inter-State Council was set up in 1990 (on the Sarkaria Commission's advice) with the Prime Minister as chair, Chief Ministers as members.
Zonal CouncilsSet up by the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 (statutory, not constitutional); five (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Puducherry); chaired by the Union Home Minister; advisory. The North-Eastern Council (1971) is separate.
All-India Services (Art 312)IAS, IPS and Indian Forest Service (1966): recruited by the Union, serve under States, controlled by the Centre; a new one needs a Rajya Sabha resolution by two-thirds (All-India Services Act, 1951).
Trade (Arts 301–307)Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India (Art 301), subject to reasonable restrictions by Parliament or States in the public interest; an Inter-State Trade and Commerce Commission (Art 307) is allowed but never set up.
Other linksArt 355: Union must protect every State from external aggression and internal disturbance; Governor appointed by the President; single integrated judiciary, Election Commission and CAG; the extra-constitutional National Development Council (1952) and, since 2015, NITI Aayog (replaced the Planning Commission).

MATHS · Time and distance

Understand it first

What is it? These problems link three quantities: distance (D), speed (S) and time (T). The one rule behind everything: Speed = Distance ÷ Time, so D = S × T and T = D ÷ S. At a fixed distance, more speed means less time (they are inversely related). You must keep units consistent: km with km/h and hours, or metres with m/s and seconds. To convert km/h to m/s multiply by 5/18; for m/s to km/h multiply by 18/5.

Time and distance: toolkit

1. Average speed and relative speed

  • Average speed = total distance ÷ total time (never the plain average of speeds). If a same distance is covered at x and then y, average speed = 2xy/(x + y). Example: to and fro at 60 and 40 km/h: 2 × 60 × 40 / 100 = 48 km/h (not 50).
  • Relative speed: two bodies moving the same way: x − y; moving towards each other (opposite): x + y. Example: two cars 150 km apart approach at 50 and 25 km/h: they meet in 150/75 = 2 hours.
  • Late or early: if a person at 5/6 of the usual speed is late, the time is 6/5 of the usual: the extra 1/5 of the usual time equals the delay. Example: delay 15 min ⇒ usual time = 5 × 15 = 75 min.

2. Trains

  • The train has length, so it must travel its own length more than a point object. Crossing a pole or a person: distance = length of train. Crossing a platform or bridge: distance = train length + platform length. Example: a 180 m train at 54 km/h (= 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s) crosses a 270 m platform in (180 + 270)/15 = 30 s.
  • Two trains: distance to cross = sum of both lengths; speed = relative speed (sum if opposite, difference if the same way). Example: 120 m and 180 m trains at 54 and 36 km/h opposite: relative = 90 km/h = 25 m/s; time = 300/25 = 12 s.

3. Boats and streams

  • A boat has its own speed in still water (b), and the stream has a speed (s). Downstream (with the current): b + s. Upstream (against it): b − s. If downstream speed = d and upstream speed = u: b = (d + u)/2 and s = (d − u)/2.
  • Example: a boat goes 20 km downstream in 2 hours (d = 10 km/h) and returns upstream in 4 hours (u = 5 km/h): b = 7.5 km/h, s = 2.5 km/h.
Exam trap

S = D/T; km/h × 5/18 = m/s; average speed (equal distances) = 2xy/(x + y); relative speed: same way x − y, opposite x + y; train and pole: its length; train and platform: both lengths; two trains: sum of lengths; boat: down b + s, up b − s, b = (d + u)/2, s = (d − u)/2; at 5/6 speed time is 6/5.
