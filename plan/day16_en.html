HISTORY · The Revolt of 1857: causes, course, impact

Understand it first

What is it? In May 1857 the Company's Indian soldiers (sepoys) at Meerut rose in revolt, marched to Delhi and declared the old Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II their leader. Within weeks, soldiers, dispossessed rulers, landlords, peasants and artisans in north and central India joined. It was the biggest challenge the Company ever faced. It failed by 1858–59, but it ended Company rule and changed how Britain governed India. Learn it as Causes (why) → Course and centres (how it spread) → Why it failed → What kind of revolt was it → Results.

Bahadur Shah II (Zafar), the last Mughal emperor, whom the rebels declared their leader in May 1857; he was exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Causes

Causes of the Revolt
AreaCauses
PoliticsDoctrine of Lapse (Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur); Awadh annexed (1856) for 'misgovernment', the home of many sepoys; Bahadur Shah's successors told they would lose the imperial title and the Red Fort; Nana Saheb (adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II) denied his pension; Indian rulers and nobles lost status and jobs.
EconomyVery high land revenue and sale of land for arrears; taluqdars of Awadh lost villages under the summary settlement (1856); artisans and weavers ruined by British goods; Inam Commission (1852) took away rent-free lands in the Deccan; Indians kept out of high-paid posts.
SocietyBritish racial arrogance; fear that Western education, railways and telegraph would break caste and custom; laws on sati (1829) and widow remarriage (1856) seen as interference.
ReligionMissionary activity; the Religious Disabilities Act (1850) let a convert to Christianity inherit ancestral property; rumours that the British wanted to convert Indians.
MilitaryLow pay, no promotion beyond subedar; General Service Enlistment Act (1856): new recruits had to serve overseas (crossing the sea was taboo for many); foreign service allowance (batta) stopped; loss of free postage (1854); a high ratio of Indian to British troops (about 5:1) gave confidence.
Immediate causeThe new Enfield rifle: its cartridges had to be bitten before loading and were said to be greased with cow and pig fat, offending both Hindus and Muslims.

2. Course and centres

  • 29 March 1857: Mangal Pandey of the 34th Native Infantry attacked his officers at Barrackpore (Bengal); he was hanged on 8 April and the regiment disbanded.
  • 10 May 1857: at Meerut, sepoys freed comrades jailed for refusing the cartridges, killed officers and marched to Delhi; on 11 May they proclaimed Bahadur Shah II Emperor of Hindustan. The British retook Delhi in September 1857 (John Nicholson killed); Hodson captured the emperor, who was exiled to Rangoon (died 1862).

3. Why the Revolt failed

  • Limited spread: the south, Punjab, Sindh, Rajputana and most of Bengal stayed largely quiet; Sikhs and Gurkhas fought for the British.
  • Princes and the educated class stayed away: Scindia, Holkar, the Nizam, the rulers of Patiala and Kashmir helped the British (Canning called them 'breakwaters to the storm'); the English-educated middle class did not join.
  • No common plan, leader or programme: leaders fought for local aims; there was no unified command and no idea of the future India they wanted.
  • British strength: modern weapons, telegraph and railways for fast news and troop movement, naval control, money, and able commanders.

5. Results

  • End of Company rule: the Government of India Act, 1858 and the Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858) (Day 15); the Mughal dynasty and the Peshwa title ended.
  • Policy changes: no more annexations, princes made allies; army reorganised (more British troops, artillery only with Europeans, 'martial races'); divide and rule; more caution about social reform; the Indian Councils Act 1861 began to include a few Indians.
  • Legacy: the Revolt became a symbol of resistance that inspired later nationalists.

POLITY · Constitutional bodies: Election Commission, UPSC and APPSC, CAG, Finance Commission and others

Understand it first

What is it? A constitutional body is created directly by an Article of the Constitution, so Parliament cannot abolish it by an ordinary law. (A statutory body like the NHRC is made by an Act of Parliament; an executive body like NITI Aayog by a government order.) These bodies do jobs that must stay neutral and independent of the party in power: conducting elections (Election Commission), recruiting civil servants on merit (UPSC, APPSC), checking how public money is spent (CAG), and sharing taxes (Finance Commission). That is why the Constitution gives them fixed terms, hard removal procedures and protected salaries. For each body, learn: Article → appointment → term → removal → functions.

Constitutional bodies at a glance

1. Election Commission of India (Arts 324–329)

PointDetails
RoleSuperintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament, State Legislatures and the offices of President and Vice-President (Art 324). Elections to panchayats and municipalities are conducted by State Election Commissions (Arts 243K, 243ZA).
CompositionChief Election Commissioner (CEC) and as many Election Commissioners as the President fixes; multi-member since 1993 (CEC + 2). First CEC: Sukumar Sen.
Appointment and termBy the President on the advice of a selection committee (Prime Minister, Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM) under the 2023 Act; term 6 years or age 65.
RemovalCEC: only like a Supreme Court judge (Parliament's special-majority address). Other ECs: by the President on the CEC's recommendation.

The Model Code of Conduct is not a law; EVMs used nationally since 2004; NOTA since 2013; VVPAT; National Voters' Day: 25 January (EC founded 25 Jan 1950).

3. Comptroller and Auditor General (Arts 148–151)

  • Who and why: the CAG is the head of the audit and accounts system, the 'guardian of the public purse'; Dr Ambedkar called it 'the most important officer' under the Constitution. Appointed by the President (warrant); term 6 years or age 65; removed like a Supreme Court judge; salary charged on the Consolidated Fund; not eligible for any further office under the Government after retirement. First CAG: V. Narahari Rao.
  • Work: audits all receipts and expenditure of the Union and the States (Art 149; CAG Duties and Powers Act, 1971): compliance, propriety and performance audit. Reports on Union accounts go to the President, who lays them before Parliament; State reports go to the Governor (Art 151). The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) examines them; the CAG is its 'friend, philosopher and guide' but not a member.

MATHS · Data analysis: tables and bar diagrams

Understand it first

What is it? Data interpretation (DI) gives you numbers in a table or chart and asks questions such as 'by what percent did it rise?', 'which is the highest?' or 'what is the average?' It tests careful reading and quick calculation, not new formulas. Almost every question uses only four tools: percentage change, share (percent of total), average and ratio. The biggest source of mistakes is misreading (wrong row, wrong year, wrong units), so read the title, units and scale first.

1. The four tools

ToolFormulaWatch out
% change(New − Old) ÷ Old × 100Divide by the earlier (base) value, never the new one
Share %Part ÷ Total × 100Find the right total (row, column or grand total)
AverageSum ÷ number of itemsCount the items carefully
RatioA : B, then simplify by the HCFKeep the order asked (A : B, not B : A)

2. Reading a table: worked example

Table 1: Number of students who passed in five colleges

College20232024
P240300
Q180210
R320288
S150195
T210252
Total1,1001,245
  • % change for P: (300 − 240) ÷ 240 × 100 = 25%. For S: 45 ÷ 150 = 30% (the highest). For R: (288 − 320) ÷ 320 = −10% (the only fall).
  • Average in 2024: 1,245 ÷ 5 = 249. Ratio of totals 2023 : 2024 = 1,100 : 1,245 = 220 : 249. Overall rise: 145 ÷ 1,100 ≈ 13.2%.
  • Quick trick: to compare percentage rises without full division, compare increase ÷ base as fractions: 45/150 (0.30) is bigger than 60/240 (0.25).

3. Bar diagrams

TypeWhat it shows
Simple barOne quantity over years or items (e.g. output each year).
Multiple (grouped) barTwo or more quantities side by side (e.g. exports and imports each year).
Sub-divided (stacked) barA total split into parts in one bar (e.g. a budget by sector).
Percentage barEach bar is 100%; parts show shares.
Bar diagram used in questions 4 and 5
  • Reading this chart: the total over 6 years = 40 + 45 + 50 + 42 + 60 + 66 = 303, so the average = 50.5 thousand. The only fall is 2021 → 2022 (50 → 42, −16%); the biggest rise is 2022 → 2023 (42 → 60, about 42.9%); 2024 over 2019 = 26 ÷ 40 = 65%.
  • Five reading checks: (1) title (what is measured), (2) units (thousand, lakh, crore, %), (3) scale (does the axis start at 0?), (4) base year for % change, (5) the right total.
Exam trap

% change = (new − old)/old × 100; share = part/total × 100; average = sum/count; ratio, simplified; read title, units, scale first; compare fractions instead of dividing fully; four bar types: simple, multiple, sub-divided, percentage.
