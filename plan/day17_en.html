HISTORY · Social and religious reform movements: 19th century

Understand it first

What is it? In the 1800s educated Indians began asking why their society had weakened. They blamed evils such as sati, child marriage, a ban on widow remarriage, purdah, untouchability, caste rigidity and superstition. Influenced by Western rational ideas and by pride in India's own past, they started organisations (samajs) to reform religion (one God, no idols, no priestly rituals) and society (women's education, widow remarriage, caste equality). Two methods: reformist (change through reason and new laws, e.g. Brahmo Samaj) and revivalist (go back to an idealised past, e.g. Arya Samaj). Learn it as Why → Hindu reform bodies → caste and women's reformers → Muslim, Sikh and Parsi reform → Andhra → Impact.

Major reform bodies

1. Raja Ram Mohan Roy and the Brahmo Samaj

Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), founder of the Brahmo Samaj, often called the 'father of the Indian Renaissance'. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
PointDetails
Ram Mohan RoyWrote Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin (Persian, against idol worship, 1803–04); Atmiya Sabha (1815); 'Precepts of Jesus' (1820); papers Sambad Kaumudi (1821, Bengali) and Mirat-ul-Akhbar (1822, Persian); supported English education (Hindu College 1817; Vedanta College 1825); campaigned until sati was banned (1829); given the title 'Raja' by Mughal emperor Akbar II; died at Bristol (1833).
Brahmo SamajBrahmo Sabha founded 20 August 1828 at Calcutta; worship of one formless God, against idols, rituals, priests, caste and sati; based on the Upanishads and reason.
Later leaders and splitsDebendranath Tagore (Tattvabodhini Sabha 1839) revived it; Keshab Chandra Sen spread it widely; split 1866: Adi Brahmo Samaj (Debendranath) and Brahmo Samaj of India (Keshab); the Native Marriage Act (1872) allowed Brahmo marriages; split 1878: Keshab married his under-age daughter to the Maharaja of Cooch Behar, against his own teaching, so his followers formed the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj.

2. Other Hindu reform bodies

BodyFounder, year, ideas
Young BengalHenry Vivian Derozio, teacher at Hindu College: radical free thinking, questioned all tradition (1820s–30s).
Prarthana SamajAtmaram Pandurang, Bombay, 1867 (inspired by Keshab); leaders M.G. Ranade and R.G. Bhandarkar; widow remarriage, women's education, inter-caste dining; worked within Hinduism.
Arya SamajSwami Dayananda Saraswati, Bombay, 1875; book Satyarth Prakash; 'Go back to the Vedas'; against idols, caste by birth, child marriage; shuddhi (reconversion); DAV schools (Lahore, 1886) and Gurukul (Haridwar, 1902) after his death.
Theosophical SocietyMadame Blavatsky and Col. H.S. Olcott, New York, 1875; headquarters at Adyar, Madras (1882); Annie Besant led it in India; praised ancient Hindu and Buddhist thought; Central Hindu College, Benares (1898).
Ramakrishna MissionSwami Vivekananda, 1897, in the name of his guru Ramakrishna Paramahamsa; all religions are paths to one truth; service to humanity is service to God; famous speech at the Chicago Parliament of Religions (1893).

4. Muslim, Sikh and Parsi reform

  • Aligarh Movement: Sir Syed Ahmad Khan: Scientific Society (1864), Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College, Aligarh (1875) (later Aligarh Muslim University, 1920), journal Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq; modern education and reason among Muslims. Deoband (1866): Darul Uloom, orthodox and anti-British. Ahmadiyya (1889): Mirza Ghulam Ahmad.
  • Sikh: Singh Sabha (Amritsar, 1873): education and reform, later the Gurdwara reform (Akali) movement. Parsi: Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851): Dadabhai Naoroji and others; women's education.

5. The Andhra renaissance

ReformerWork

Gurajada Apparao (1862–1915)Play Kanyasulkam (1892) attacked bride price and child marriage of girls to old men; used spoken Telugu; Vizianagaram.

The tomb of Raja Ram Mohan Roy at Arnos Vale Cemetery, Bristol, where he died in 1833. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

6. Impact

AreaPoints
PoliticsCreated a self-confident educated class and pride in India's heritage, the base of nationalism; reformers used press and associations, early forms of political work.
SocietyLaws on sati (1829), widow remarriage (1856), age of consent (1891); women's education; attack on untouchability and caste rules, though change was slow and mostly urban and upper-caste at first.
EconomySchools and colleges produced teachers, lawyers, doctors and clerks; reformers such as Ranade linked reform with economic progress.
ReligionRational, monotheistic religion; less priestly control; answer to missionary criticism; but revivalist groups also sharpened religious identities.

POLITY · National Human Rights Commission and other statutory bodies

Understand it first

What is it? A statutory body is created by an Act of Parliament (or a State law), not by the Constitution. So Parliament can change or abolish it by amending the law. These bodies watch over the government: they protect human rights (NHRC), fight corruption (CVC, Lokpal, Lokayukta), and protect women, minorities and children (NCW, NCM, NCPCR). Most of them can inquire and recommend, but cannot punish; their power comes from publicity, reports to Parliament and moral pressure. Learn each as Act → composition → who appoints → term → functions → limits.

Statutory bodies at a glance

1. National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

PointDetails
Law and originProtection of Human Rights Act, 1993 (amended 2006 and 2019); set up on 12 October 1993; in line with the UN's Paris Principles. 'Human rights' means rights to life, liberty, equality and dignity guaranteed by the Constitution or international covenants and enforceable by Indian courts.
CompositionChairperson: a former Chief Justice of India or Supreme Court judge; members: a former SC judge, a former High Court Chief Justice or judge, and three persons with knowledge of human rights (at least one woman). Deemed members: chairpersons of NCSC, NCST, NCW, NCM, NCBC, NCPCR and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities.
Appointment and termBy the President on a committee's advice: PM (chair), Lok Sabha Speaker, Rajya Sabha Deputy Chairman, Leaders of the Opposition in both Houses, Union Home Minister. Term 3 years or age 70, eligible for reappointment. Removal by the President (misbehaviour or incapacity after a Supreme Court inquiry).
Functions and powersInquire into violations (on a petition or suo motu), with the powers of a civil court; visit jails and detention centres; review laws and treaties; spread human rights literacy; it may recommend compensation or prosecution, and approach the Supreme Court or High Courts.
LimitsRecommendations are not binding; it cannot inquire into a matter more than one year after it happened; limited role over the armed forces (it can only seek a report from the Centre).
State Human Rights CommissionUnder the same Act; chair: former High Court Chief Justice or judge; appointed by the Governor on a committee led by the Chief Minister, but removed only by the President. Each district has a Human Rights Court (a Sessions Court).

2. Bodies against corruption

BodyKey facts
Central Vigilance Commission (CVC)Set up in 1964 by a government resolution on the Santhanam Committee's advice; made statutory by the CVC Act, 2003; CVC + up to 2 Vigilance Commissioners; 4 years or 65; chosen by a committee of the PM, Home Minister and Leader of the Opposition; supervises vigilance and the CBI's corruption cases.
LokpalLokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 (in force 2014); idea from the First ARC (1966); chairperson + up to 8 members, half judicial, and at least half from SC, ST, OBC, minorities and women; chosen by the PM, Speaker, Leader of the Opposition, CJI (or a judge he names) and an eminent jurist; covers the PM (with safeguards), ministers, MPs and officials; first Lokpal: Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose (2019).
LokayuktaState-level anti-corruption body under State laws; Maharashtra set up the first (1971); Andhra Pradesh: AP Lokayukta and Upa-Lokayukta Act, 1983.
CBI (note)Set up by a Home Ministry resolution (1963); gets its powers from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946; so it is not a statutory body in the strict sense.

3. Other statutory bodies

BodyAct and role
National Commission for Women (NCW)NCW Act, 1990; set up 1992; reviews laws and safeguards for women, inquires into complaints.
National Commission for Minorities (NCM)NCM Act, 1992; six notified minorities: Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis (Zoroastrians) and Jains (Jains added in 2014).
NCPCRCommissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005; set up 2007; monitors the RTE Act and POCSO Act.
NGT, NDMA and othersNational Green Tribunal (2010 Act) for environment cases; National Disaster Management Authority (2005 Act), chaired by the PM; Central Information Commission under the RTI Act, 2005 (Day 18).
Exam trap

Constitutional (by an Article) vs statutory (by an Act) vs executive (by an order); NHRC: PHRA 1993, 12 Oct 1993, chair ex-CJI or SC judge, 3 + 2 judicial members, 7 deemed members, PM-led committee, 3 yrs/70, 1-year limit, not binding; SHRC: Governor appoints, President removes; CVC 1964, Act 2003, 4 yrs/65; Lokpal Act 2013, chair + 8, first 2019; Lokayukta: Maharashtra 1971, AP Act 1983; CBI 1963, DSPE Act 1946; NCW 1990/1992; NCM 1992, six minorities; NCPCR 2005/2007; NGT 2010; NDMA 2005.

MATHS · Data analysis: line graphs and pie charts

Understand it first

What is it? A line graph joins points over time, so it shows trends: where it rises, falls, how steeply (a steeper line means a bigger change), and when two lines cross. A pie chart shows how a whole (100% = 360°) is divided into parts. In a pie chart, one percent is 3.6°, so the central angle = percentage × 3.6 and the amount = percentage × total ÷ 100. You use the same four tools as Day 16 (% change, share, average, ratio).

1. Line graph: worked example

Line graph used in questions 1–3
  • Gap between lines = surplus or deficit: exports − imports = 10, 10, −5, 5, 10, 15. So imports were more than exports only in 2021, and the surplus was largest in 2024.
  • Steepest segment = biggest change: exports 2021 → 2022 rose by 15 (55 → 70, about 27.3%), the biggest rise; imports rose every year, but exports fell once (2020 → 2021).
  • % change still divides by the earlier year: exports 2022 → 2023: 10 ÷ 70 ≈ 14.3%.

2. Pie chart: worked example

Pie chart used in questions 4 and 5
ItemShareAngle (× 3.6)Amount (of ₹40,000)
Food30%108°₹12,000
Rent25%90°₹10,000
Education15%54°₹6,000
Savings12%43.2°₹4,800
Transport10%36°₹4,000
Others8%28.8°₹3,200
  • Ratio shortcut: the ratio of two items = the ratio of their percentages (Rent : Education = 25 : 15 = 5 : 3); you need the total only to find amounts.
  • Common traps: a pie chart shows shares, not growth; when two pie charts have different totals, a bigger share does not always mean a bigger amount.
Exam trap

Line graph: steeper = bigger change; gap between lines = difference; crossing point = they become equal. Pie chart: 100% = 360°, 1% = 3.6°, angle = % × 3.6, amount = % × total ÷ 100, ratio = ratio of %; compare amounts only with the same total.
