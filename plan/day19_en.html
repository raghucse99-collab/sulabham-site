HISTORY · National Movement I: founding of the Congress; Moderates and Extremists

Understand it first

What is it? By the 1880s, educated Indians across the country began to feel that they were one nation with common grievances against British rule. They formed associations, then an all-India body, the Indian National Congress (1885). In its first twenty years the Congress was led by Moderates, who trusted British justice and asked for reforms through petitions. After 1905, a younger group, the Extremists (Assertive nationalists), demanded swaraj and used mass action: boycott and swadeshi. Learn it as Why nationalism rose → early associations → founding of the Congress → Moderates → rise of Extremists → Surat split.

Early national movement

1. Why Indian nationalism rose

AreaCauses
PoliticsPolitical unification under one administration; Lytton's measures: the Delhi Durbar (1877) held during famine, Vernacular Press Act and Arms Act (1878), lowering the ICS age limit to 19; the Ilbert Bill controversy (1883), where Europeans opposed trial by Indian judges, showing racial discrimination.
EconomyDrain of wealth, ruin of crafts, heavy land revenue and famines made economic exploitation clear; the railways, telegraph and post linked people across India.
SocietyWestern education spread ideas of liberty and democracy, and English became a common language; the press (The Hindu 1878, Amrita Bazar Patrika, Kesari) carried these ideas.
Religion and cultureReform movements and the rediscovery of India's past (by Indian and European scholars) built pride and self-confidence.

3. Founding of the Indian National Congress

Delegates at the first session of the Indian National Congress, Bombay, December 1885. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • First session: 28–31 December 1885 at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay (planned for Poona, shifted because of cholera); 72 delegates; president W.C. Bonnerjee; organised by A.O. Hume, a retired British civil servant; Viceroy Lord Dufferin; the name 'Congress' was suggested by Dadabhai Naoroji.
  • 'Safety valve' theory: Hume founded the Congress so that Indian discontent could escape safely and not become another 1857 (stated by Lala Lajpat Rai in 'Young India', 1916). Historians like Bipan Chandra say Indian leaders used Hume as a 'lightning conductor': an Englishman at the head would avoid official suspicion.

5. Why extremism grew, and the Surat split

Aurobindo presides over a meeting of the Nationalists after the Surat Congress (1907), with Tilak speaking. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Causes: failure of Moderate methods; Curzon's policies (Calcutta Corporation Act 1899, Universities Act 1904, Partition of Bengal 1905); the plague and famine of 1896–97 and harsh plague measures (the Chapekar brothers shot the plague officer Rand, 1897; Tilak jailed for sedition); world events: Ethiopia beat Italy (1896), Japan beat Russia (1905); influence of Vivekananda, Dayananda and Aurobindo's 'New Lamps for Old' (1893).
  • Surat split (1907): quarrel over the president (Moderates chose Rash Behari Ghosh; Extremists wanted Lajpat Rai or Tilak) and fear that Moderates would drop the 1906 resolutions on swadeshi and boycott. The Congress split; the British then crushed the Extremists (Tilak deported to Mandalay, 1908–14, where he wrote Gita Rahasya) and wooed the Moderates with the 1909 reforms. The two groups reunited at Lucknow in 1916 (Day 20).
  • Telugu link: P. Ananda Charlu presided over the Congress (1891); Nyapathi Subba Rao of Rajahmundry was one of the founders of The Hindu (1878); the Telugu districts sent delegates from the first sessions, and Bipin Chandra Pal's 1907 tour of Andhra (Day 20) carried the swadeshi message.

POLITY · Lokpal and Lokayukta

Understand it first

What is it? A Lokpal ('protector of the people') is an ombudsman: an independent authority that hears complaints of corruption against public servants, including the highest ones. Ordinary police and courts are slow and may face political pressure when a minister or senior officer is accused; an ombudsman gives citizens an independent forum. The idea came from Sweden (1809). India's Lokpal works at the Centre; each State has a Lokayukta. Learn it as origin → composition and selection → jurisdiction → powers → Lokayukta → criticism.

Lokpal at a glance

1. Origin

  • Ombudsman: first set up in Sweden (1809); spread to Finland, Denmark, Norway, New Zealand and the UK. In India, L.M. Singhvi coined the words 'Lokpal' and 'Lokayukta' (1963); the First Administrative Reforms Commission (1966), chaired by Morarji Desai, recommended them.
  • Long wait: Lokpal Bills were introduced from 1968 onwards but lapsed many times; the 2011 anti-corruption movement led by Anna Hazare pushed Parliament to pass the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 (in force 16 January 2014). First Lokpal: Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose (March 2019); Justice A.M. Khanwilkar became chairperson in 2024.

2. Composition, selection and removal

PointDetails
MembersChairperson (a former CJI or Supreme Court judge, or an eminent person with 25 years' experience in anti-corruption, public administration, law etc.) + up to 8 members; half judicial; at least half from SC, ST, OBC, minorities and women.
Selection committeePrime Minister (chair), Lok Sabha Speaker, Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or leader of the largest opposition party), CJI or a Supreme Court judge he nominates, and an eminent jurist nominated by the President on the others' advice; a search committee prepares the panel. Appointed by the President.
Term and removal5 years or age 70; not eligible for re-appointment or most later government posts. Removed by the President for misbehaviour after a Supreme Court inquiry, on a petition signed by at least 100 MPs (or on a reference by the President).

3. Jurisdiction and powers

  • Covers: the Prime Minister, Union ministers, MPs, Group A, B, C and D central officers, and bodies or NGOs funded by government or receiving foreign donations above ₹10 lakh. Prime Minister: limits: no inquiry on international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space; an inquiry needs the full bench and two-thirds of members, held in camera. MPs are not covered for what they say or vote in Parliament (Art 105).
  • Not covered: judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Powers: has an inquiry wing and a prosecution wing; powers of a civil court; can order attachment of property gained by corruption; superintendence over the CBI for cases it refers; trials by special courts. Complaints must be made within 7 years of the offence; false complaints can be punished.

4. Lokayukta in the States

  • Section 63 of the 2013 Act requires every State to set up a Lokayukta by its own law within one year; the structure, powers and coverage are left to each State, so they differ widely. Maharashtra set up the first Lokayukta (1971). Andhra Pradesh: the AP Lokayukta and Upa-Lokayukta Act, 1983; the Lokayukta is appointed by the Governor after consulting the Chief Justice of the High Court and the Leader of the Opposition, and covers State ministers and public servants.

5. Criticism and evaluation

  • Strengths: independent body with judicial members and social diversity; covers even the PM; time-bound process; property attachment. Weaknesses: only statutory (not constitutional); the selection committee can be dominated by the government side; the judiciary is excluded; delays in appointments and few convictions; complainants may fear exposure; State Lokayuktas vary in strength.

SCIENCE · Space technology: launch vehicles, satellites and ISRO missions

Understand it first

What is it? A satellite is a machine that goes round the Earth (or another body) in an orbit. To put it there, a rocket (launch vehicle) must give it a very high speed (about 8 km per second for a low orbit). Satellites help daily life: TV and phone links, weather forecasts and cyclone warnings, maps, crops and disasters (remote sensing), and navigation. India's space work began to serve development, as Vikram Sarabhai said. Learn it as history → launch vehicles → orbits → satellite families → missions → centres and private players.

1. History

  • INCOSPAR (1962) under Vikram Sarabhai (father of the Indian space programme); first rocket (Nike-Apache) from Thumba (TERLS), Kerala, on 21 November 1963; ISRO formed on 15 August 1969; Department of Space and Space Commission (1972).
  • Aryabhata, India's first satellite, launched by a Soviet rocket on 19 April 1975; Bhaskara (1979); SLV-3 put Rohini in orbit in 1980 (project director A.P.J. Abdul Kalam), making India the sixth nation with its own launch capability.

2. Launch vehicles and orbits

Indian launch vehicles
OrbitHeight and use
Low Earth orbit (LEO)About 200–2,000 km; the space station, crewed flights, some Earth observation.
Sun-synchronous polar orbit (SSPO)About 600–800 km, passing over the poles; crosses each place at the same local time every day, so images have the same lighting; remote sensing (IRS, Cartosat); usually launched by PSLV.
Geostationary orbit (GEO)35,786 km above the equator; goes round once in 24 hours with the Earth, so it seems fixed over one place; communication, TV, weather (INSAT, GSAT); reached via a geosynchronous transfer orbit (GTO) by GSLV or LVM3.
Lagrange point L1About 1.5 million km from Earth towards the Sun; a spacecraft there can watch the Sun without eclipses (Aditya-L1).
  • PSLV: four stages (solid and liquid alternately); India's workhorse; first success 1994; launched 104 satellites in one flight (15 February 2017). GSLV: uses a cryogenic upper stage (liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen at very low temperatures); India's own cryogenic stage flew in 2014. LVM3 (GSLV Mk III): heaviest, about 4 tonnes to GTO; used for Chandrayaan-2 and -3 and Gaganyaan. SSLV: small satellites up to 500 kg, quick and cheap.

3. Satellite families

FamilyPurpose
INSAT / GSATCommunication, TV, telemedicine, weather (INSAT-3D series), disaster warnings.
IRS / EOSRemote sensing for crops, water, forests, cities, disasters: Resourcesat, Cartosat (maps), RISAT (radar, sees through clouds), Oceansat.
NavIC (IRNSS)India's regional navigation system (7 satellites; covers India and about 1,500 km around); GAGAN improves GPS accuracy for aircraft.

4. Major missions

MissionFacts
Chandrayaan-1 (2008)PSLV; its instruments found evidence of water molecules on the Moon.
Mangalyaan (MOM)Launched 5 November 2013 by PSLV; entered Mars orbit on 24 September 2014: India became the first country to succeed on its first attempt and the first in Asia to reach Mars orbit.
Chandrayaan-2 and -3Chandrayaan-2 (2019): the orbiter works, but the lander Vikram crashed; Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole on 23 August 2023, the first to land there; the Pragyan rover confirmed sulphur.
Aditya-L1, XPoSatAditya-L1 (launched September 2023, at L1 in January 2024) studies the Sun's corona and solar wind; XPoSat (January 2024) studies X-ray polarisation from black holes and neutron stars.
Recent and futureSpaDeX achieved docking of two satellites (January 2025), making India the fourth country to do so; NISAR (NASA–ISRO radar satellite) launched in July 2025; Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian to visit the International Space Station (Axiom-4, June 2025); Gaganyaan human spaceflight; Bharatiya Antariksh Station planned by 2035.

5. Centres and private players

  • Satish Dhawan Space Centre (SDSC SHAR), Sriharikota, Andhra Pradesh: India's main launch port (on the east coast, near the equator, launching over the sea). VSSC (Thiruvananthapuram, rockets), URSC (Bengaluru, satellites), LPSC (liquid engines), SAC (Ahmedabad, payloads), NRSC (Hyderabad, remote-sensing data); a second spaceport at Kulasekarapattinam (Tamil Nadu) for SSLV.
  • Private sector: IN-SPACe (2020) authorises private firms; Skyroot's Vikram-S (November 2022) was India's first private rocket; Agnikul's Agnibaan (May 2024) flew a 3D-printed engine; NSIL sells ISRO's launch and satellite services.
