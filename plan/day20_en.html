HISTORY · National Movement II: Swadeshi, Home Rule, Lucknow Pact, revolutionaries

Understand it first

What is it? Between 1905 and the early 1930s the national movement moved beyond petitions. The Partition of Bengal (1905) produced the first mass movement, Swadeshi (use Indian goods, boycott British ones). The British answered with repression and 'divide and rule' (separate electorates for Muslims, 1909). During the First World War, the Home Rule Leagues kept the demand alive, and the Lucknow Pact (1916) briefly united the Congress and the Muslim League. Alongside, revolutionaries used arms against British officials, at home and abroad. Learn it as Partition → Swadeshi → League and reforms → Home Rule and Lucknow → revolutionaries (two phases) → Andhra → impact.

From Partition to Lucknow Pact

1. Partition of Bengal and the Swadeshi Movement

Map of the 1905 Partition: Eastern Bengal and Assam (capital Dacca) was separated from Bengal. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
PointDetails
PartitionAnnounced by Curzon in July 1905, effective 16 October 1905: Eastern Bengal and Assam (Muslim-majority, capital Dacca) and the rest of Bengal. Official reason: Bengal was too big to govern; real aim: to weaken Bengali nationalism by dividing Hindus and Muslims and Bengali speakers.
ProtestBoycott resolution at the Calcutta Town Hall (7 August 1905); 16 October observed as a day of mourning: people fasted, bathed in the Ganga, tied rakhis (Rabindranath Tagore) as a sign of unity, and sang 'Amar Sonar Bangla' and 'Bande Mataram'.
MethodsBoycott and public bonfires of foreign cloth; swadeshi goods and industries (P.C. Ray's Bengal Chemicals, Bengal Lakshmi Cotton Mills, V.O. Chidambaram Pillai's Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company, Tuticorin, 1906); national education (Bengal National College, 1906, Aurobindo principal; National Council of Education); samitis (Ashwini Kumar Dutt's Swadesh Bandhab Samiti, Barisal); art and songs (Abanindranath Tagore).
Limits and endFew peasants and Muslims joined; repression and the Surat split (1907) weakened it; it turned partly to revolutionary action. The Partition was annulled in 1911 at the Delhi Durbar of George V, and the capital moved from Calcutta to Delhi.

2. Muslim League and the Morley–Minto Reforms

  • Simla Deputation (1 October 1906): Muslim leaders led by the Aga Khan met Viceroy Minto and asked for separate electorates. All-India Muslim League founded at Dacca on 30 December 1906 (Aga Khan, Nawab Salimullah).
  • Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley–Minto): enlarged councils, some elected members, right to discuss the budget; separate electorates for Muslims (Muslims vote only for Muslim candidates), so Minto is called the 'father of communal electorate'; S.P. Sinha became the first Indian in the Viceroy's Executive Council.

3. Home Rule Leagues and the Lucknow Pact

Annie Besant, who founded the All-India Home Rule League (1916) and was the first woman president of the Congress (1917). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
PointDetails
Home Rule Leagues (1916)Idea from Irish Home Rule: self-government within the Empire. Tilak's League (April 1916, Belgaum): Maharashtra (except Bombay city), Karnataka, Central Provinces, Berar. Annie Besant's League (September 1916, Madras): rest of India; papers New India and Commonweal. Besant's arrest (June 1917) caused protests.
Lucknow Session and Pact (December 1916)Moderates and Extremists reunited; the Congress and the Muslim League agreed on joint demands (more elected members, Indian majority in councils); the Congress accepted separate electorates; key figures: Tilak and Jinnah; Congress president A.C. Mazumdar.
ResultsMontagu's August Declaration (20 August 1917) promised the gradual development of self-governing institutions and responsible government; Besant became the first woman Congress president (Calcutta, 1917); this led to the 1919 reforms (Day 21).

5. The Andhra region

  • Vande Mataram movement: Bipin Chandra Pal's tour (April 1907) of Rajahmundry, Kakinada, Machilipatnam, Vijayawada and Madras spread Swadeshi; at Rajahmundry, students wearing 'Vande Mataram' badges were punished; a clash at Kakinada (1907) followed an insult to a student; Gadicherla Harisarvottama Rao was jailed (1908) for writings in his paper 'Swarajya', among the first political prisoners of Andhra.
  • Press and education: Krishna Patrika (1902, Machilipatnam), edited by Mutnuri Krishna Rao; Andhra Patrika (1908) by Kasinathuni Nageswara Rao; Andhra Jateeya Kalasala (national college), Machilipatnam (1910), Kopalle Hanumantha Rao. Home Rule branches spread in Andhra towns (1916–17). Alluri Sitarama Raju led the Rampa rebellion (1922–24) of tribal people in the Godavari hills; killed in May 1924 (Day 21).

POLITY · Centre–State relations and the need for reforms: Rajamannar, Sarkaria, Punchhi

Understand it first

What is it? Days 14 and 15 showed how powers and money are divided. In practice, friction arises: States complain that the Centre is too strong, that Governors act politically, that Art 356 is misused, and that they get too little money for their duties. Several committees and commissions studied the problem and suggested reforms. Exams ask who set up which body, when, and what it recommended. Learn it as areas of friction → each review body → court cases → present trends (cooperative federalism).

1. Areas of friction

  • Governor: appointed by the Centre and seen as its agent; disputes over choosing a Chief Minister in a hung assembly, recommending Art 356, and delaying assent to State Bills. Art 356: used more than 100 times, often against opposition-ruled States, before the Bommai judgment.
  • Others: All-India Services controlled by the Centre; Concurrent List laws by Parliament; deployment of central forces; river water disputes; finance: dependence on central grants and schemes, use of cesses and surcharges that are not shared, and GST compensation issues.
Review bodies

2. Review bodies and their recommendations

BodySet up by, yearKey recommendations
Administrative Reforms CommissionCentre, 1966 (Morarji Desai, later K. Hanumanthaiah)Set up an Inter-State Council; appoint non-partisan Governors; delegate more powers to States.

Sarkaria CommissionCentre, 1983; Justice R.S. Sarkaria (members B. Sivaraman, S.R. Punchhi CommissionCentre, 2007; Justice M.M. Punchhi; report 2010273 recommendations; Governor: fixed 5-year term, removal only by a resolution of the State legislature, decide on Bills within 6 months, should not be university chancellor, may sanction prosecution of ministers; 'localised emergency': apply Arts 355/356 to a district or part of a State instead of the whole; stronger Inter-State Council; laws on Concurrent subjects after consultation through it.

3. Court cases and present trends

  • S.R. Bommai (1994): Art 356 is subject to judicial review; majority must be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Governor's room; secularism and federalism are basic features. Rameshwar Prasad (2006): the dissolution of the Bihar Assembly (2005) was unconstitutional. Nabam Rebia (2016): the Governor's discretion is limited; he cannot summon or advance an Assembly session on his own.
  • Governors and State Bills: in April 2025 the Supreme Court (State of Tamil Nadu v Governor of Tamil Nadu) set time limits for Governors to act on Bills. A Presidential reference under Art 143 followed; in November 2025 the Court's opinion said courts cannot impose fixed time limits or 'deemed assent', though Governors cannot sit on Bills indefinitely. (Check current affairs for later developments.)
  • Cooperative federalism: the Inter-State Council (1990), NITI Aayog's Governing Council (PM and all Chief Ministers, from 2015), the GST Council (2017), and higher tax devolution (42%, then 41%) are steps where the Centre and States decide together. Competitive federalism means States compete for investment and development (e.g. ease of doing business rankings).

SCIENCE · Defence technology: DRDO and the IGMDP

Understand it first

What is it? A country that buys all its weapons depends on other countries in a war, and pays heavily. So India built its own defence research system, DRDO, to design missiles, aircraft, tanks, ships, radars and electronics. The Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme (IGMDP) made India a missile power. Missiles are classified by where they are fired from and at what (surface-to-surface, surface-to-air, air-to-air, anti-tank, submarine-launched) and by how they fly: ballistic (rocket-powered up, then falls like a thrown stone) or cruise (flies like a plane, powered all the way, low and guided).

1. DRDO and the IGMDP

  • DRDO was formed in 1958 by merging older defence science units; it works under the Ministry of Defence with about 50 laboratories. IGMDP began in 1983 under Dr A.P.J. Abdul Kalam (the 'Missile Man') to build five missiles; it was formally closed in 2008 after its goals were met, and each line continued separately.
IGMDP: five missiles

2. Important missiles

MissileType and facts
Agni seriesSurface-to-surface ballistic; Agni-I (about 700 km) to Agni-V (over 5,000 km); Agni-V tested with MIRV (several warheads hitting different targets) in Mission Divyastra (March 2024); Agni-Prime (1,000–2,000 km) is a new-generation canister missile.
Prithvi, Dhanush, PralayShort-range surface-to-surface; Dhanush is the naval version of Prithvi; Pralay is a newer quasi-ballistic short-range missile.
BrahMosSupersonic cruise missile (about Mach 2.8–3) by BrahMos Aerospace, an India–Russia joint venture (1998); name from the Brahmaputra and Moskva rivers; launched from land, ships, submarines and aircraft; range extended beyond the original 290 km; exported to the Philippines.
Akash, Nag, Astra, K-series, NirbhayAkash: medium-range surface-to-air; Nag: anti-tank fire-and-forget (Helina from helicopters); Astra: air-to-air beyond visual range; K-15 (Sagarika), K-4: submarine-launched ballistic missiles; Nirbhay: subsonic long-range cruise missile.
Missile defence and othersBallistic Missile Defence: Prithvi Air Defence (high altitude) and Advanced Air Defence (low altitude); Mission Shakti (27 March 2019): an anti-satellite (ASAT) missile destroyed a satellite in low Earth orbit; hypersonic tests (HSTDV 2020; a long-range hypersonic missile, November 2024). Indian systems such as Akash and BrahMos were used in Operation Sindoor (May 2025).

3. Platforms, policies and the Telugu states

  • Platforms: LCA Tejas (light combat aircraft; ADA and HAL); Arjun main battle tank; Pinaka rocket launcher; Dhruv helicopter; Prachand light combat helicopter (2022); INS Vikrant, the first indigenous aircraft carrier (Cochin Shipyard, 2022); INS Arihant, the first nuclear-powered ballistic-missile submarine (SSBN), which completed India's nuclear triad (land, air, sea) in 2018; INS Arighaat (2024).
  • Policies: positive indigenisation lists (items that must be made in India), Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020, iDEX (2018) for start-ups, defence industrial corridors in Uttar Pradesh and Tamil Nadu; defence exports reached a record of about ₹23,600 crore in 2024–25.
  • Andhra Pradesh and Telangana: Naval Science and Technological Laboratory (NSTL), Visakhapatnam (torpedoes and underwater weapons); the Eastern Naval Command and the Ship Building Centre at Visakhapatnam, where the Arihant-class submarines were built; the Dr A.P.J. Abdul Kalam Missile Complex, Hyderabad (DRDL, RCI, ASL), the heart of missile design. Missile tests are mostly at Chandipur and Abdul Kalam Island (Odisha).
