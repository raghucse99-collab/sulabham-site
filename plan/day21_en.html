HISTORY · National Movement III: Gandhi, Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience

Understand it first

What is it? From 1917 Mahatma Gandhi turned the national movement into a mass struggle of peasants, workers, women and students. His method was satyagraha: non-violent resistance to unjust laws, accepting punishment without hatred, to change the opponent's heart. He led three all-India movements; this day covers the first two: Non-Cooperation (1920–22) and Civil Disobedience (1930–34). Learn it as Gandhi's early satyagrahas → 1919 (Act, Rowlatt, Jallianwala) → Non-Cooperation → 1922–29 → Civil Disobedience and Round Tables → Andhra → impact.

The Gandhian phase

1. Gandhi's early work

EventFacts
South Africa (1893–1914)Fought racial discrimination against Indians; first used satyagraha (1906, against pass laws); Phoenix Settlement (1904), Tolstoy Farm (1910). Returned to India on 9 January 1915 (now Pravasi Bharatiya Divas); Gokhale was his political guru; Sabarmati Ashram (1917).
Champaran (1917)Bihar; indigo peasants forced under the tinkathia system (3/20 of land under indigo); invited by Rajkumar Shukla; helpers Rajendra Prasad, J.B. Kripalani; India's first civil disobedience; the system was ended.
Ahmedabad (1918)Cotton mill workers' wage dispute; Gandhi's first hunger strike in India; workers got a 35% raise.
Kheda (1918)Gujarat; crop failure, peasants asked for remission of land revenue; Gandhi's first non-cooperation; Vallabhbhai Patel joined.

2. 1919: reforms, Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh

  • Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford): dyarchy in the provinces (transferred subjects like education and health under Indian ministers; reserved subjects like police and finance under the Governor); a bicameral central legislature; separate electorates extended (to Sikhs and others); in force from 1921.
  • Rowlatt Act (March 1919): allowed arrest and jail without trial ('no vakil, no dalil, no appeal'). Gandhi called the Rowlatt Satyagraha, an all-India hartal (April 1919), his first nationwide movement.
  • Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919, Baisakhi): at Amritsar, people met peacefully to protest the arrest of Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal; General Dyer blocked the only exit and fired without warning, killing hundreds (official figure 379). Rabindranath Tagore returned his knighthood; the Hunter Committee inquired; Udham Singh later shot Michael O'Dwyer (London, 1940).
Bullet marks preserved on a wall at Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

3. Khilafat and Non-Cooperation (1920–22)

PointDetails
KhilafatIndian Muslims protested Britain's harsh treatment of the Turkish Sultan (Khalifa) after the First World War; leaders Ali brothers (Mohammad Ali, Shaukat Ali), Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Hakim Ajmal Khan; Gandhi joined it to build Hindu–Muslim unity.
LaunchApproved at the Calcutta special session (September 1920) and confirmed at Nagpur (December 1920), where the Congress goal became swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means and it was reorganised on a linguistic basis (Pradesh Congress Committees).
ProgrammeGive up titles; boycott government schools, colleges, law courts, councils and foreign cloth; swadeshi, khadi and the charkha; national schools (Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith, Bihar Vidyapith); Tilak Swaraj Fund (₹1 crore); boycott of the Prince of Wales' visit (November 1921).
EndAt Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur, UP), on 5 February 1922, a mob burnt a police station, killing 22 policemen; Gandhi withdrew the movement (Bardoli resolution, 12 February 1922), as violence broke his principle; he was jailed for six years (released 1924). Many leaders felt the withdrawal was a mistake.

4. 1922–1929: between the movements

  • Swaraj Party (January 1923): C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru entered councils to oppose from within; the 'no-changers' (Rajagopalachari, Patel) did constructive work. Simon Commission (appointed November 1927, all seven members British) arrived on 3 February 1928 and met 'Simon go back' protests; Lala Lajpat Rai died after a lathi charge at Lahore (1928); in Madras, Tanguturi Prakasam bared his chest before police guns and became 'Andhra Kesari'.
  • Nehru Report (August 1928): Motilal Nehru's draft constitution asked for dominion status; Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929) rejected it. Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) against a revenue rise made Vallabhbhai Patel 'Sardar'. Lahore session (December 1929), president Jawaharlal Nehru, passed the Purna Swaraj (complete independence) resolution; the flag was hoisted on the Ravi (31 December 1929); 26 January 1930 was celebrated as Independence Day.

5. Civil Disobedience (1930–34)

Gandhi on the Salt March to Dandi, 1930. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
EventFacts
Dandi MarchGandhi sent 11 demands to Viceroy Irwin; then marched from Sabarmati (12 March 1930) with 78 followers about 385 km to Dandi, and broke the salt law on 6 April 1930. Salt was chosen because it touched every Indian, rich and poor.
SpreadVedaranyam (C. Rajagopalachari, Tamil Nadu); Dharasana salt works (Sarojini Naidu); North-West Frontier: Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan's Khudai Khidmatgars ('Red Shirts'), the Qissa Khwani Bazaar firing (Peshawar, April 1930), where Garhwal Rifles soldiers refused to fire; Rani Gaidinliu in the North-East; no-tax and forest satyagrahas; women joined in large numbers; boycott of foreign cloth and liquor shops.
Round Tables and PactFirst RTC (London, November 1930–January 1931): Congress absent. Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931): movement suspended, prisoners (not convicted of violence) released, coastal people allowed to make salt for personal use, Congress to attend the next RTC. Karachi session (March 1931), president Vallabhbhai Patel: resolutions on Fundamental Rights and National Economic Programme. Second RTC (September–December 1931): Gandhi the sole Congress delegate; failed over minority representation.
Second phase and afterMovement renewed (January 1932) and crushed by Viceroy Willingdon; Communal Award (August 1932) and Poona Pact (September 1932) (Day 18); Third RTC (November–December 1932): Congress absent; movement withdrawn (1934). These led to the Government of India Act, 1935: provincial autonomy (dyarchy ended in provinces), a proposed All-India Federation (never formed), dyarchy at the Centre; in the 1937 elections the Congress formed ministries in most provinces, including Madras (C. Rajagopalachari; T. Prakasam as revenue minister).

6. The Andhra region

EventFacts
Bezwada AICC (1921)The All-India Congress Committee met at Bezwada (Vijayawada), 31 March–1 April 1921; Pingali Venkayya presented a design for a national flag to Gandhi; the target of one crore members and ₹1 crore was set.
Chirala-Perala (1921)Against heavy municipal taxes, people led by Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya ('Andhra Ratna') left the town and lived in a camp called Ramnagar.
Pedanandipadu and Palnadu (1921–22)Pedanandipadu (Guntur): a no-tax campaign led by Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary; Palnadu forest satyagraha: villagers grazed cattle in reserved forests against forest rules; Kanneganti Hanumanthu was shot dead (1922).
Rampa Rebellion (1922–24)Alluri Sitarama Raju led tribal people of the Godavari Agency hills against forest laws that banned podu (shifting) cultivation and against forced labour; attacked police stations at Chintapalli, Krishnadevipeta and Rajavommangi (August 1922); captured and shot on 7 May 1924 at Koyyuru.
1928–1934Prakasam's stand against the Simon Commission; salt satyagraha camps along the Andhra coast; women such as Durgabai (Deshmukh) took part; leaders like Konda Venkatappayya, Bulusu Sambamurthy and Ayyadevara Kaleswara Rao led local campaigns.

POLITY · Unitary and federal features of the Constitution

Understand it first

What is it? In a federation (like the USA), power is divided by the Constitution between a national government and regional governments, and neither can take away the other's powers on its own. In a unitary state (like the UK), the national government holds all power and may delegate some. India's Constitution never uses the word 'federation'; Art 1 calls India a 'Union of States'. Dr Ambedkar explained why: the Union is not the result of an agreement among States, and no State can leave it. India has federal features but a strong unitary bias, especially in emergencies. Learn it as federal features → unitary features → how scholars describe India → special arrangements (asymmetric federalism) → court rulings.

Federal or unitary?
Federal featuresUnitary (non-federal) features
Dual polity: Union and State governmentsStrong Centre: longer Union List, residuary powers with the Centre
Written Constitution, the supreme lawStates not indestructible: Parliament can change their area, boundaries and names (Art 3) without their consent
Division of powers in three listsSingle Constitution for Union and States; single citizenship
Rigid: federal provisions need ratification by half the StatesFlexible: most of the Constitution can be amended by Parliament alone
Independent judiciary to settle Union–State disputes (Art 131)Emergency provisions (Arts 352, 356, 360) can turn the system unitary
Bicameralism: the Rajya Sabha represents the StatesUnequal representation of States in the Rajya Sabha (by population, unlike the US Senate)
Governor appointed by the Centre; reservation of State Bills for the President (Art 200, 201)
Integrated judiciary, All-India Services, single Election Commission and CAG; Parliament's power over the State List (Arts 249–253); Centre's directions to States (Arts 256, 257)

How scholars describe Indian federalism

ScholarDescription
K.C. Wheare'Quasi-federal': a unitary state with subsidiary federal features
Ivor Jennings'A federation with a strong centralising tendency'
Granville Austin'Cooperative federalism'
Morris-Jones'Bargaining federalism'
Dr AmbedkarFederal in normal times, unitary in emergencies; an 'indestructible union of destructible States'
  • 'Holding together' federation: India's federation was formed by dividing a unitary British India into States, not by independent States coming together (as in the USA). That is why the Centre is stronger.
  • Asymmetric federalism: some States have special provisions: Art 371 (Maharashtra, Gujarat), 371A (Nagaland), 371D (Andhra Pradesh and Telangana: equitable opportunities in public employment and education for different parts of the State through local cadres and zones; 32nd Amendment, 1973), 371E (a Central University in Andhra Pradesh), 371J (Hyderabad-Karnataka region); Fifth and Sixth Schedules for tribal areas.
  • Court rulings: State of West Bengal v Union of India (1963): the Constitution is not truly federal; S.R. Bommai (1994): federalism is a basic feature; Kuldip Nayar (2006): removing the domicile rule for Rajya Sabha members did not violate federalism; Government of NCT of Delhi cases (2018, 2023) spoke of 'asymmetric federalism' and elected-government control over services.
Exam trap

Art 1 'Union of States'; Ambedkar: no agreement, no right to secede; federal: dual polity, written, division of powers, rigid (half States), independent judiciary (Art 131), bicameral; unitary: strong Centre, residuary, Art 3, single Constitution and citizenship, flexible, emergencies, unequal Rajya Sabha, Governor, Art 200–201, integrated judiciary, AIS, EC, CAG, Arts 249–253, 256–257; Wheare quasi-federal; Jennings centralising; Austin cooperative; Morris-Jones bargaining; holding-together; 371D (AP, Telangana; 32nd Amendment 1973), 371E; West Bengal 1963, Bommai 1994, Kuldip Nayar 2006.

SCIENCE · ICT: Digital India, e-governance and cyber security

Understand it first

What is it? ICT (information and communication technology) means using computers, the internet and mobile phones to store, share and use information. E-governance uses ICT to deliver government services quickly, transparently and at the citizen's doorstep: certificates, pensions, land records, payments. As more life moves online, cyber security (protecting systems and data from attack, theft and fraud) becomes essential. You see this daily in Sachivalayam work. Learn it as Digital India → digital public infrastructure → e-governance (national and AP) → cyber laws and institutions → common threats.

1. Digital India and digital public infrastructure

  • Digital India (1 July 2015), MeitY: three visions: digital infrastructure as a utility to every citizen, governance and services on demand, digital empowerment of citizens. Nine pillars: broadband highways, universal mobile access, public internet access, e-governance, e-Kranti (electronic delivery of services), information for all, electronics manufacturing, IT for jobs, early-harvest programmes.
  • Digital public infrastructure ('India Stack'): Aadhaar (UIDAI 2009, Aadhaar Act 2016; upheld with limits in Puttaswamy, 2018) for identity and eKYC; UPI (NPCI, 2016) for instant payments; DigiLocker (2015) for documents; eSign; Account Aggregator for consent-based data sharing; ONDC (2022) for open e-commerce; the JAM trinity (Jan Dhan 2014, Aadhaar, Mobile) powers Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT).

InitiativeFacts

Benefits and challengesSpeed, transparency, fewer middlemen, less corruption; challenges: the digital divide (rural, elderly, women), connectivity, digital literacy, data privacy and cyber fraud.

3. Cyber security: laws and institutions

Law or bodyFacts
Information Technology Act, 2000Legal recognition of e-records and digital signatures; amended 2008. Sec 43: damage to computers; 66: hacking; 66C: identity theft; 66D: cheating by personation; 66E: violation of privacy; 66F: cyber terrorism; 69A: blocking online content; 70A: critical infrastructure agency; 79: safe harbour for intermediaries. Sec 66A (offensive messages) was struck down in Shreya Singhal (2015).
InstitutionsCERT-In (2004, MeitY): national nodal agency for cyber incidents (since 2022 incidents must be reported within 6 hours); NCIIPC: protects critical information infrastructure (power, banking, telecom); Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C, 2020, MHA); national cybercrime helpline 1930 and cybercrime.gov.in; National Cyber Security Policy (2013).
Data protection and telecomDigital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: consent-based processing, rights of the 'data principal', a Data Protection Board; its Rules were notified in 2025. Telecommunications Act, 2023 replaced the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885.
Common threatsPhishing (fake links and messages), malware and ransomware (locks data for ransom, e.g. WannaCry, 2017), DDoS attacks (flooding a website), identity theft and SIM swap, 'digital arrest' scams (fake police or officials on video calls), deepfakes. India is not a party to the Budapest Convention on cybercrime; the UN adopted a cybercrime convention in December 2024.
