HISTORY · Independence and Partition; integration of the princely States

Understand it first

What is it? In 1947 British rule ended, but India was divided into India and Pakistan, amid terrible violence. At the same time about 560 princely States, which covered about two-fifths of the land, became legally free when British paramountcy ended. If they had stayed apart, India would have broken into pieces. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V.P. Menon, through persuasion, pressure and, where needed, force, brought almost all of them into India by 1949. Learn it as Mountbatten and the 3 June Plan → Independence Act → Partition and its cost → integration method → difficult cases (Junagadh, Kashmir, Hyderabad) → later additions.

The road to 15 August 1947

1. The Mountbatten Plan and the Independence Act

StepFacts
Mountbatten (March 1947)Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, found the Cabinet Mission plan dead and communal violence spreading; he concluded Partition was unavoidable.
3 June Plan (1947)Punjab and Bengal assemblies to vote on partition (both voted to divide); referendums in NWFP and Sylhet (Assam); Sindh and Baluchistan to decide; a Boundary Commission under Cyril Radcliffe; princely States free to join either dominion; the date was advanced to 15 August 1947. The Congress and the League accepted it.
Indian Independence Act (assent 18 July 1947)Created two independent dominions, India and Pakistan, from 15 August 1947; a Governor-General for each; the Constituent Assemblies also worked as legislatures; British paramountcy over princely States lapsed; the Secretary of State for India was abolished; the King dropped the title 'Emperor of India'.
Partition and its costThe Radcliffe Award was announced on 17 August 1947; riots killed an estimated several hundred thousand to two million people, and about 15 million were uprooted. Gandhi stayed in riot-hit Noakhali and Calcutta on 15 August and was assassinated on 30 January 1948. Mountbatten was free India's first Governor-General; C. Rajagopalachari (1948–50) was the first and last Indian Governor-General.
Map of the Partition of British India in 1947. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

2. Integration of the princely States

  • Machinery: the States Department (July 1947) under Sardar Patel, with V.P. Menon as secretary. Rulers signed an Instrument of Accession, handing over only defence, external affairs and communications, and a Standstill Agreement to keep existing arrangements. By 15 August 1947, almost all States had acceded except Junagadh, Hyderabad and Jammu and Kashmir.
  • Second stage (1948–49): small States were merged into neighbouring provinces (e.g. Orissa and Chhattisgarh States) or grouped into unions (e.g. Saurashtra, Rajasthan, PEPSU, Madhya Bharat, Travancore–Cochin) under a Rajpramukh; some became centrally administered (e.g. Manipur, Tripura, 1949). Rulers received privy purses, which were abolished by the 26th Amendment (1971).
The difficult cases
StateHow it joined India
JunagadhA Hindu-majority State in Kathiawar; its Nawab acceded to Pakistan (August 1947); people protested and Indian troops moved in; a plebiscite (February 1948) voted overwhelmingly for India.
Jammu and KashmirMaharaja Hari Singh hesitated; tribal raiders backed by Pakistan invaded (22 October 1947); he signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947; Indian troops were flown in; India took the matter to the UN (January 1948); a ceasefire (1949) left part of the State under Pakistan; Art 370 gave special status (made inoperative in 2019).
HyderabadThe largest State, ruled by Nizam Mir Osman Ali Khan, wanted to stay independent; signed a Standstill Agreement (November 1947); the Razakars of Qasim Razvi (Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen) terrorised people; the Telangana armed struggle (1946–51) by peasants and Communists fought the Nizam and the landlords; the Hyderabad State Congress ran a satyagraha. India launched 'Operation Polo' (police action), 13–17 September 1948; the Nizam surrendered on 17 September; Maj. Gen. J.N. Chaudhuri became military governor; the Nizam later served as Rajpramukh; Burgula Ramakrishna Rao became the first elected Chief Minister (1952).
Later additionsFrench Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe and Yanam (de facto 1954, de jure 1962); Portuguese Goa, Daman and Diu by 'Operation Vijay' (December 1961); Dadra and Nagar Haveli (freed 1954, merged 1961); Sikkim became the 22nd State by the 36th Amendment (1975).

3. Impact

AreaPoints
PoliticsA united India of former provinces and States; Patel called the 'Iron Man' and 'Bismarck of India'; Kashmir remained a lasting dispute.
SocietyMass migration and refugee resettlement (Punjab, Bengal); deep trauma; feudal rule in States ended, giving people democratic rights.
EconomyPartition split jute and cotton areas from mills, canals and railways; land reforms began in former States (e.g. after the Telangana struggle).
ReligionIndia chose to be a secular State despite partition on religious lines.

POLITY · Elections and electoral reforms; the anti-defection law

Understand it first

What is it? Free and fair elections are the base of democracy and part of the basic structure. The Constitution (Part XV, Arts 324–329) and two laws, the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951, set the rules; the Election Commission runs the process. Over the years, problems such as money power, muscle power, booth capturing and defections led to electoral reforms. The anti-defection law stops elected members from switching parties for gain. Learn it as the system → laws → reforms with years → committees → anti-defection law.

1. The electoral system

PointDetails
LawsRPA 1950: electoral rolls and allocation of seats; RPA 1951: conduct of elections, qualifications and disqualifications, corrupt practices, election disputes (election petitions go to the High Court). Art 329: courts cannot interfere during the election process.
Voting methodsFirst-past-the-post (the candidate with the most votes wins) for the Lok Sabha and Assemblies; proportional representation by single transferable vote for the Rajya Sabha, Legislative Councils, President and Vice-President.
Delimitation and reservationDelimitation Commissions (1952, 1963, 1973, 2002) redraw constituencies; the number of seats is frozen until the first census after 2026 (84th Amendment, 2001). Seats reserved for SCs and STs (Arts 330, 332); one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and Assemblies by the 106th Amendment (2023), to start after the next census and delimitation.

2. Major electoral reforms

ReformDetails
Voting age 1861st Amendment (1988), effective 1989: lowered from 21 to 18.
EVM and VVPATEVMs first used at Paravur, Kerala (1982); used in all constituencies since 2004; VVPAT (a paper slip the voter can see) first used in Nagaland (2013), at all booths since 2019.
Voter ID, rollsElectors' Photo Identity Card (EPIC) from 1993 (CEC T.N. Seshan); the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021: voluntary Aadhaar linking and four qualifying dates a year (1 January, 1 April, 1 July, 1 October); a Special Intensive Revision of rolls began in 2025.
CandidatesAffidavits on criminal cases, assets and education (ADR case, 2002); NOTA (PUCL case, 2013); contest from at most two constituencies (1996 amendment); expenditure limits (up to ₹95 lakh for the Lok Sabha in big States); Lily Thomas (2013): immediate disqualification on conviction.
Campaign rulesModel Code of Conduct (from the moment of announcement; not a law, enforced by the EC's authority); 48-hour silence period before polling (Sec 126); exit polls banned until the last phase ends (Sec 126A); home voting for voters 85+ and persons with disabilities.
Committees and proposalsTarkunde (1975), Dinesh Goswami (1990), Vohra (1993, criminal–political nexus), Indrajit Gupta (1998, State funding), Law Commission 170th (1999) and 255th (2015) Reports; One Nation, One Election: the Ram Nath Kovind Committee reported in March 2024; a Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill was introduced in December 2024 and sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee.

3. The anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule)

Tenth Schedule at a glance
  • Why: after 1967, frequent defections ('Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram', from a Haryana MLA who changed parties three times in a day) toppled governments. The 52nd Amendment (1985) added the Tenth Schedule.
  • Disqualification: a party member who voluntarily gives up membership, or votes or abstains against the party's direction (whip) without permission and is not condoned within 15 days; an independent member who joins a party; a nominated member who joins a party after six months. Exception: a merger supported by two-thirds of the legislature party (para 4); the earlier 'split' (one-third) exception was removed by the 91st Amendment (2003), which also bars defectors from ministerial or paid political posts until re-elected.
  • Who decides: the Speaker or Chairman (para 6). Kihoto Hollohan (1992): the decision is subject to judicial review; para 7 (barring courts) was struck down. Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020): Speakers should decide petitions within about three months. Subhash Desai (2023): the political party, not just the legislature party, appoints the whip. Criticism: Speakers' partisan delays, members cannot vote freely, and mass 'mergers'; proposals: let the President or Governor decide on the Election Commission's advice (Dinesh Goswami Committee, Law Commission).
Exam trap

SCIENCE · Energy: policy, capacity and demand; biofuels; Bharat Stage norms

Understand it first

What is it? Energy drives farms, factories, homes and transport. India is the world's third-largest energy consumer and third-largest electricity producer, and its demand keeps rising. The challenge is to provide enough, affordable and clean energy while cutting imports (India imports about 85% of its crude oil) and emissions. Two tools covered here: biofuels (fuels made from plants and waste, to replace some petrol and diesel) and Bharat Stage norms (limits on harmful gases from vehicles). Learn it as energy mix and capacity → targets and policies → biofuels → BS norms. Renewable sources and schemes come on Day 24.

1. Capacity, demand and targets

  • Installed power capacity is about 500 GW (2025); coal is still the largest source of generation (about 70% of electricity produced), but non-fossil sources (solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, biomass) reached about half of installed capacity in 2025, five years ahead of the 2030 NDC target. Peak demand touched about 250 GW (2024). All villages were electrified by April 2018 (DDUGJY) and nearly all households under Saubhagya (2017).
  • Panchamrit (COP26, Glasgow, 2021): 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030; 50% of energy needs from renewables by 2030; cut one billion tonnes of projected emissions by 2030; reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% (from 2005 levels) by 2030; net zero by 2070. Laws and bodies: Electricity Act 2003; Energy Conservation Act 2001 (Bureau of Energy Efficiency, 2002; 2022 amendment created a carbon credit trading scheme); PAT scheme for industries; UJALA (2015) LED bulbs; star labels on appliances; International Solar Alliance (2015).

2. Biofuels

Biofuel generations
ItemFacts
Why biofuelsCut crude oil imports and foreign-exchange outgo; give farmers extra income (sugarcane, surplus grain, crop residue); reduce stubble burning and emissions.
National Policy on Biofuels (2018, amended 2022)The Ethanol Blended Petrol programme (since 2003) aimed at 20% ethanol in petrol (E20); the 2022 amendment advanced E20 from 2030 to 2025–26, and India reached about 20% blending in 2025. Concerns: food vs fuel, water use by sugarcane, and older vehicle compatibility.
SchemesPM JI-VAN Yojana (2019): support for 2G (second-generation) ethanol plants (e.g. Panipat, 2022); SATAT (2018): compressed biogas plants; GOBAR-Dhan (2018): cattle dung and waste to biogas; RUCO: biodiesel from used cooking oil; Sustainable Aviation Fuel blending targets for flights from 2027; Global Biofuels Alliance, launched at the G20 Delhi summit (2023).

3. Bharat Stage (BS) emission norms

  • What: limits on carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter (PM) from vehicles, based on European (Euro) norms; notified by the Ministry of Road Transport and Highways under the Central Motor Vehicles Rules, with standards from the Central Pollution Control Board (Environment Ministry).
  • Timeline: BS-I (2000) → BS-II, BS-III → BS-IV nationwide (2017) → India skipped BS-V and moved straight to BS-VI from 1 April 2020 (the Supreme Court banned sale of BS-IV vehicles from that date). BS-VI fuel has at most 10 ppm sulphur (BS-IV allowed 50 ppm); diesel vehicles need devices such as diesel particulate filters (DPF) and selective catalytic reduction (SCR). BS-VI Phase 2 (April 2023) added Real Driving Emissions (RDE) testing and on-board diagnostics.
Exam trap
