HISTORY · Post-independence consolidation and reorganisation of States

Understand it first

What is it? After 1947, India had to turn a newly freed, divided and poor country into one stable democratic nation. This is called consolidation: making the Constitution, holding elections, starting planning, reforming land, settling the language question and choosing a foreign policy. A big part of it was redrawing the map of States. People wanted States based on the language they speak, so that government and schools work in their own language. Leaders first feared this would divide the nation, but the Andhra movement forced the issue, and in 1956 India was reorganised largely on linguistic lines. Learn it as consolidation tasks → early rejection → Andhra State → SRC and the 1956 Act → Andhra Pradesh → later States.

1. Consolidation: the first tasks

Foreign policyNon-alignment (not joining either Cold War bloc); Panchsheel with China (1954); Bandung (1955); NAM founded at Belgrade (1961).

2. Linguistic States: from refusal to reorganisation

From Andhra State to Andhra Pradesh
  • Background: the Congress had organised its own provincial committees by language since Nagpur (1920) and promised linguistic provinces. After Partition, leaders feared new divisions. The S.K. Dar Commission (1948) favoured administrative convenience over language, and the JVP Committee (1948–49) (Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya) also advised postponing it.
  • Andhra State: Potti Sriramulu fasted from 19 October 1952 for a Telugu State with Madras city; he died on 15 December 1952 after 58 days. Protests spread, and Nehru announced the State. Andhra State was formed on 1 October 1953, the first State formed on a linguistic basis, from the Telugu districts of Madras State; capital Kurnool, first Chief Minister T. Prakasam, High Court at Guntur.
  • States Reorganisation Commission (December 1953): chairman Fazl Ali, members K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru; report 30 September 1955. It accepted language as an important basis but rejected 'one language, one State', and also weighed unity, security, economic and administrative needs. It recommended ending the Part A, B, C, D classification of States.
  • States Reorganisation Act, 1956 (from 1 November 1956): 14 States and 6 Union Territories; the 7th Amendment (1956) ended the Part A–D classes; five Zonal Councils were set up to promote cooperation between States.
  • Andhra Pradesh (1 November 1956): the SRC had suggested keeping Telangana separate for a while, but Andhra State and the Telangana region of Hyderabad State were merged, with capital Hyderabad. The Gentlemen's Agreement (20 February 1956) promised Telangana safeguards (a Regional Council, Mulki rules for jobs). First Chief Minister Neelam Sanjiva Reddy. Later: Jai Telangana (1969) and Jai Andhra (1972) movements; the Six-Point Formula (1973) led to Art 371D (32nd Amendment) for local cadres in jobs and education.

POLITY · Centralisation vs decentralisation; the Community Development Programme

Understand it first

What is it? Centralisation means power is held mainly at the top (the Union); decentralisation means power is shared downwards with States and then with villages and towns. India's Constitution is federal with a strong Centre (sometimes called 'quasi-federal'), because the framers feared disunity after Partition. At the same time, Gandhi's dream was village self-rule (Gram Swaraj). The first big attempt to develop villages from below was the Community Development Programme (1952). Its weaknesses led to Panchayati Raj (Day 25). Learn it as meaning → centralising features → decentralising features → debate → rural experiments → CDP → why it fell short.

1. Centralising and decentralising features

Centralising (strong Centre)Decentralising (sharing power)
Parliament can change State boundaries (Art 3) without their consent; India is an 'indestructible Union of destructible States'.Three lists in the Seventh Schedule; States make laws on the State List (police, agriculture, health).
Union List is the longest; residuary powers with the Centre (Art 248); Union law prevails in a conflict on the Concurrent List (Art 254).Finance Commission (Art 280) shares central taxes with States; GST Council (Art 279A, 101st Amendment, 2016) where States hold two-thirds of the voting weight.
Parliament can legislate on State subjects (Art 249, Rajya Sabha resolution; Art 250, during Emergency; Art 252, at States' request; Art 253, treaties).Art 40 (DPSP): the State shall organise village panchayats; 73rd and 74th Amendments (1992) gave panchayats and municipalities constitutional status (29 subjects in the Eleventh Schedule, 18 in the Twelfth).
Emergency powers (Arts 352, 356, 360); Governor appointed by the Centre; single citizenship; all-India services; integrated judiciary; single Constitution.Inter-State Council (Art 263, set up 1990); NITI Aayog (1 January 2015) replaced the Planning Commission to promote cooperative federalism; S.R. Bommai (1994) limited misuse of Art 356.
  • Commissions on Centre–State relations: Rajamannar Committee (1969), set up by Tamil Nadu, sought more State autonomy; the Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) in Punjab; the Sarkaria Commission (1983–88) advised sparing use of Art 356 and set up the Inter-State Council; the Punchhi Commission (2007–10) gave further advice.
  • Gandhi vs Ambedkar: Gandhi wanted a decentralised India of self-reliant village republics. B.R. Ambedkar called the village 'a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism' and made the individual, not the village, the unit. The compromise: panchayats went into the Directive Principles (Art 40), not the binding part.

2. The Community Development Programme (CDP)

Rural development before and after 1952
PointDetails
Earlier experimentsSriniketan (Rabindranath Tagore, 1921, with Leonard Elmhirst); Gurgaon (F.L. Brayne, 1920s); Marthandam (Spencer Hatch, YMCA, Travancore); Baroda (V.T. Krishnamachari, 1932); Firka Development Scheme (Madras Presidency, 1946, under T. Prakasam); Etawah pilot project (Albert Mayer, 1948, UP); Nilokheri (refugee township, S.K. Dey).
Launch and aimLaunched on 2 October 1952 with 55 projects, partly with US (Ford Foundation) help; aim: all-round development of villages (agriculture, irrigation, health, education, roads, cottage industries) through people's participation and self-help.
StructureThe development block (about 100 villages) was the unit, headed by a Block Development Officer (BDO) with extension officers; a Village Level Worker (Gram Sevak) linked villages to the block. The National Extension Service (2 October 1953) spread it across India; a Ministry of Community Development came in 1956 (S.K. Dey).
Why it fell shortIt became a government (bureaucratic) programme; people did not participate; benefits went mostly to richer farmers; too many departments with poor coordination. The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) studied it and recommended democratic decentralisation, that is, Panchayati Raj.
Exam trap

SCIENCE · Non-renewable and renewable energy: sources, capacity, recent schemes

Understand it first

What is it? Non-renewable sources (coal, oil, natural gas, uranium) took millions of years to form and will run out; burning fossil fuels also causes pollution and climate change. Renewable sources (sun, wind, water, biomass, geothermal, tides) are naturally replaced and are cleaner, but sun and wind vary with weather and time, so they need storage. India still depends on coal, but is adding solar and wind faster than almost any other country. Learn it as each source: what, where in India, facts → AP's energy places → schemes with years.

India's energy sources

1. Non-renewable sources

SourceFacts
CoalGrades by carbon: anthracite (best, ~90%) > bituminous (most used) > lignite (brown coal) > peat. About 98% is Gondwana coal (Damodar valley: Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro; Talcher in Odisha; Korba in Chhattisgarh; Singareni in the Godavari valley, Telangana); tertiary coal in the north-east; lignite at Neyveli (Tamil Nadu). India is the second-largest coal producer; output crossed 1 billion tonnes (2024–25).
Oil and natural gasDigboi (Assam, 1889), India's oldest oil field; Mumbai High (offshore, the largest producer); Gujarat (Ankleshwar); Krishna–Godavari (KG) basin off the Andhra coast (Ravva, KG-D6 gas); Rajasthan (Barmer). India imports about 85% of its crude oil; Strategic Petroleum Reserves at Visakhapatnam, Mangaluru and Padur.
NuclearHomi J. Bhabha's three-stage programme: (1) natural uranium in pressurised heavy water reactors; (2) plutonium in fast breeder reactors (Kalpakkam); (3) thorium, because India has small uranium but large thorium reserves (monazite sands of Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Odisha and the Andhra coast). Plants: Tarapur (1969, first), Rawatbhata, Kalpakkam, Narora, Kakrapar, Kaiga, Kudankulam (Russian help, largest); Kovvada (Srikakulam, AP) is proposed. Capacity is about 8–9 GW.

2. Renewable sources

SourceFacts
SolarThe largest renewable source; capacity crossed 100 GW in 2025. Leading States: Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra. Big parks: Bhadla (Rajasthan), Pavagada (Karnataka), Kamuthi (TN); in AP, NP Kunta (Anantapur) and the Kurnool Ultra Mega Solar Park. Khavda (Gujarat) is being built as the world's largest renewable energy park (about 30 GW).
WindAbout 50 GW; leading States Gujarat, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka; Muppandal (TN) is a famous wind farm area; offshore wind potential off Gujarat and Tamil Nadu (a viability gap funding scheme, 2024). National Institute of Wind Energy at Chennai.
HydroLarge hydro (above 25 MW) is clean but affects forests and people; small hydro (up to 25 MW) is counted as renewable. Pumped storage stores surplus solar power by pumping water uphill and releasing it at peak hours (e.g. Pinnapuram, Nandyal district, AP). Examples: Srisailam, Nagarjuna Sagar, Polavaram (under construction), Tehri, Bhakra.
OthersGeothermal: Puga valley (Ladakh), Manikaran (HP); tidal: Gulf of Khambhat, Gulf of Kutch, Sundarbans; biomass and waste-to-energy; green hydrogen: hydrogen made by splitting water with renewable power.

3. Recent schemes

  • National Solar Mission (JNNSM, 2010), one of the eight missions of the National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008); target raised to 100 GW by 2022. Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI).
  • PM-KUSUM (2019): solar pumps and small solar plants for farmers, replacing diesel pumps and letting farmers sell surplus power.
  • PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana (February 2024): rooftop solar for 1 crore households, up to 300 units of free electricity a month, with subsidy.
  • National Green Hydrogen Mission (January 2023): produce 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen a year by 2030; SIGHT programme for electrolysers. Production-linked incentive (PLI) for solar modules and batteries. Green Energy Corridors to carry renewable power across States.
