GEOGRAPHY · The Earth in the Solar System

Understand it first

What is it? Geography starts with our home in space. The Earth is one of eight planets that go around the Sun; the Sun is one of billions of stars in our galaxy, the Milky Way. The Earth's shape, its spin (rotation) and its journey round the Sun (revolution), together with the tilt of its axis, explain day and night, seasons, time zones, the length of days and eclipses. Exams ask both facts (which planet is hottest?) and reasoning (why are there seasons? what time is it at a given longitude?). Learn it as universe → Sun → planets → small bodies → Earth's shape and motions → latitudes and longitudes → Moon and eclipses.

The Earth seen from Apollo 17 (1972), the famous 'Blue Marble' photo. Africa and Antarctica are visible. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Universe, galaxy and the Sun

  • Universe: began with the Big Bang about 13.8 billion years ago (Georges Lemaître; Edwin Hubble showed the universe is expanding). Galaxy: a huge group of stars, gas and dust; ours is the Milky Way (Akash Ganga), a spiral galaxy; our nearest big neighbour is Andromeda. Light year = distance light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion km (a unit of distance, not time). Astronomical Unit (AU) = average Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km.
  • Solar System: formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a spinning cloud of gas and dust (nebular hypothesis, Kant and Laplace). The Sun holds about 99.8% of its mass; it is a medium-sized star made mostly of hydrogen and helium; it shines by nuclear fusion (hydrogen → helium). Surface (photosphere) about 5,500°C, core about 15 million °C; outer layers chromosphere and corona (seen in a total solar eclipse). Sunspots are cooler, darker patches that follow an 11-year cycle. Sunlight reaches Earth in about 8 minutes 20 seconds. India studies the Sun with Aditya-L1 (Day 19).

3. The Earth: shape and motions

PointDetails
Shape and sizeNot a perfect sphere: slightly flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator, called a geoid (oblate spheroid). Equatorial diameter about 12,756 km, polar about 12,714 km; circumference about 40,075 km. Third planet from the Sun, about 150 million km away.

4. Latitudes, longitudes and time

  • Latitudes are imaginary circles parallel to the Equator (0°), measured north or south up to 90° at the poles. Key ones: Tropic of Cancer (23.5° N), Tropic of Capricorn (23.5° S), Arctic Circle (66.5° N), Antarctic Circle (66.5° S). The Tropic of Cancer passes through eight Indian States: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram. Heat zones: torrid (between the tropics), temperate, frigid.
  • Longitudes (meridians) run pole to pole; 0° is the Prime Meridian at Greenwich (London), up to 180° east and west. The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, so 15° = 1 hour and 1° = 4 minutes; places to the east are ahead in time. IST is based on 82.5° E (near Mirzapur, UP), so it is 5 h 30 min ahead of GMT; this meridian crosses UP, MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Andhra Pradesh. India spans about 30° of longitude, so the Sun rises about two hours earlier in Arunachal Pradesh than in Gujarat. The International Date Line roughly follows 180°; crossing it eastward you repeat a day, westward you skip a day.

5. The Moon and eclipses

  • The Moon is about 384,400 km away and about one-quarter of Earth's diameter. It goes round the Earth in about 27.3 days, but new moon to new moon takes about 29.5 days. It rotates once in the same time as it revolves, so we always see the same face. Its gravity (with the Sun's) causes tides: spring tides at new and full moon, neap tides at quarter moons.
  • Solar eclipse: the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth; only on a new moon day. Lunar eclipse: the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon; only on a full moon night. Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth's orbit. An annular solar eclipse leaves a ring of fire when the Moon is farther away.

POLITY · Balwant Rai Mehta and Ashok Mehta Committees; the 73rd Amendment (Panchayati Raj)

Understand it first

What is it? Panchayati Raj means rural local self-government: villagers elect their own bodies to plan and run local development. The Community Development Programme (1952) failed because people had no say (Day 24). So committees recommended democratic decentralisation. States set up panchayats, but they were often not elected on time, had little money and no power. The 73rd Amendment (1992) gave them constitutional status, making elections, reservation and finance compulsory. Learn it as Balwant Rai Mehta → Ashok Mehta → later committees → 73rd Amendment features → exemptions and PESA → AP's system.

The road to the 73rd Amendment

1. The committees

CommitteeRecommendations
Balwant Rai Mehta (appointed January 1957; report November 1957)Studied the CDP and NES; recommended 'democratic decentralisation' through a three-tier system: gram panchayat (village), panchayat samiti (block) and zila parishad (district). Village panchayat directly elected; samiti and zila parishad indirectly elected. The panchayat samiti to be the executive body; the zila parishad an advisory and supervisory body with the Collector as chairman; real powers and resources to be transferred. The National Development Council accepted it in January 1958. Rajasthan was first (Nagaur, 2 October 1959, by Nehru); Andhra Pradesh followed in 1959.
Ashok Mehta (appointed December 1977 by the Janata Government; report August 1978)Recommended a two-tier system: zila parishad (district) and mandal panchayat (a group of villages, about 15,000–20,000 people). The zila parishad to be the executive body and the main unit of planning below the State. Political parties may take part officially; compulsory taxation powers; reservation for SCs and STs; nyaya panchayats separate from development panchayats; a minister for Panchayati Raj in each State; social audit. Not implemented nationally because the Janata Government fell, but Karnataka, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh used its ideas (AP's mandals, 1985–86).
Later committeesG.V.K. Rao (1985): development had been bureaucratised, making Panchayati Raj like 'grass without roots'; the district should be the key unit. L.M. The 64th Amendment Bill (1989) passed the Lok Sabha but failed in the Rajya Sabha.

2. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

Passed in December 1992 (Narasimha Rao Government), it came into force on 24 April 1993, now celebrated as National Panchayati Raj Day. It added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243-O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects). It turned Art 40 (a Directive Principle) into a working reality. Some provisions are compulsory for States; others are left to the State's choice.

SCIENCE · Ecology and ecosystems

Understand it first

What is it? Ecology is the study of how living things relate to each other and to their surroundings. An ecosystem is a working unit where plants, animals and microbes (biotic) interact with air, water, soil, light and temperature (abiotic), for example a pond, a forest or the Kolleru lake. In every ecosystem, energy flows in one direction (from the Sun through plants to animals) and nutrients cycle round and round. Understanding this explains why tigers are few, why pesticides harm eagles and why lakes turn green. Learn it as terms → components → food chains and energy → pyramids → cycles → succession → interactions → key concepts.

1. Basic terms and components

  • The word 'ecology' was coined by Ernst Haeckel (1866); 'ecosystem' by A.G. Tansley (1935). Levels: individual → population (same species in an area) → community (all species in an area) → ecosystem (community + non-living surroundings) → biome (large region with similar climate and vegetation, e.g. tropical rainforest, desert) → biosphere (all life on Earth). Habitat is the address of an organism; niche is its 'profession', its full role (what it eats, when it is active, what eats it).
  • Biotic components: producers (autotrophs), mainly green plants and algae that make food by photosynthesis; consumers (heterotrophs): primary (herbivores), secondary and tertiary (carnivores), omnivores; decomposers (bacteria, fungi) that break dead matter into nutrients. Abiotic components: sunlight, temperature, water, soil, air and nutrients.

2. Energy flow, food chains and pyramids

Pyramid of energy and the 10% law
IdeaExplanation
Food chain and food webA food chain shows who eats whom (grass → deer → tiger). The grazing chain starts with living plants; the detritus chain starts with dead matter (important in forests and mangroves). Chains interlink to form a food web, which makes the ecosystem stable. Each step is a trophic level.
Energy flow and the 10% lawEnergy flow is one-way (unidirectional): it cannot go back to the Sun. Lindeman's 10% law (1942): only about 10% of energy passes to the next level; the rest is used in living or lost as heat. That is why food chains are short (3–5 levels) and top predators are few.
Ecological pyramidsPyramid of numbers: usually upright, but inverted in a tree ecosystem (one tree, many insects). Pyramid of biomass: upright on land, but inverted in the ocean (small mass of fast-growing phytoplankton supports a larger mass of fish). Pyramid of energy: always upright.
ProductivityGross primary productivity (GPP) is the total food made by plants; net (NPP) = GPP minus what plants use in respiration, the food available to consumers. Tropical rainforests, estuaries, coral reefs and swamps are the most productive; deserts and the open ocean (per area) the least.

3. Nutrient cycles and succession

  • Biogeochemical cycles move nutrients between living things and the environment. Carbon cycle: photosynthesis takes CO₂ in; respiration, decay and burning fossil fuels release it. Nitrogen cycle: fixation of air nitrogen by Rhizobium (in root nodules of legumes), Azotobacter and cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc) and by lightning; nitrification by Nitrosomonas (ammonia → nitrite) and Nitrobacter (nitrite → nitrate); denitrification by Pseudomonas returns N₂ to the air. Phosphorus cycle is sedimentary (no gas phase; source is rocks).
  • Ecological succession is the slow, orderly change of communities in an area until a stable climax community forms. Primary succession starts on bare rock or new land (lava, sand dunes) where there was no soil: lichens are the pioneers, then mosses, grasses, shrubs and trees. Secondary succession happens where life was removed but soil remains (after fire, flood, an abandoned field) and is faster. Succession in water is a hydrosere; on dry rock, a xerosere.

4. Interactions and key concepts

TermMeaning and example
Mutualism (+/+)Both gain: lichen (alga + fungus), mycorrhiza (fungus + plant roots), Rhizobium in legumes, bees and flowers.
Commensalism (+/0)One gains, the other is unaffected: an orchid growing on a mango branch; cattle egret and grazing cattle.
Parasitism and predation (+/−)Cuscuta (dodder) on a host plant; lice, tapeworm; a tiger hunting deer.
Competition (−/−) and amensalism (−/0)Species compete for the same resource; in amensalism one is harmed and the other is unaffected, e.g. Penicillium mould stopping bacteria.
Keystone and flagship speciesA keystone species has an effect far bigger than its numbers; removing it changes the whole ecosystem (e.g. tiger, fig trees). A flagship species is a popular animal used to win support for conservation (tiger, elephant).
Ecotone and edge effectAn ecotone is a transition zone between two ecosystems (e.g. mangroves between land and sea, a forest–grassland border); it often has more species (edge effect).
Biomagnification and eutrophicationBiomagnification: non-degradable poisons (DDT, mercury) become more concentrated at each higher level; fish-eating birds lay thin-shelled eggs. Eutrophication: nitrates and phosphates from fertilisers and sewage cause algal blooms; when algae die and decay, oxygen falls and fish die.

Ecosystems in Andhra Pradesh: Eastern Ghats forests (Araku and Paderu hills in the north, Nallamala and Seshachalam in the south); Kolleru (a large freshwater lake between the Godavari and Krishna deltas, a Ramsar site); Pulicat (India's second-largest brackish lagoon); mangroves at Coringa (Godavari) and the Krishna estuary; grasslands of Rollapadu (home of the Great Indian Bustard).

  1. A. Grasses
  2. B. Shrubs
  3. C. Lichens
  4. D. Large trees

Answer: C. In primary succession on bare rock, lichens are the pioneers; they break rock and start soil formation.
