GEOGRAPHY · Interior of the Earth: rocks, earthquakes and volcanoes

Understand it first

What is it? Nobody can travel to the centre of the Earth (the deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola borehole in Russia, is only about 12 km deep, while the centre is 6,371 km down). So scientists study the interior indirectly, mainly through earthquake (seismic) waves. The interior explains why the ground shakes, why volcanoes erupt, how mountains and oceans form and what rocks we stand on. Learn it as how we know → seismic waves → layers → rocks → moving plates → earthquakes → volcanoes.

1. How we know: sources and seismic waves

  • Direct sources: mines and deep drilling (only a few km), and lava from volcanoes. Indirect sources: seismic waves, rise of temperature and pressure with depth (about 1°C for every 32 m near the surface), gravity and magnetism differences, and meteorites (thought to be made of material like the Earth's interior).
  • Body waves: P (primary) waves are the fastest, travel like sound (push–pull) and pass through solids, liquids and gases. S (secondary) waves move the ground up and down (sideways) and pass only through solids. Surface waves (Love and Rayleigh) are the slowest but cause the most damage. Between about 105° and 145° from the epicentre lies a shadow zone with no direct P or S waves; beyond 105°, no S waves arrive at all, proving the outer core is liquid.
Seismic waves and the shadow zone

2. Layers of the Earth

Layers of the Earth
Cut-away view of the Earth's layers: crust, mantle, outer core and inner core. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
LayerFacts
CrustThin outer skin: about 5 km under oceans, about 30–35 km on average under continents and up to 70 km under big mountains. Continental crust is lighter, granitic, rich in silica and aluminium (SiAl); oceanic crust is heavier, basaltic, rich in silica and magnesium (SiMa). The Conrad discontinuity separates the upper and lower crust. The Mohorovičić (Moho) discontinuity (1909) separates crust and mantle.
MantleDown to about 2,900 km; about 83% of the Earth's volume. Its upper part includes the soft, partly molten asthenosphere (up to about 400 km), the main source of magma on which plates slide. The lithosphere = crust + rigid top of the mantle (about 100 km thick). The Gutenberg discontinuity at 2,900 km separates mantle and core.
CoreMade mainly of nickel and iron (NiFe). Outer core (2,900–5,150 km) is liquid; its movement creates the Earth's magnetic field. Inner core (5,150–6,371 km) is solid because of enormous pressure, about as hot as the Sun's surface (~5,500°C). The Lehmann discontinuity separates them (Inge Lehmann, 1936). Iron is the most common element in the whole Earth.
Crust compositionOxygen (~46.6%) > silicon (~27.7%) > aluminium (~8.1%) > iron (~5%) > calcium > sodium > potassium > magnesium. Aluminium is the most common metal in the crust.

3. Rocks

The three rock types
  • Igneous rocks ('fire rocks') form from cooled magma or lava, so they are called primary rocks; they have no fossils. Intrusive (plutonic) rocks cool slowly inside the Earth and have large crystals (granite); extrusive (volcanic) rocks cool quickly on the surface and have fine grains (basalt, as in the Deccan Traps, which weather into black cotton soil).
  • Sedimentary rocks form from layers of sediments pressed together; they cover about 75% of the land surface but only about 5% of the crust's volume; they contain fossils, coal and oil. Examples: sandstone, limestone, shale, conglomerate, coal. Metamorphic rocks form when heat and pressure change existing rocks: granite → gneiss, limestone → marble, sandstone → quartzite, shale → slate → schist, coal → graphite.
  • Andhra Pradesh: the Cuddapah basin has sedimentary rocks such as limestone (cement industry) and the barytes of Mangampet; the Eastern Ghats have metamorphic khondalites and charnockites; black granite is quarried at Chimakurthy (Prakasam); the Godavari valley has Gondwana sedimentary rocks.

4. Moving plates

  • Continental drift (Alfred Wegener, 1912): all continents were once one supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by one ocean, Panthalassa; evidence: the jigsaw fit of South America and Africa, matching fossils and rocks. Sea-floor spreading (Harry Hess, 1960s): new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges. These led to plate tectonics (1967): the lithosphere is broken into seven major plates and many minor ones that move a few cm a year; the Indian plate moves north at about 5 cm a year.
  • Plate boundaries: divergent (plates move apart; Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift); convergent (plates collide; one sinks under the other in subduction, forming deep trenches such as the Mariana Trench, volcanoes and fold mountains; India hitting Eurasia raised the Himalayas); transform (plates slide past each other; San Andreas Fault, California).
The major tectonic plates of the world. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

5. Earthquakes and volcanoes

TopicFacts
Earthquake basicsThe point inside the Earth where energy is released is the focus (hypocentre); the point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre (most damage). Recorded by a seismograph. Magnitude (energy) is measured on the Richter scale (1935), now the moment magnitude scale; each step is about 10 times bigger in shaking and about 32 times in energy. Intensity (damage seen) is measured on the Modified Mercalli scale (I–XII).
Causes and IndiaPlate movement along faults, volcanic activity, and human causes such as large reservoirs (Koyna, 1967) and mining. The BIS seismic zoning map (IS 1893) groups India into zones II to V; zone V (highest risk) covers the north-east, parts of J&K, Ladakh, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Bihar, the Rann of Kutch and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands (BIS has been revising this map; check the latest). Major quakes: Latur (1993), Bhuj (26 January 2001), Sikkim (2011). The Indian Ocean tsunami (26 December 2004) followed a magnitude 9.1 quake off Sumatra; India's Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS, Hyderabad started in 2007.
VolcanoesBy activity: active, dormant, extinct. By shape: shield volcanoes (runny basalt lava, gentle slopes; Hawaii); composite (strato) volcanoes (layers of lava and ash, explosive; Fuji, Vesuvius); calderas (huge collapsed craters); fissure eruptions that pour out flood basalts (Deccan Traps). About two-thirds or more of active volcanoes lie on the Pacific 'Ring of Fire'. India's only active volcano is Barren Island (Andaman Sea); Narcondam is dormant.
Intrusive formsMagma that cools inside the crust forms batholiths (huge granite masses), laccoliths (dome-shaped), lopoliths (saucer-shaped), sills (horizontal sheets) and dykes (vertical walls).

POLITY · The 74th Amendment (urban local government) and its implementation

Understand it first

What is it? Towns and cities are run by urban local bodies (ULBs), called municipalities in common speech. They supply water, drains, roads, street lights, waste collection, birth and death registration and more. Before 1992 many State Governments kept them under their thumb: elections were postponed for years and bodies were superseded. The 74th Amendment (1992) did for towns what the 73rd did for villages: it gave ULBs constitutional status, regular elections, reservation and a finance commission. Learn it as history → the Amendment's features → planning committees → Twelfth Schedule → other urban bodies → problems and schemes.

1. Background

  • The first municipal corporation in India was set up at Madras in 1688; Bombay and Calcutta followed in 1726. Lord Ripon's Resolution of 1882 is called the 'Magna Carta of local self-government', and Ripon the father of local self-government in India. Local government became a provincial (State) subject (Government of India Act 1919, dyarchy) and is in the State List (Entry 5) today.
  • The 65th Amendment Bill (1989) on municipalities failed in the Rajya Sabha. The 74th Amendment Act was passed in 1992 and came into force on 1 June 1993. It added Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects).

3. Other urban bodies and implementation

  • Other urban bodies: Cantonment Boards (for military areas, under the Union Ministry of Defence, Cantonments Act 2006); township administrations of public-sector industries; port trusts; notified area committees; and special-purpose agencies (development authorities, water boards), often called parastatals.
  • Problems: ULBs raise little own revenue (property tax is under-collected) and depend on grants; many Twelfth Schedule functions remain with State departments and parastatals; elections are often delayed (the Supreme Court in Suresh Mahajan (2022) said they cannot be postponed); Mayors are mostly indirectly elected with short terms and little power, while the commissioner (a State officer) runs the administration; DPCs and MPCs are weak or absent in many States; staff and planning capacity are low. The Second ARC (6th Report, 2007) suggested stronger Mayors and more devolution.
  • Schemes: JNNURM (2005); Swachh Bharat Mission–Urban (2014; 2.0 in 2021); Smart Cities Mission (2015, 100 cities; in AP: Visakhapatnam, Kakinada, Tirupati, Amaravati); AMRUT (2015; 2.0 in 2021) for water and sewerage; PMAY–Urban (2015; 2.0 in 2024); municipal bonds; Finance Commission grants tied to property tax reforms. AP: AP Municipalities Act, 1965 and AP Municipal Corporations Act, 1994; ward secretariats (2019) bring services closer to people.

SCIENCE · Biodiversity: hotspots, loss and conservation

Understand it first

What is it? Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life: all the different genes, species and ecosystems on Earth. It gives us food, medicines, clean water, pollination, fertile soil and a stable climate. But species are now disappearing far faster than normal, mostly because of humans. India is one of the world's richest countries in biodiversity, so the topic is asked often, along with international agreements and Indian laws. Learn it as meaning and levels → India's richness → hotspots → causes of loss → conservation methods → treaties and laws → AP's special species. (Protected areas, CITES and the IUCN Red List come on Day 27.)

1. Meaning, levels and India's richness

  • The term 'biodiversity' was popularised by E.O. Wilson (from a 1986 forum; the word was coined by Walter G. Rosen). Three levels: genetic diversity (variation within a species, e.g. thousands of rice varieties), species diversity (number and variety of species) and ecosystem diversity (forests, grasslands, wetlands, deserts, coral reefs). Alpha diversity is within one community, beta between communities, and gamma across a whole region.
  • India is one of the 17 megadiverse countries: with only about 2.4% of the world's land, it has about 7–8% of recorded species, including over 45,000 plant species and over 1 lakh animal species. It has 10 biogeographic zones (from the trans-Himalaya to the islands), and is a centre of origin of crops like rice, sugarcane, mango and pepper.

2. Biodiversity hotspots

India's four biodiversity hotspots
Map of the world's biodiversity hotspots. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • The idea was given by Norman Myers (1988) and is used by Conservation International. A region is a hotspot if it has at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species (found nowhere else) and has lost at least 70% of its original natural vegetation. There are 36 hotspots worldwide; they cover only about 2.5% of the land but hold over half of the world's endemic plant species.
  • India has parts of four: the Himalaya, Indo-Burma (north-east), the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands). Exam trap: the Eastern Ghats are not a hotspot, although they have many endemic species.

3. Loss of biodiversity

  • The IPBES report (2019) warned that about one million species face extinction. Main causes are remembered as 'HIPPO': Habitat loss and fragmentation (the biggest cause), Invasive alien species, Pollution, human Population growth and Overexploitation (over-hunting, over-fishing, illegal trade), plus climate change. When one species dies out, others that depend on it may also be lost (co-extinction).
  • Invasive species in India: Lantana, Parthenium ('congress grass'), water hyacinth (Eichhornia) choking lakes, Prosopis juliflora (sarkaru tumma) and the African catfish in ponds. Lost species: the cheetah was declared extinct in India in 1952; Project Cheetah brought cheetahs to Kuno (MP) in September 2022.

4. Conservation, treaties and laws
