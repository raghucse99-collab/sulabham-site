GEOGRAPHY · Major landforms and their features

Understand it first

What is it? Landforms are the natural shapes of the Earth's surface: mountains, plateaus, plains, valleys, dunes, caves and so on. Two kinds of forces make them. Endogenic (internal) forces from inside the Earth build up land (folding, faulting, volcanoes). Exogenic (external) forces like running water, wind, glaciers, waves and groundwater wear it down and deposit material elsewhere. Exams ask you to match landforms with the agent that makes them and to give Indian examples. Learn it as forces → weathering → mountains, plateaus, plains → landforms made by each agent → AP examples.

1. Forces and weathering

  • Endogenic forces: diastrophism, which includes orogenic movements (mountain building by folding and faulting) and epeirogenic movements (slow uplift or sinking of large areas), plus volcanism and earthquakes. Exogenic forces: weathering, mass movement (landslides) and erosion and deposition by agents; together they level the land (denudation, gradation).
  • Weathering is the breaking of rocks in place (no movement). Physical weathering: heating and cooling make rock layers peel off (exfoliation, 'onion weathering', common in granite hills); water freezing in cracks (frost wedging). Chemical weathering: oxidation (rusting, red soils), carbonation (rainwater with CO₂ dissolves limestone), hydration. Biological weathering: roots and burrowing animals.

2. Mountains, plateaus and plains

LandformTypes and examples
MountainsFold mountains, formed when plates push rocks into folds: young ones like the Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies; old ones worn down like the Aravallis and Appalachians. Block (fault) mountains: a block raised between faults is a horst (Vosges, Black Forest; the Satpura is often cited); a block that sinks forms a rift valley (graben) (Narmada and Tapi valleys, the Rhine valley, the Great Rift Valley). Volcanic mountains (Fuji, Kilimanjaro). Residual mountains, the worn remains of old ranges (Aravallis, Eastern Ghats). Mount Everest is 8,849 m (2020 survey); the highest peak of the Eastern Ghats is Arma Konda (about 1,680 m, AP); the highest in south India is Anamudi (2,695 m).
PlateausFlat-topped highlands. Intermontane (surrounded by mountains; Tibet, the highest, 'roof of the world'); piedmont (at the foot of mountains; Patagonia); continental (large raised areas; the Deccan Plateau, built of lava in its north-west). The Chota Nagpur plateau is India's mineral heartland. In AP, the Rayalaseema uplands are part of the Deccan Plateau.
PlainsLow, flat land, mostly made by river deposits (alluvial plains such as the Indo-Gangetic plain); coastal plains (the east coast is wider, with deltas); peneplains are almost flat surfaces left after long erosion. Plains support dense population and farming.

3. Landforms made by rivers

A river's life
Meanders and oxbow lakes of a river in its old stage (Red River, USA). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Upper course (youth): the river cuts down fast, making V-shaped valleys, deep gorges and canyons (the Grand Canyon), waterfalls (Jog Falls on the Sharavati), rapids and potholes. Middle course (maturity): the river cuts sideways and swings in loops called meanders across a flood plain. Lower course (old age): a meander is cut off to form an oxbow lake; floods build raised banks called natural levees; the river splits into distributaries.
  • Delta vs estuary: a delta is a triangle of silt at the mouth, formed where a slow river drops its load into a calm sea (Ganga–Brahmaputra, the world's largest; Krishna, Godavari, Mahanadi, Kaveri). An estuary is a funnel-shaped mouth where tides wash the silt away (west-flowing Narmada and Tapi). AP waterfalls: Talakona (Seshachalam hills; the highest in AP), Ethipothala (on the Chandravanka, near Nagarjuna Sagar) and Katiki (Araku valley).

4. Landforms made by wind, glaciers, waves and groundwater

Other agents and their landforms
AgentErosional and depositional landforms
Wind (deserts)Sand-laden wind cuts the base of rocks into mushroom (pedestal) rocks; isolated hills are inselbergs; ridges are yardangs and zeugens; deflation hollows form where sand is blown away. Deposits: barchans (crescent-shaped dunes), seif (long, linear dunes) and fine dust called loess (north China). The Thar has many shifting dunes.
GlaciersMoving ice makes U-shaped valleys, armchair-shaped cirques, sharp arêtes and pyramid peaks called horns (the Matterhorn), hanging valleys and, where the sea floods a glacial valley, fjords (Norway). Deposits: moraines (heaps of rock debris), eskers (winding ridges), drumlins and outwash plains.
Sea wavesErosion: cliffs, sea caves, arches and stacks. Deposition: beaches, spits and bars; a bar can enclose a lagoon (Chilika, Odisha, India's largest brackish lagoon; Pulicat, AP–TN, the second largest; Vembanad, Kerala). A tombolo joins an island to the land. AP's coast (about 974 km, second longest after Gujarat) has beaches such as RK Beach, Suryalanka and Mypadu.
Groundwater (karst)In limestone, water dissolves rock to form sinkholes, underground caves, stalactites (hanging from the ceiling: remember 'c') and stalagmites (rising from the ground: 'g'); when they meet they form pillars. Examples: Borra Caves (Ananthagiri hills, Alluri Sitharama Raju district; found by William King, 1807) and Belum Caves (Nandyal district; among the longest in India); India's longest cave is Krem Liat Prah (Meghalaya).
Exam trap

ECONOMY · National income: concept and measurement

Understand it first

What is it? National income is the total value of all goods and services a country produces in a year, counted in money. It tells us how big the economy is, how fast it is growing and, divided by population, how rich people are on average (per capita income). Governments use it to plan budgets; news reports on 'GDP growth' every quarter. The terms (GDP, GNP, NNP, factor cost) confuse many students, but they follow a simple chain of add or subtract one item. Learn it as basic terms → the GDP-to-NI chain → prices (market, factor, basic; nominal, real) → three methods → what is counted → India's history → limits and alternatives.

1. Basic terms and the chain

From GDP to National Income
TermMeaning
GDP (Gross Domestic Product)Market value of all final goods and services produced within the country's borders in a year, by citizens or foreigners. Final goods are bought for final use; intermediate goods (raw materials used up in production) are not counted separately to avoid double counting.
GNP (Gross National Product)GDP + net factor income from abroad (NFIA). NFIA = what Indians earn abroad (wages, interest, profits) minus what foreigners earn in India. GNP is about nationals, GDP about territory. For India NFIA is usually negative, so GNP is slightly less than GDP.
NDP and NNPNet = Gross − depreciation (consumption of fixed capital, the wear and tear of machines and buildings). NDP = GDP − depreciation; NNP = GNP − depreciation.
Market price vs factor costFactor cost is what producers actually receive for land, labour, capital and enterprise. Factor cost = market price − indirect taxes + subsidies (that is, minus net indirect taxes). National Income = NNP at factor cost. Since 2015, India reports GVA at basic prices (which includes production taxes but not product taxes like GST); GDP = GVA at basic prices + product taxes − product subsidies.
Nominal vs real; per capitaNominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP uses constant (base-year) prices, so it shows real growth without inflation. GDP deflator = (nominal GDP ÷ real GDP) × 100, a broad measure of inflation. Per capita income = national income ÷ population. Personal income adds transfer payments; disposable income = personal income − direct taxes.

2. Three methods of measurement

MethodHow it works
Product (value-added) methodAdds the value added at each stage of production in every sector (output minus intermediate inputs). Example: a farmer sells wheat for ₹50, the miller sells flour for ₹70, the baker sells bread for ₹100: value added is 50 + 20 + 30 = ₹100, equal to the final good's price.
Income methodAdds incomes earned by factors: compensation of employees (wages) + rent + interest + profit + mixed income of the self-employed.
Expenditure methodAdds spending on final goods: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M): private consumption, investment, government spending and net exports.
  • Included: imputed rent of owner-occupied houses, own-account production (farm produce a farmer keeps), the commission on sale of second-hand goods. Excluded: transfer payments (old-age pensions, scholarships, unemployment allowance), sale of second-hand goods (already counted when new), purely financial transactions (buying shares), illegal activities, and unpaid household work by family members.
  • India's history: Dadabhai Naoroji made the first estimate (1867–68, about ₹20 per head) in 'Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'; later William Digby and Findlay Shirras; V.K.R.V. Rao made the first scientific estimate (1931–32). The National Income Committee (1949) was chaired by P.C. Mahalanobis (members D.R. Gadgil, V.K.R.V. Rao). The Central Statistical Organisation (CSO, 1951) prepared estimates; in 2019 the CSO and NSSO merged into the National Statistical Office (NSO) under MoSPI. India uses mainly the production and income methods. The base year has been 2011–12; MoSPI is moving the series to a new base year, 2022–23 (check the latest release).
  • Limits of GDP and alternatives: GDP does not show inequality, environmental damage, leisure, unpaid work or the informal sector well, and it counts harmful spending as growth. Alternatives: Human Development Index (UNDP, 1990; Mahbub ul Haq, with Amartya Sen): health (life expectancy), education (schooling) and income (GNI per capita, PPP); India ranked about 130th of 193 in the 2025 report; Green GDP (subtracting environmental loss); Gross National Happiness (Bhutan); the Multidimensional Poverty Index. AP: the State's Socio-Economic Survey reports GSDP, sector shares and per capita income every year.
Exam trap

SCIENCE · Wildlife conservation: IUCN, CITES, endangered species, biosphere reserves

Understand it first

What is it? Yesterday we saw why biodiversity is being lost. Today is how the world and India protect wild animals and plants: by grading how endangered each species is (IUCN Red List), controlling international trade in wildlife (CITES), strict laws (Wildlife Protection Act) and protected areas such as national parks, tiger reserves and biosphere reserves. Learn it as IUCN categories → CITES and other conventions → Indian laws → protected areas and projects → biosphere reserves → AP's protected areas.

1. The IUCN Red List

IUCN Red List categories
  • The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) was founded in 1948 (headquarters Gland, Switzerland); its Red List (since 1964) is the world's main record of species' risk. 'Threatened' means Critically Endangered, Endangered or Vulnerable. It is a scientific list, not a law.
  • Indian examples: Critically Endangered: Great Indian Bustard, gharial, Jerdon's courser, white-rumped vulture; Endangered: Bengal tiger, Asian elephant, Ganges river dolphin, red panda; Vulnerable: snow leopard, one-horned rhinoceros. The Great Indian Bustard is threatened mainly by collisions with overhead power lines in Rajasthan and Gujarat.
The Great Indian Bustard, a Critically Endangered bird (also seen at Rollapadu, AP). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

2. CITES and other conventions

ConventionFacts
CITES (Washington Convention)Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora; signed 3 March 1973 (now World Wildlife Day), in force 1975; India joined in 1976; secretariat in Geneva (under UNEP). It is legally binding on members but works through national laws. Appendix I: species threatened with extinction; commercial trade banned (tiger, Asiatic lion, most rhinos). Appendix II: trade allowed only with permits (e.g. red sanders, many corals). Appendix III: species protected in one country that asks others to help control trade. CoP20 was held at Samarkand, Uzbekistan (2025).
CMS (Bonn Convention)Conservation of Migratory Species (1979); India hosted CoP13 at Gandhinagar (2020), mascot 'Gibi' the Great Indian Bustard.
Ramsar ConventionFor wetlands (Ramsar, Iran, 1971); India joined in 1982 (first sites Chilika and Keoladeo) and has over 90 Ramsar sites; AP's site is Kolleru. 2 February is World Wetlands Day.
Other bodiesWWF (1961); TRAFFIC (monitors wildlife trade); International Whaling Commission (1946); Global Tiger Forum; the International Big Cat Alliance (2023), launched by India.

3. Indian laws, protected areas and projects

  • Constitution: the 42nd Amendment (1976) moved forests and protection of wild animals and birds from the State List to the Concurrent List, and added Art 48A (State to protect environment and wildlife) and Art 51A(g) (citizens' duty to protect nature and have compassion for living creatures).
  • Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972: bans hunting of listed animals; set up National Parks and Wildlife Sanctuaries; the 2002 amendment (in force 2003) added Conservation Reserves and Community Reserves; the 2006 amendment created the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB); the 2022 amendment cut the schedules from six to four (Schedule I highest protection, II lesser, III plants, IV CITES specimens) and gave effect to CITES. The National Board for Wildlife is chaired by the Prime Minister. A national park allows no human activity such as grazing; a sanctuary may allow some.
  • Projects: Project Tiger (1 April 1973, started with 9 reserves; now about 58; the 2022 census estimated about 3,682 tigers, around three-quarters of the world's wild tigers); Project Elephant (1992); Project Dolphin (2020) (the Gangetic dolphin is the national aquatic animal); Project Cheetah (2022, Kuno); vulture conservation (veterinary diclofenac banned, 2006). India's largest tiger reserve is Nagarjunasagar–Srisailam in AP.

4. Biosphere reserves

Zones of a biosphere reserve
  • Biosphere reserves come from UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere (MAB) Programme (1971). They protect whole ecosystems together with the people who live in them, using three zones: a strictly protected core, a buffer for research and education, and a transition zone with villages and sustainable use.
  • India has 18 biosphere reserves, declared by the Union Government. The first was Nilgiri (1986); the largest is the Great Rann of Kutch. 13 are in UNESCO's World Network: Panna joined in 2020, and Cold Desert (Himachal Pradesh) in 2025 (check the latest). AP has the Seshachalam Biosphere Reserve (2010), home of red sanders, which is not yet in the UNESCO network.

AP's protected areas (all regions): Nagarjunasagar–Srisailam Tiger Reserve (Nallamala); Papikonda National Park (Godavari gorge); Sri Venkateswara National Park (Seshachalam); Coringa and Krishna wildlife sanctuaries (mangroves); Kolleru (wetland birds); Pulicat (Nelapattu) bird sanctuary (pelicans, flamingos); Rollapadu (Great Indian Bustard); Sri Lankamalleswara (Jerdon's courser); Kambalakonda (Visakhapatnam); Telineelapuram and Telukunchi (Srikakulam; pelicans and painted storks).
