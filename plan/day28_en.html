GEOGRAPHY · Atmosphere: structure and composition; heat and pressure

Understand it first

What is it? The atmosphere is the blanket of air around the Earth, held by gravity. It gives us oxygen to breathe, blocks harmful ultraviolet rays, keeps the planet warm enough for life (greenhouse effect) and is where all weather happens. The Sun heats the Earth unevenly, and differences in heat create differences in air pressure, which make winds blow (Day 29). Learn it as gases → layers → how the Earth is heated → heat budget → temperature changes with height → pressure and pressure belts.

The thin layers of the atmosphere seen from space at sunset. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Composition

  • Dry air by volume: nitrogen ~78%, oxygen ~21%, argon ~0.93% (the most common inert gas), carbon dioxide ~0.04% (over 420 ppm and rising); traces of neon, helium, ozone, hydrogen. Most of the air (about 99%) lies within about 32 km of the surface.
  • Water vapour varies from almost 0 to 4% (most in hot, humid tropics); it absorbs heat and forms clouds and rain. Dust particles act as hygroscopic nuclei around which water condenses. Ozone (mainly at 15–35 km) absorbs the Sun's ultraviolet rays. CO₂ and water vapour let sunlight in but trap outgoing heat (greenhouse effect).

2. Layers of the atmosphere

Layers of the atmosphere and temperature
LayerFeatures
TroposphereLowest layer: about 8 km at the poles and 18 km at the equator (thicker where strong heating pushes air up). Holds most of the air, water vapour and dust, so all weather (clouds, rain, storms) happens here. Temperature falls with height at about 6.5°C per km (normal lapse rate). Its top is the tropopause.
StratosphereUp to about 50 km. Contains the ozone layer; because ozone absorbs UV, temperature rises with height. Almost no clouds or weather and little turbulence, so jet aircraft like to fly in its lower part. Rare nacreous (mother-of-pearl) clouds form near the poles.
MesosphereAbout 50–80/85 km. Temperature falls again to about −90°C, the coldest part of the atmosphere. Meteors burn up here.
Thermosphere and ionosphereAbove about 85 km; very thin air, but temperature rises sharply as gases absorb solar energy. The ionosphere (about 80–400 km) has electrically charged particles (ions) that reflect radio waves back to Earth (long-distance radio), and is where auroras glow near the poles. The International Space Station orbits here (~400 km).
ExosphereThe outermost layer, slowly merging into space; very light gases (hydrogen, helium).

3. How the Earth is heated

  • Insolation (incoming solar radiation) arrives as short waves. It is greatest where the Sun's rays are vertical (tropics) and least where they are slanting (poles); it also depends on length of day, cloud cover and dust. Albedo is the share of sunlight reflected: Earth's average is about 30%; fresh snow reflects up to 80–90%, forests and oceans much less.
  • Heat budget: of 100 units of sunlight reaching the top of the atmosphere, about 35 are reflected back (albedo), about 14 are absorbed by the atmosphere and about 51 by the Earth's surface. The Earth sends all this back as long-wave terrestrial radiation, so its average temperature stays steady. Because the atmosphere is heated mainly from below by the ground, it is warmest near the surface.
  • Ways air is heated: conduction (heat passing by contact with warm ground), convection (warm air rises vertically, forming clouds), advection (horizontal movement of heat by wind, e.g. the hot 'loo' winds of north India), and radiation. Temperature inversion: sometimes air near the ground is colder than the air above, e.g. in valleys on clear, calm winter nights when cold, heavy air drains down slopes; it traps fog and smog (as in Delhi's winters). Isotherms join places of equal temperature; the thermal equator (the belt of highest temperature) lies a little north of the equator because the north has more land.

4. Air pressure and pressure belts

  • Air pressure is the weight of the air column above a place. At mean sea level it is about 1013.25 millibars (hPa). It falls with height (about 1 mb for every 10 m near the ground), which is why mountaineers feel breathless and food takes longer to cook. It is measured with a barometer (mercury or aneroid); a barograph records it. Lines joining places of equal pressure are isobars. Warm air rises → low pressure; cool, sinking air → high pressure. Winds blow from high to low pressure.
  • Seven pressure belts: the Equatorial Low (0°–5°; calm 'doldrums', the ITCZ, caused by heat); two Sub-tropical Highs (about 30°; calm 'horse latitudes', caused by sinking air; most hot deserts lie here); two Sub-polar Lows (about 60°, caused by the Earth's rotation); and two Polar Highs (caused by cold). The belts shift north in June and south in December with the overhead Sun, which helps explain India's monsoon.

ECONOMY · Occupational pattern and sectoral distribution of income

Understand it first

What is it? The occupational pattern shows what work people do: how many are in farming, in industry or in services. The sectoral distribution of income shows how much each sector contributes to national income (GVA). In rich countries most people work in services; in India, nearly half of workers are still in agriculture, but agriculture produces less than a fifth of output. This gap explains low rural incomes, disguised unemployment and the need for jobs in industry and services. Learn it as three sectors → theories → India's data and trends → labour-force terms → types of unemployment → informal sector and reforms.

1. Sectors and theories

SectorActivities
PrimaryUsing natural resources directly: agriculture, animal husbandry, forestry, fishing, mining and quarrying.
Secondary (industry)Turning raw materials into goods: manufacturing, construction, and electricity, gas and water supply.
Tertiary (services)Trade, hotels, transport, communication, banking, insurance, real estate, IT, public administration, education, health. Some writers call knowledge work (research, IT) quaternary.
  • Clark–Fisher hypothesis (Colin Clark and Allan Fisher, 1930s–40s): as an economy develops, workers and income shift from primary to secondary and then to tertiary activities. Arthur Lewis's dual-economy model (1954): a backward farm sector with surplus labour supplies workers to a modern industrial sector at low wages until the surplus is used up. India's path is different: output moved straight from agriculture to services ('service-led growth'), while manufacturing stayed small (about 17% of GVA) and too few workers left farming.

2. India: output vs jobs

Share in output vs share in jobs
  • Output (GVA, approx. 2023–24): agriculture and allied ~18%, industry ~28% (manufacturing ~17%), services ~54%. In the 1950s agriculture gave over half of national income. Jobs (PLFS 2023–24): agriculture ~46%, industry ~25% (construction ~12%, manufacturing ~11%), services ~29%. Agriculture's share of jobs fell from about 70% in 1951 to about 42% in 2019–20, then rose after COVID as workers returned to villages.
  • What it means: output per worker in farming is very low, so many farm families are poor; much farm work hides disguised unemployment. Construction has become the main exit from farming for unskilled workers. AP has a larger farm-and-fisheries share of GSVA than India as a whole (strong in aquaculture, horticulture and rice), with services next.

3. Measuring work and unemployment

TermMeaning
PLFSThe Periodic Labour Force Survey by the NSO (since 2017) gives annual and quarterly figures; from 2025 it also gives monthly estimates. (It replaced the NSSO's five-yearly surveys.)
LFPR, WPR, URLabour Force Participation Rate = (employed + unemployed seeking work) ÷ population; Worker Population Ratio = employed ÷ population; Unemployment Rate = unemployed ÷ labour force. For ages 15+ in 2023–24 (usual status): LFPR about 60%, female LFPR about 42%, UR about 3%.
Usual status vs CWSUsual status looks at the main activity over the last 365 days (principal + subsidiary); Current Weekly Status (CWS) looks at the last 7 days, so it catches short-term unemployment.
Types of unemploymentDisguised (more people on a farm than needed; removing some does not reduce output; marginal productivity zero); seasonal (idle between seasons); structural (skills do not match jobs as the economy changes); frictional (short gap while changing jobs); cyclical (during recessions); educated unemployment.
  • Informal work: about 90% of India's workers are in the informal sector, with no written contract, paid leave or social security. The e-Shram portal (August 2021) registers unorganised workers (over 30 crore). The four Labour Codes (Wages 2019; Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions 2020) merged 29 central labour laws and were brought into force in November 2025.
  • Demographic dividend: about two-thirds of Indians are of working age (15–64) and the median age is under 30. This is a growth chance only if people get skills, health and productive jobs; low female participation is a big gap.

SCIENCE · Indian wildlife and forest laws, projects and recent initiatives

Understand it first

What is it? Day 27 covered the Wildlife Protection Act, the IUCN list, CITES and biosphere reserves. Today completes the picture: the other laws that protect forests and forest people, the institutions that study and guard wildlife, the species projects in more detail, and the new schemes since 2020 that exams love to ask about. Learn it as laws in time order → institutions → species projects → recent initiatives → forest cover → AP.

1. Laws in time order

LawKey points
Indian Forest Act, 1927Colonial law classifying forests as reserved, protected and village forests; the 2017 amendment said bamboo grown outside forests is not a 'tree', so farmers can cut and sell it freely.
Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960Punishes cruelty to animals; set up the Animal Welfare Board of India.
Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972Hunting ban, protected areas, NTCA, WCCB, four schedules (see Day 27).
Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980Forest land cannot be diverted for non-forest use (mining, dams, roads) without the Union Government's prior approval. In T.N. Godavarman (1996) the Supreme Court said 'forest' means any land that is forest in the dictionary sense, whatever its record. The 2023 amendment renamed it the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, limited it mainly to recorded forests, and exempted some strategic projects near borders; the Supreme Court has asked that the broad meaning of 'forest' be kept while cases are heard.
Environment (Protection) Act, 1986An umbrella law passed after the Bhopal gas tragedy (1984); the basis for Environmental Impact Assessment and coastal rules.
Forest Rights Act, 2006Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act: recognises the rights of forest-dwelling STs and other traditional forest dwellers (who must show 75 years / three generations of residence) who lived there before 13 December 2005. Individual rights to land under cultivation (up to 4 hectares) and community forest resource rights. The Gram Sabha starts the process. The nodal ministry is Tribal Affairs.
Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016When forest land is diverted, the user pays for compensatory afforestation; the money goes to CAMPA funds (90% to States, 10% to the Centre).

2. Institutions

Botanical Survey of India (1890) and Zoological Survey of India (1916), both in Kolkata; Wildlife Institute of India (1982, Dehradun); Salim Ali Centre for Ornithology and Natural History (SACON, Coimbatore); National Green Tribunal (2010) for environmental cases; NTCA and WCCB (Day 27). Wildlife Week is held in the first week of October.
