GEOGRAPHY · Winds, rainfall and climate types

Understand it first

What is it? Wind is air moving from high pressure to low pressure (Day 28). Winds carry heat and moisture around the world, so they decide where it rains and what the climate is like. Weather is the condition of the air on a given day; climate is the average weather over about 30 years. India's farming depends on the monsoon winds, and AP faces cyclones almost every year, so this topic is asked often. Learn it as forces on wind → planetary winds → periodic and local winds → jet streams and cyclones → humidity and clouds → types of rainfall → climate types (Köppen) → AP.

1. Forces and planetary winds

  • Three forces shape wind: the pressure gradient force (the bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the wind; closely packed isobars mean strong wind); the Coriolis force from the Earth's rotation, which turns winds to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern (Ferrel's law); it is zero at the equator and greatest at the poles, which is why cyclones do not form right on the equator; and friction with the ground, which slows surface winds. High up, winds blow parallel to isobars (geostrophic winds).
  • Planetary (permanent) winds: the trade winds blow from the sub-tropical highs to the equatorial low: north-east trades in the north, south-east trades in the south; steady, they helped old sailing ships trade. The westerlies blow from the sub-tropical highs towards the sub-polar lows (30°–60°); they are strongest in the Southern Hemisphere, where there is little land ('roaring forties', 'furious fifties', 'shrieking sixties'). Polar easterlies blow from the polar highs. Air circulates in three cells: Hadley (tropics), Ferrel (mid-latitudes) and Polar.
Planetary winds

2. Periodic and local winds

  • Periodic winds change with time. Monsoons reverse direction with the season (south-west in summer, north-east in winter over India). Land and sea breezes: by day the land heats faster, so a cool sea breeze blows onto the coast; at night a land breeze blows out to sea. Mountain and valley winds: by day warm air climbs the slopes (valley breeze, anabatic); at night cold air slides down (mountain breeze, katabatic).

  • Jet streams are fast winds high in the troposphere. The sub-tropical westerly jet lies south of the Himalayas in winter; when it jumps north in early summer, the monsoon can burst over India. The tropical easterly jet blows over the peninsula in summer.
  • Cyclones are low-pressure systems with winds spiralling inwards: anticlockwise in the north, clockwise in the south. Tropical cyclones form over warm seas (above ~27°C), have a calm eye, and hit India's east coast mostly in May and October–December; they are called hurricanes (Atlantic) and typhoons (west Pacific). IMD (RSMC, New Delhi) tracks them; names come from a list of 13 countries (WMO/ESCAP panel). AP examples: Hudhud (2014, Visakhapatnam), Titli (2018, Srikakulam), Michaung (2023, Bapatla). An anticyclone is a high-pressure system with calm, clear weather.

3. Humidity, clouds and rainfall

  • Humidity is water vapour in the air. Relative humidity = actual vapour ÷ what the air can hold at that temperature × 100; measured with a hygrometer (wet-and-dry bulb). When air cools to its dew point, vapour condenses into dew, frost, fog, mist or clouds. Clouds: cirrus (high, feathery), cumulus (cotton heaps, fair weather), stratus (grey layers), nimbus (rain clouds); tall cumulonimbus give thunder, lightning and hail.
Three types of rainfall

Orographic rain explains India's pattern: the windward side of the Western Ghats (Konkan, Malabar) gets very heavy rain, while the leeward rain-shadow side (interior Karnataka, Marathwada and Rayalaseema) stays dry. Mawsynram and Cherrapunji on the windward Khasi hills are among the wettest places on Earth, while nearby Shillong is in the rain shadow. Anantapur is one of India's driest districts.

ECONOMY · Economic growth and economic development

Understand it first

What is it? Economic growth is a rise in the output of an economy, usually real GDP or per capita income: it is about 'how much'. Economic development is a broader improvement in people's lives: less poverty and inequality, better health, education, housing, more freedom and choices, and a shift to better jobs: it is about 'how well'. A country can grow fast but develop slowly if the gains go to a few. Learn it as meanings and differences → measures of development (with India's figures) → theories of growth → Indian ideas and goals.

1. Growth vs development

PointEconomic growthEconomic development
NatureQuantitative; narrowQualitative and quantitative; broad
Measured byReal GDP, GNP, per capita incomeHDI, poverty, MPI, literacy, life expectancy, inequality, SDG Index
TimeCan happen in the short runA long-run process; includes structural change
LinkNecessary but not sufficient for developmentUsually needs growth; 'growth without development' is possible
  • Amartya Sen (Nobel 1998), in 'Development as Freedom' (1999), saw development as expanding people's capabilities and freedoms (to be healthy, educated, take part in society), not just income. Sustainable development, defined by the Brundtland Report 'Our Common Future' (1987), is development that meets present needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs. Inclusive growth (the theme of India's 11th Five-Year Plan, 2007–12) means growth that reaches all sections. Jobless growth is GDP rising without enough new jobs.

2. Measures of development

MeasureDetails
Per capita incomeThe World Bank groups countries as low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income by GNI per capita; India is lower-middle income. Simple, but hides inequality.
PQLIPhysical Quality of Life Index by Morris D. Morris (1979): life expectancy at age one, infant mortality and basic literacy, scored 0–100.
HDIHuman Development Index in UNDP's Human Development Report (since 1990), designed by Mahbub ul Haq with Amartya Sen: health (life expectancy at birth), education (mean and expected years of schooling) and standard of living (GNI per capita, PPP), combined by a geometric mean. India's value is about 0.685 and its rank about 130 of 193 (2025 report). Related indices: IHDI (adjusted for inequality), GDI, GII (gender).
MPIMultidimensional Poverty Index (UNDP and Oxford's OPHI, 2010) looks at health, education and living standards together, not income alone. NITI Aayog's national MPI estimated that about 24.8 crore Indians escaped multidimensional poverty between 2013–14 and 2022–23 (headcount from about 29% to 11%).
SDGs and other measuresThe UN's Sustainable Development Goals (2015): 17 goals, 169 targets for 2030. NITI Aayog's SDG India Index (since 2018) scored India 71 in 2023–24, with Kerala and Uttarakhand at the top. Others: Green GDP, Gross National Happiness (Bhutan), the World Happiness Report.

3. Theories of growth

Rostow's stages of growth
  • Harrod–Domar model: growth rate = saving (investment) rate ÷ capital–output ratio; to grow faster, save and invest more or use capital better. It guided India's First Five-Year Plan (1951–56). Solow (1956) added technology as the main source of long-run growth.
  • Rostow's five stages (1960) (see the diagram), with take-off as the key stage. Ragnar Nurkse's 'vicious circle of poverty': low income → low saving → low investment → low productivity → low income. Balanced growth (Nurkse, Rosenstein-Rodan's 'big push': invest in many sectors together) vs unbalanced growth (Albert Hirschman: invest in key sectors with strong linkages). Simon Kuznets' inverted-U curve (1955): inequality first rises, then falls as an economy grows.
  • India: Raj Krishna called the slow growth of about 3.5% a year (1950–80) the 'Hindu rate of growth'; growth sped up after the 1991 reforms, and India has been among the fastest-growing major economies in recent years. National goal: 'Viksit Bharat @2047' (a developed India by the 100th year of independence). AP: the 'Swarna Andhra @2047' vision (2024) aims to make AP a $2.4 trillion economy with high per capita income, through ten principles such as zero poverty, skills and water security.

SCIENCE · Solid waste: classification, disposal and management

Understand it first

What is it? Solid waste is any unwanted solid material we throw away: kitchen scraps, paper, plastic, glass, old phones, hospital waste, construction debris. Indian cities produce a huge amount every day, and much of it still ends up in open dumps that pollute groundwater, release methane, catch fire and breed mosquitoes and disease. Good management means segregating at source, reducing and recycling, and safely treating the rest. As a Panchayat Secretary you deal with this directly. Learn it as types → India's situation → disposal methods → waste hierarchy → rules for each type → conventions → Swachh Bharat and AP.

1. Types and India's situation

  • By source: municipal solid waste (homes, shops, markets), industrial, agricultural (crop residue), bio-medical, construction and demolition (C&D), e-waste, hazardous (chemicals, batteries) and plastic. By nature: biodegradable (food, leaves, paper; broken down by microbes) and non-biodegradable (plastic, glass, metal). For collection, households separate waste into wet (kitchen), dry (paper, plastic, metal) and domestic hazardous (batteries, paint, expired medicines, sanitary waste).
  • India generates about 1.6 lakh tonnes of municipal waste a day (CPCB, 2020–21); most is collected, but only about half is treated, and large legacy dumps remain (such as Ghazipur, Delhi), which are now being cleared by biomining. About half of urban waste is organic (wet), ideal for composting. India is the world's third-largest e-waste generator.

2. Disposal methods and the waste hierarchy

MethodHow it works; merits and problems
Open dumpingWaste piled on open land: the worst method; causes leachate (toxic liquid) in groundwater, methane, fires, smell and disease.
Sanitary landfillAn engineered pit with a clay or plastic liner, leachate collection, daily soil cover and methane (landfill gas) capture; only for inert, non-recyclable residue.
CompostingAerobic (with air) breakdown of wet waste by microbes into manure; vermicomposting uses earthworms (e.g. *Eisenia fetida*). Cheap; suits villages and towns.
BiomethanationAnaerobic (without air) digestion of wet waste gives biogas (mainly methane) for fuel and slurry as manure (e.g. GOBAR-Dhan plants).
Incineration and waste-to-energyBurning at high temperature cuts volume by about 90% and can make electricity; but mixed, wet Indian waste burns poorly, and poor plants emit dioxins and furans. Refuse-derived fuel (RDF) from dry waste is used in cement kilns. Other options: pyrolysis, plasma gasification.
Waste hierarchy

The aim is a circular economy, where materials are kept in use instead of being thrown away. Mission LiFE (2022) encourages less waste in daily life.
