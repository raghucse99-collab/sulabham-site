GEOGRAPHY · Ocean water: relief, temperature, salinity, waves, tides and currents

Understand it first

What is it? Oceans cover about 71% of the Earth. Their water is never still: waves ride on the surface, tides raise and lower the sea twice a day, and currents move water like rivers in the sea. These movements shape climate (warm and cold coasts, deserts, fog), fishing grounds, ports and shipping, and even India's monsoon (El Niño). Learn it as oceans and floor relief → temperature and salinity → waves and tsunamis → tides → currents and their effects → coral reefs.

1. Oceans, floor relief, temperature and salinity

  • Oceans: the Pacific is the largest and deepest (the Mariana Trench, about 11 km deep); then the Atlantic, the Indian Ocean (the only ocean named after a country) and the Arctic (smallest). Some also count the Southern Ocean around Antarctica.
  • Floor relief: the continental shelf (shallow, up to about 200 m; the best fishing grounds and oil fields such as Mumbai High; wider off India's west coast), the steep continental slope, the continental rise, the flat abyssal plain, mid-ocean ridges (underwater mountain chains, e.g. the Mid-Atlantic Ridge) and deep trenches where plates sink.
  • Temperature falls with depth; the zone of sharp fall is the thermocline. Salinity (salt content) averages about 35 parts per thousand (‰); it is high where evaporation is high and fresh water is low (Red Sea, about 40‰; inland lakes like the Dead Sea and Lake Van are far saltier) and low where rivers and rain add fresh water (Baltic Sea, about 7‰). The Bay of Bengal is less salty than the Arabian Sea because the Ganga, Brahmaputra, Godavari and Krishna pour fresh water into it.
Relief of the ocean floor

2. Waves and tides

  • Waves are mostly made by wind blowing over the sea; the water moves up and down in circles, while the energy moves forward. The top is the crest, the low point the trough. A tsunami ('harbour wave' in Japanese) is a series of huge waves caused by an undersea earthquake, landslide or volcano; in deep water it is fast and low, but it rises to great heights near the coast (Indian Ocean tsunami, 2004).
  • Tides are the regular rise (high tide) and fall (low tide) of the sea, caused mainly by the Moon's gravity (the Sun's pull is about half as strong) along with the Earth's rotation. Most coasts get two high and two low tides in about 24 hours 52 minutes. Spring tides (biggest range) come at new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon are in a line; neap tides (smallest range) come at the first and third quarter, when the Sun and Moon are at right angles.
  • The Bay of Fundy (Canada) has one of the world's highest tidal ranges (about 16 m). In India, the Gulf of Khambhat and Gulf of Kutch have large ranges, good for tidal energy. Uses of tides: they help ships enter tidal ports (Kolkata on the Hooghly, Kandla), clear silt from river mouths, and aid fishing.

3. Ocean currents

  • Ocean currents are large, steady streams of water. Causes: planetary winds, differences in temperature and salinity (density), the Coriolis force and the shape of coastlines. They form big loops (gyres) that turn clockwise in the north and anticlockwise in the south. Warm currents flow from the equator towards the poles (usually along eastern coasts of continents in low latitudes); cold currents flow from the poles towards the equator (usually along western coasts).

  • Effects: warm currents warm the coasts and bring rain; cold currents make coasts cool and dry, which is why coastal deserts (Atacama, Namib) lie beside them. Where warm and cold currents meet (the Grand Banks near Newfoundland, where the Gulf Stream meets the Labrador; off Japan, where the Kuroshio meets the Oyashio), mixing brings up nutrients for plankton, making the world's richest fishing grounds, though also thick fog.
  • El Niño is an unusual warming of the eastern Pacific off Peru (it weakens the cold Humboldt current); it often weakens India's monsoon. La Niña, the opposite cooling, often brings good rains to India. A positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) (western Indian Ocean warmer than the eastern) helps India's monsoon.
  • Coral reefs grow in warm (20–28°C), clear, shallow tropical seas: fringing reefs (close to the coast), barrier reefs (separated by a lagoon; the Great Barrier Reef, Australia) and atolls (ring-shaped, e.g. Lakshadweep). India's reefs: Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kutch, Andaman and Nicobar, Lakshadweep. Warmer seas cause coral bleaching.

ECONOMY · Strategy of planning in India; NITI Aayog

Understand it first

What is it? Economic planning means the government sets targets and priorities for a period (usually five years) and decides where to invest. After 1947, India chose planned development in a mixed economy, with the public sector leading heavy industry. Twelve Five-Year Plans ran from 1951 to 2017. After the 1991 reforms, planning became indicative (guiding, not commanding), and in 2015 the Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog, a think tank that works with the States. Learn it as early ideas → Planning Commission and NDC → the twelve Plans → strategy changes → NITI Aayog → comparison.

Planning ideas before 1950
  • M. Visvesvaraya wrote 'Planned Economy for India' (1934). The Congress set up the National Planning Committee (1938) under Nehru (when Subhas Chandra Bose was Congress president). The Bombay Plan (1944) was written by industrialists such as J.R.D. Tata and G.D. Birla; the Gandhian Plan (1944, S.N. Agarwal), the People's Plan (1945, M.N. Roy) and the Sarvodaya Plan (1950, Jayaprakash Narayan) followed. The Planning Commission was set up on 15 March 1950 by a Cabinet resolution: it was neither constitutional nor statutory; the Prime Minister was its chairman. The National Development Council (NDC, 6 August 1952), with the PM and all Chief Ministers, approved the Plans.

1. The Five-Year Plans

  • Strategy shift: till 1991, planning was close to imperative (the State decided investment), aimed at import substitution, heavy industry and a big public sector, with licences controlling private firms. Results: a strong industrial base, dams, IITs, self-sufficiency in food after the Green Revolution; but slow growth ('Hindu rate'), inefficiency, persistent poverty and a balance-of-payments crisis (1991). After 1991, plans became indicative, letting the market and private sector lead.

2. NITI Aayog

NITI Aayog at a glance
  • The National Institution for Transforming India (NITI) Aayog was set up on 1 January 2015, also by a Cabinet resolution (not constitutional or statutory). Chairperson: the Prime Minister; a Vice-Chairperson (the first was Arvind Panagariya), full-time members, up to four Union Ministers as ex-officio members, special invitees and a CEO. The Governing Council includes all Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors; Regional Councils deal with regional issues.
  • Role: a policy think tank promoting cooperative federalism (working with States) and competitive federalism (ranking States on indices: SDG India Index, Composite Water Management Index, School Education Quality Index, Health Index, Export Preparedness Index, India Innovation Index, Fiscal Health Index, the national MPI). It does not allocate funds (that went to the Finance Ministry). Programmes: Atal Innovation Mission (2016) with Atal Tinkering Labs; the Aspirational Districts Programme (2018, 112 districts, including Vizianagaram, Visakhapatnam and YSR Kadapa in AP); the Aspirational Blocks Programme (2023, 500 blocks); the Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office (DMEO); work on the 'Viksit Bharat @2047' vision.

SCIENCE · Environmental pollution: types, sources and impacts

Understand it first

What is it? Pollution is the entry of harmful substances or energy (pollutants) into air, water, soil or surroundings that harms living things or property. India has some of the world's most polluted cities, rivers with high sewage loads, and areas with fluoride or arsenic in groundwater. Exams ask about pollutants, their sources, health effects, measuring terms (AQI, BOD), diseases, laws and Indian cases. Learn it as air → water → soil → noise → thermal, radioactive, light and marine → laws and bodies → AP cases.

1. Air pollution

PointDetails
PollutantsPrimary (released directly): particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5), sulphur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide (CO), volatile organic compounds, lead. Secondary (formed in the air): ground-level ozone and PAN (from NOx and hydrocarbons in sunlight), acid rain. PM2.5 (smaller than 2.5 micrometres) goes deep into the lungs and blood. CO binds to haemoglobin and cuts oxygen supply.
SourcesVehicles, industries and coal power plants, construction and road dust, burning of crop stubble (Punjab and Haryana in October–November), waste burning, firecrackers, and indoor chulhas using wood and dung (cut by Ujjwala LPG).
Smog and acid rainClassical (sulphurous) smog: smoke + fog in cold, damp air (London, 1952). Photochemical smog: NOx and hydrocarbons react in sunlight to form ozone (Los Angeles; Delhi). Acid rain (pH below 5.6) comes from SO₂ and NOx; it damages lakes, forests and buildings; the yellowing of the Taj Mahal ('marble cancer') led to the Taj Trapezium Zone (Supreme Court, M.C. Mehta, 1996).
Measuring and controlNational Ambient Air Quality Standards (2009) cover 12 pollutants (PM2.5 annual limit 40 µg/m³, while the WHO 2021 guideline is 5 µg/m³). The National Air Quality Index (2014) uses 8 pollutants and 6 categories. The National Clean Air Programme (NCAP, 2019) covers about 130 non-attainment cities, aiming to cut particulate levels by up to 40% by 2025–26 (from 2017 levels). In Delhi–NCR, the Commission for Air Quality Management (2021) enforces the Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP). The Bhopal gas tragedy (1984) was caused by methyl isocyanate (MIC).
National Air Quality Index categories

2. Water and soil pollution

  • Sources: untreated sewage, industrial effluents, fertiliser and pesticide run-off, and aquaculture waste. Measures: Dissolved Oxygen (DO): fish need about 4–5 mg/L or more; Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD): oxygen microbes need to break down organic waste; higher BOD means more pollution and less oxygen for fish; COD measures all oxidisable matter; faecal coliform bacteria show sewage. Excess nitrates and phosphates cause eutrophication (Day 25).
  • Diseases from chemicals: mercury → Minamata disease (Japan); cadmium → itai-itai ('ouch-ouch') disease; nitrates → blue baby syndrome; excess fluoride → fluorosis (teeth and bones; found in Prakasam and parts of Rayalaseema, as well as Nalgonda in Telangana); arsenic → skin lesions and cancer (Ganga–Brahmaputra plains, West Bengal); lead → brain damage in children. Soil pollution comes from overuse of chemicals, industrial dumping, plastics and salinity from poor irrigation. The Namami Gange Programme (2014) cleans the Ganga.

3. Other types

TypeFacts
NoiseMeasured in decibels (dB); long exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing. The Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 set day/night limits: industrial 75/70, commercial 65/55, residential 55/45, silence zone 50/40 dB(A); a silence zone covers 100 m around hospitals, schools and courts; loudspeakers are not allowed from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m. (with limited exceptions).
ThermalHot water released by power plants and industries into rivers and the sea lowers dissolved oxygen and kills fish.
RadioactiveFrom nuclear accidents (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011), nuclear waste and testing; causes cancer and genetic damage. Natural background radiation is high on Kerala's monazite beaches.
Light and marineLight pollution confuses birds, insects and sea-turtle hatchlings. Marine pollution: oil spills, plastics and microplastics, sewage, and 'dead zones' with no oxygen.
  • Laws and bodies: Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 set up the Central and State Pollution Control Boards; Air Act, 1981; Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; National Green Tribunal (2010). AP cases: the LG Polymers styrene gas leak at Visakhapatnam (7 May 2020); pollution of Kolleru by fish tanks (cleared in 'Operation Kolleru', 2006); fluoride in groundwater in Prakasam and Rayalaseema; industrial pollution near Visakhapatnam and Kakinada.
