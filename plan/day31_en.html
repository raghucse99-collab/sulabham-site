GEOGRAPHY · India: physiographic divisions

Understand it first

What is it? Physiography means the shape of the land: mountains, plains, plateaus, deserts, coasts and islands. India's relief was made over very long periods: the Peninsular Plateau is one of the oldest landmasses (part of Gondwana), the Himalayas are young fold mountains pushed up when the Indian plate hit Asia, and the Northern Plains were filled in by river silt. Each division has its own soils, crops, rivers and people. Learn it as India's size and location → Himalayas → Northern Plains → Peninsular Plateau → Desert → Coastal Plains → Islands → AP.

Physical map of India showing mountains, plains and plateaus. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

1. Size and location

Area about 32.87 lakh sq km, about 2.4% of the world's land: the 7th largest country. Mainland latitudes 8°4′ N to 37°6′ N; longitudes 68°7′ E to 97°25′ E. North–south about 3,214 km; east–west about 2,933 km. Land frontier about 15,200 km; the coastline was long given as 7,516.6 km (with islands), and a 2025 re-measurement put it at about 11,098 km (check the latest). The Tropic of Cancer divides India almost in half; the standard meridian is 82°30′ E. India's southernmost point is Indira Point (Great Nicobar).

Six physiographic divisions

2. The Himalayas

Cross-section of the Himalayas
Mount Everest (8,849 m), the world's highest peak, in the Greater Himalaya. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
RangeFeatures
Greater Himalaya (Himadri)The highest and most continuous range, average about 6,000 m, with a granite core and permanent snow; peaks: Everest (8,849 m, Nepal), Kangchenjunga (8,586 m, highest in India proper, Sikkim), Nanga Parbat, Nanda Devi; passes: Zoji La, Shipki La, Nathu La.
Lesser Himalaya (Himachal)3,700–4,500 m; ranges Pir Panjal, Dhauladhar, Mahabharat; valleys of Kashmir, Kullu, Kangra; hill stations Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital, Darjeeling.
Shiwaliks (Outer Himalaya)The outermost and lowest range (900–1,100 m), made of loose river sediments; long flat valleys between them and the Lesser Himalaya are duns (Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun).
Trans-Himalaya and PurvanchalNorth of the Greater Himalaya: Karakoram (K2, 8,611 m, India's claimed highest), Ladakh, Zaskar. In the east, the Himalaya bends south as the Purvanchal: Patkai Bum, Naga hills, Manipur hills, Mizo (Lushai) hills.
Regional divisions (west to east)Punjab Himalaya (Indus–Sutlej), Kumaon Himalaya (Sutlej–Kali), Nepal Himalaya (Kali–Teesta), Assam Himalaya (Teesta–Dihang).

3. The Northern Plains

Belts of the Northern Plains

Formed by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra and their tributaries; about 2,400 km long, among the most fertile and densely populated regions in the world. Three parts: the Punjab plains (land between two rivers is a doab), the Ganga plains and the Brahmaputra plains (with Majuli, among the world's largest river islands). Saline patches in dry areas are called reh or kallar.

4. The Peninsular Plateau and the Desert

  • Peninsular Plateau: a roughly triangular block of old, hard rocks. Central Highlands (north of the Narmada): Malwa plateau, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand and the mineral-rich Chota Nagpur; ranges: Aravallis (old, worn; highest Guru Shikhar, 1,722 m, Mount Abu), Vindhyas and Satpura. Deccan Plateau (south of the Narmada), sloping east, so most rivers flow east into the Bay of Bengal; its north-west is covered by the Deccan Traps (lava). The Meghalaya (Shillong) plateau (Garo, Khasi, Jaintia hills) is part of the peninsula, cut off by the Malda gap.
  • Western and Eastern Ghats: the Western Ghats (Sahyadri) are higher and continuous, crossed only by passes (Thal, Bhor, Pal ghats); highest peak Anamudi (2,695 m), then Doddabetta (2,637 m) in the Nilgiris, where the two Ghats meet. The Eastern Ghats are lower and broken by the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri; high points include Mahendragiri (Odisha) and Arma Konda (about 1,680 m, AP, the highest).
  • Indian (Thar) Desert: west of the Aravallis; rainfall usually below 150 mm; sand dunes (barchans); the Luni is the only large river; salt lakes such as Sambhar. The Aravallis lie parallel to the south-west monsoon winds, so they do not stop them to cause rain.

5. Coastal Plains and Islands

PartFeatures
Western coastal plainNarrow; from north: Kachchh and Kathiawar, Konkan (Mumbai–Goa), Kannad (Goa–Mangaluru), Malabar (Kerala) with backwaters (kayals) such as Vembanad; west-flowing rivers form estuaries, not deltas.
Eastern coastal plainWider, with big deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri); the Northern Circars (Mahanadi to Krishna, including most of the AP coast) and the Coromandel coast (Krishna to Kanyakumari); lagoons Chilika (largest brackish lagoon) and Pulicat; Kolleru lake between the Godavari and Krishna deltas.
Andaman and NicobarOver 500 islands in the Bay of Bengal, thought to be the raised parts of submerged mountain ranges; the Ten Degree Channel separates the Andamans from the Nicobars; highest point Saddle Peak; Barren Island (active volcano) and Narcondam (dormant).
Lakshadweep36 coral islands in the Arabian Sea (atolls); capital Kavaratti; the Nine Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the main group, and the Eight Degree Channel separates Minicoy from the Maldives. The Palk Strait lies between India and Sri Lanka.

Andhra Pradesh: three relief zones: the coastal plains and deltas (Krishna–Godavari, Kolleru, Pulicat); the Eastern Ghats (Araku and Ananthagiri hills in the north, Papikonda at the Godavari gorge, Nallamala, Velikonda, Erramala, Seshachalam and Palakonda in the south, and Horsley Hills near Madanapalle); and the Rayalaseema plateau, part of the Deccan.

ECONOMY · The New Economic Reforms of 1991

Understand it first

What is it? In 1991 India faced a severe balance-of-payments crisis: it had almost no foreign exchange left to pay for imports. The Government of P.V. Narasimha Rao, with Finance Minister Manmohan Singh, responded with sweeping reforms, known as LPG: Liberalisation, Privatisation and Globalisation. They ended the 'licence raj', opened India to foreign trade and investment, and shrank the State's direct role in business. They changed India's growth path. Learn it as why the crisis came → immediate rescue → stabilisation vs structural reform → LPG measures → committees → results and criticisms → later reforms.

1. The crisis and the rescue

  • Causes: large fiscal deficits through the 1980s (about 8% of GDP for the Centre); growing foreign debt; the Gulf War (1990–91) pushed up oil prices and cut remittances from Indian workers in the Gulf; political instability; inflation in double digits; a credit-rating downgrade, after which NRIs withdrew deposits. By mid-1991, foreign exchange reserves had fallen to about $1 billion, enough for only about two weeks of imports.
  • Rescue: India pledged its gold (about 47 tonnes with the Bank of England and Bank of Japan in July 1991, after an earlier sale-and-repurchase of 20 tonnes) to raise dollars, and took loans from the IMF (with conditions) and the World Bank. The rupee was devalued in two steps (1 and 3 July 1991, about 18–19%). The New Industrial Policy and Union Budget were presented on 24 July 1991.
  • Two kinds of measures: stabilisation (short-term, linked to the IMF): cut the fiscal deficit, control inflation and fix the balance of payments by managing demand. Structural adjustment (long-term, linked to the World Bank): change the supply side of the economy through LPG, so it grows faster and competes globally.
The 1991 reforms: LPG

2. The LPG measures

AreaWhat changed
IndustryIndustrial licensing abolished for all but a few industries (now only a handful, such as defence, hazardous chemicals, tobacco and alcohol); industries reserved for the public sector cut from 17 to 8, and now only 2 (atomic energy and railway operations); MRTP limits on big firms removed (later replaced by the Competition Act, 2002; CCI).
Trade and foreign exchangeImport licensing relaxed and customs duties cut sharply; a market-determined exchange rate (1993); current account convertibility (1994); FERA replaced by the milder FEMA (1999); India joined the WTO as a founding member (1995). Full capital account convertibility has not been adopted (Tarapore committees, 1997 and 2006).
Foreign investmentFDI allowed up to 51% automatically in many industries (now up to 100% in most sectors); foreign institutional investors allowed into stock markets; SEBI became a statutory regulator (1992).
PrivatisationDisinvestment of government shares in public sector units; the Department of Disinvestment later became DIPAM (2016). Strategic disinvestment means selling management control, e.g. Air India to the Tata group (January 2022); the LIC IPO came in 2022.
CommitteesNarasimham Committee (1991, 1998): banking reforms (lower CRR and SLR, new private banks, prudential norms); Raja Chelliah Committee (1991): tax reforms; Malhotra Committee (1993): insurance reforms, leading to IRDA (1999); Rangarajan Committee (1993): disinvestment.

3. Results, criticisms and later reforms

  • Gains: faster growth (about 6–7% on average, compared with the 'Hindu rate'); forex reserves rose to over $600 billion in recent years; booms in IT, telecom, banking and services; more consumer choice; India became a major destination for foreign investment.
  • Criticisms: agriculture got less public investment; jobless growth and informal work; rising inequality; small industries hurt by cheap imports; services grew but manufacturing jobs lagged; reforms were sometimes called 'reforms by stealth' because they came without wide public debate.
  • Second-generation reforms: FRBM Act (2003) for fiscal discipline; the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016); inflation targeting with a Monetary Policy Committee (2016); GST (2017); the four Labour Codes (in force 2025); Make in India (2014) and PLI schemes for manufacturing.

SCIENCE · Pollution control: laws, regulation and recent initiatives

Understand it first

What is it? Day 30 explained what pollution is and what it does. Today is about how India controls it: laws passed by Parliament, rules and notifications made under them, regulators (the Pollution Control Boards and the NGT), principles laid down by the courts, international agreements, and new schemes for clean air, clean rivers and clean transport. Learn it as laws in time order → tools of regulation (consent, EIA, CRZ, industry categories) → NGT → court principles → Montreal and Kigali → recent initiatives → AP.

India's main pollution-control laws

1. Laws and regulation

Law or toolKey points
Water Act, 1974India's first major pollution law; set up the Central and State Pollution Control Boards; industries need the Board's 'consent to establish' and 'consent to operate'. A 2024 amendment replaced jail terms for minor violations with penalties.
Air Act, 1981Passed after the UN Stockholm Conference (1972); States can declare air pollution control areas; the same Boards enforce it.
Environment (Protection) Act, 1986An umbrella law passed after the Bhopal gas tragedy; gives the Centre wide powers to set standards and make rules. Under it come the EIA Notification (1994, revised 2006): big projects need environmental clearance after an impact study and a public hearing (Category A by the Centre, Category B by State authorities); the Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification (1991; 2011; 2019); and all the waste rules. The Jan Vishwas Act (2023) replaced jail with penalties for many offences under this Act and the Air Act.
Industry categoriesSince 2016, industries are grouped by pollution potential: Red (most polluting), Orange, Green, and White (practically non-polluting; no consent needed). The most polluting units must install continuous emission and effluent monitoring linked online to the Boards.
National Green Tribunal (2010)A specialised court for environmental cases; principal bench in New Delhi, with zonal benches (Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai); chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge or retired High Court Chief Justice; aims to decide cases within six months; applies sustainable development, precautionary and polluter-pays principles; appeals go to the Supreme Court.

2. Principles from the courts and international agreements

PrincipleCase
Absolute liability: a hazardous industry must pay for all harm, with no excusesM.C. Mehta (Oleum gas leak), 1986
Right to a pollution-free environment is part of the right to life (Art 21)Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar, 1991
Polluter pays and precautionary principle are part of Indian lawVellore Citizens' Welfare Forum, 1996
Public trust doctrine: the State holds rivers, forests and the sea in trust for the peopleM.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath, 1997

Ozone layer: the Vienna Convention (1985) and the Montreal Protocol (1987) phase out ozone-depleting substances such as CFCs; it is seen as the most successful environmental treaty. The Kigali Amendment (2016) adds a phase-down of HFCs, which do not harm ozone but are powerful greenhouse gases; India will freeze HFC use in 2028 and cut it from 2032. India's Cooling Action Plan (2019) supports this.

3. Recent initiatives

  • Clean air: NCAP (2019) with the PRANA portal to track city action plans; Swachh Vayu Survekshan (since 2023) ranks cities on clean-air work; CAQM (2021) and GRAP for Delhi–NCR; Surat launched the world's first emissions trading scheme for particulate matter (2019).
  • Clean transport and fuel: BS-VI (2020); FAME I (2015) and FAME II (2019) for electric vehicles, followed by PM E-DRIVE (2024) and PM-eBus Sewa (2023); the Vehicle Scrappage Policy (2021) to remove old polluting vehicles; ethanol blending; PM Ujjwala Yojana (2016) LPG connections to cut indoor smoke.
  • Stubble and rivers: the Crop Residue Management scheme (machines such as the Happy Seeder), the Pusa bio-decomposer that rots straw in the field, and using straw for 2G ethanol and biomass power; Namami Gange (2014) with sewage treatment plants and real-time monitoring of industrial effluents.
  • AP: the AP Pollution Control Board grants consents and monitors industries along the coast (Visakhapatnam, Kakinada, Nellore); Visakhapatnam, Vijayawada, Guntur, Nellore and other cities are under NCAP; after the LG Polymers leak (2020), rules on hazardous chemical storage near homes were tightened.
