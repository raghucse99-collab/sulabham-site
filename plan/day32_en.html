GEOGRAPHY · India: climate and monsoon

Understand it first

What is it? India has a tropical monsoon climate. The word monsoon (from Arabic *mausim*, 'season') means winds that reverse direction with the seasons: moist south-west winds from the sea in summer and dry north-east winds from the land in winter. Nearly half of India's farmland is rain-fed, so a good or poor monsoon affects food prices, rural incomes, power and drinking water. Learn it as factors of India's climate → how the monsoon works → four seasons → onset, branches, breaks and retreat → rainfall pattern → importance → AP.

1. Factors and mechanism

  • Factors of India's climate: latitude (the Tropic of Cancer runs through the middle); the Himalayas (they block cold Central Asian winds in winter and trap the monsoon, making India warmer and wetter than other places at the same latitude); distance from the sea (coasts have a mild, maritime climate; the interior has extreme, continental temperatures); altitude (hill stations are cooler); relief (windward and rain-shadow sides); pressure and winds; jet streams; and ocean conditions (El Niño, La Niña, Indian Ocean Dipole).
What drives the south-west monsoon

2. The four seasons

India's four seasons
SeasonFeatures
Cold weather (December–February)Clear skies, cool dry north-east winds; western disturbances from the Mediterranean bring light rain to the north-west, which helps rabi wheat, and snow to the Himalayas; the Tamil Nadu coast gets rain from the north-east winds crossing the Bay of Bengal.
Hot weather (March–May)Temperatures rise from south to north; loo winds in the north; pre-monsoon storms: mango showers (Kerala, Karnataka), blossom showers (Karnataka coffee), Kal Baisakhi (West Bengal) and Bordoichila (Assam).
South-west monsoon (June–September)Onset over Kerala around 1 June (it came on 24 May in 2025); covers the whole country by about 8 July. Two branches: the Arabian Sea branch (stronger; heavy rain on the Western Ghats, Mumbai) and the Bay of Bengal branch (heavy rain in the north-east, Mawsynram; then moves west along the Himalayas). It gives about three-quarters of India's annual rain. The long period average (LPA) is about 87 cm; 96–104% of LPA is 'normal'. Dry spells within the season are breaks in the monsoon. The Tamil Nadu coast stays fairly dry because it is in the rain shadow and the winds blow parallel to it.
Retreating monsoon (October–November, into December)Withdrawal starts from north-west India around mid-September and is over by about mid-October. Clear skies with hot, humid days cause 'October heat'. The north-east monsoon brings most of the year's rain to the Tamil Nadu coast, south coastal AP and Rayalaseema; cyclones from the Bay of Bengal are common.
Normal onset dates of the south-west monsoon across India. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

3. Rainfall pattern and importance

  • Heavy rain (over 200 cm): the windward Western Ghats and the north-east (Mawsynram, Cherrapunji). Low rain (under 60 cm): western Rajasthan, Ladakh and the rain-shadow interior of the Deccan (parts of Maharashtra, Karnataka and Rayalaseema). Rainfall is uneven and unreliable: the drier the area, the more it varies from year to year, which brings droughts and floods.
  • Importance: the monsoon decides kharif sowing, refills reservoirs and groundwater, and affects hydropower, inflation and rural demand; it also gives India a cultural unity of seasons and festivals. IMD issues a long-range forecast in April and an update in late May; the Monsoon Mission (2012) and Mission Mausam (2024) aim to improve forecasts.
  • Andhra Pradesh gets rain from both monsoons: the south-west monsoon gives the larger share to north coastal AP and the Godavari–Krishna region, while the north-east monsoon matters greatly for Nellore, Prakasam and Rayalaseema (Tirupati, Chittoor). Anantapur, in the rain shadow, is among India's driest districts.
Annual rainfall across India: wettest on the Western Ghats and in the north-east. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

ECONOMY · Decentralisation of financial resources

Understand it first

What is it? Fiscal federalism is about who collects taxes and who spends. The Union has the most productive taxes (income tax, corporation tax, a large share of GST), while States and local bodies run most services (police, health, schools, water, roads). This mismatch is the vertical imbalance; differences between rich and poor States are the horizontal imbalance. The Constitution fixes it by sharing taxes and giving grants, mainly through the Finance Commission, the GST system and, within States, the State Finance Commission. Learn it as the imbalance → constitutional provisions → Finance Commissions → GST → State and local finances → AP.

How money flows down to local bodies

1. Constitutional provisions (Part XII)

ArticleProvision
268, 269268: duties levied by the Union but collected and kept by States (e.g. some stamp duties). 269: taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to States (e.g. on inter-State sale of goods outside GST).
269A, 279A269A: IGST on inter-State supply, shared between the Union and States. 279A: the GST Council: chaired by the Union Finance Minister; the Centre has one-third and the States two-thirds of the vote; decisions need a three-fourths majority.
270, 271270: taxes levied and collected by the Union are shared with States (the divisible pool), as the Finance Commission recommends. 271: surcharges for Union purposes are not shared; cesses are also outside the divisible pool, which States criticise.
275, 282275: statutory grants-in-aid to States in need (e.g. revenue deficit grants) on Finance Commission advice. 282: discretionary grants by the Union for any public purpose (used for centrally sponsored schemes).
276, 293276: States and local bodies may levy a professional tax up to ₹2,500 a year. 293: a State that owes money to the Centre needs the Centre's consent to borrow.
280; 243I, 243Y280: the Finance Commission, set up by the President every five years (a chairman and four members). After the 73rd and 74th Amendments, it also suggests measures to augment State funds for panchayats and municipalities (Art 280(3)(bb) and (c)), based on State Finance Commission reports (Arts 243I, 243Y).

2. Finance Commissions

  • The 1st Finance Commission (1951) was chaired by K.C. Neogy. The 14th (Y.V. Reddy; 2015–20) raised the States' share of the divisible pool sharply to 42%. The 15th (N.K. Singh; 2021–26) gave 41% (1% less because J&K became a UT), and about ₹4.36 lakh crore in grants to local governments, part of them tied to sanitation and drinking water. The 16th (Arvind Panagariya; 2026–31) was set up in December 2023 (check its recommended share in the latest Budget).
  • Horizontal sharing among States uses a formula. Under the 15th FC, income distance (poorer States get more) had the biggest weight, with population (2011), area, forest and ecology, demographic performance (rewarding States that controlled population) and tax effort. Andhra Pradesh's share of the States' pool under the 15th FC was about 4.05%; AP also received revenue-deficit grants after bifurcation.
15th Finance Commission criteria

3. GST, State and local finances

  • GST came through the 101st Amendment (2016) and started on 1 July 2017, merging many Union and State taxes. It has CGST (Union), SGST (State) and IGST (inter-State). States were promised compensation for revenue loss for five years (till June 2022); the compensation cess was continued to repay loans taken during COVID. In September 2025, GST was simplified to mainly two rates (5% and 18%) plus a higher rate for luxury and 'sin' goods (check the latest).
  • States' own revenue: SGST, excise on alcohol, stamp duty and registration, motor vehicle tax, electricity duty, land revenue; States also borrow within FRBM limits (fiscal deficit target about 3% of GSDP). Local bodies' own revenue: property tax, professional tax, user charges and fees, advertisement tax. Local bodies remain weak: own revenue is small, many States delay SFCs or ignore their reports, and most money comes as tied grants. The 15th FC made grants conditional on audited accounts and property tax floor rates.

SCIENCE · Transgenics, eco-friendly agriculture and bioremediation

Understand it first

What is it? Biotechnology uses living things to make useful products. A transgenic (genetically modified, GM) organism carries a gene from another species, put in by recombinant DNA technology, for example cotton with a bacterial gene that kills pests. Eco-friendly agriculture grows food while protecting soil, water and health, using biofertilisers, biopesticides and natural farming instead of heavy chemicals; Andhra Pradesh leads India in natural farming. Bioremediation uses microbes and plants to clean up pollution. Learn it as how GM organisms are made → examples and India's status → regulation and concerns → eco-friendly farming → bioremediation.

1. Transgenics

Making a transgenic organism
ItemFacts
ToolsRestriction enzymes ('molecular scissors'), DNA ligase ('molecular glue'), vectors such as plasmids; Agrobacterium tumefaciens is called a 'natural genetic engineer' because its Ti plasmid carries DNA into plant cells; the gene gun shoots DNA-coated particles into cells. Gene editing with CRISPR-Cas9 (Nobel Prize 2020, Charpentier and Doudna) changes a plant's own genes without adding foreign DNA; India exempted such simple edits (SDN-1, SDN-2) from GM rules in 2022 and released two genome-edited rice varieties in May 2025.
GM cropsBt cotton (cry genes from Bacillus thuringiensis kill bollworms; approved in 2002) is the only GM crop commercially grown in India; pink bollworm later developed resistance. Bt brinjal was cleared by GEAC in 2009 but put under a moratorium in 2010 (Bangladesh grows it). GM mustard (DMH-11) got approval for environmental release in 2022; the Supreme Court gave a split verdict (2024) and asked for a national GM policy. Golden rice has beta-carotene (vitamin A); the first GM food was the Flavr Savr tomato (1994).
Animals and medicineHuman insulin made by GM E. coli (Humulin, 1982, the first rDNA medicine); vaccines such as hepatitis B; transgenic animals: Rosie the cow (milk with a human protein), GM salmon, GM mosquitoes to fight dengue and malaria.
Regulation and concernsThe Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Environment Ministry (Rules of 1989 under the EPA) approves field trials and release; the RCGM under the Department of Biotechnology reviews research; internationally, the Cartagena Protocol on biosafety. Concerns: gene flow to wild relatives, pests becoming resistant, effects on non-target insects, farmers' dependence on seed companies, and labelling of GM food. Benefits: higher yields, less pesticide, better nutrition, drought tolerance.

2. Eco-friendly agriculture

  • Biofertilisers: Rhizobium (nitrogen fixation in legume root nodules), Azotobacter and Azospirillum (free-living nitrogen fixers), blue-green algae and Azolla (a water fern hosting Anabaena) for paddy, and mycorrhiza (VAM) fungi that help roots absorb phosphorus. Biopesticides: Bt, the fungus Trichoderma (controls soil-borne plant diseases), NPV (a virus against caterpillars) and neem. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) combines resistant varieties, crop rotation, natural enemies and only need-based chemicals.
  • Organic and natural farming: organic farming avoids synthetic fertilisers and pesticides; Sikkim became India's first fully organic State (2016); schemes: Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (PKVY, 2015) for organic clusters and MOVCDNER for the north-east. Natural farming (inspired by Subhash Palekar's 'zero budget' method) uses on-farm inputs such as jeevamrutha (from cow dung and urine), beejamrutha (seed treatment), mulching and soil aeration. Andhra Pradesh Community-managed Natural Farming (APCNF), run by Rythu Sadhikara Samstha since 2016, is one of the world's largest agroecology programmes and won the Gulbenkian Prize for Humanity (2024). The Centre launched the National Mission on Natural Farming (November 2024).
  • Other steps: Soil Health Cards (2015); neem-coated urea (2015); nano urea (2021); PM-PRANAM (2023) rewards States that cut chemical fertiliser use; micro-irrigation under PMKSY ('per drop more crop'); the System of Rice Intensification (SRI); zero tillage and crop diversification.

3. Bioremediation

Types of bioremediation
  • Bioremediation uses microbes, plants or fungi to break down or remove pollutants such as oil, pesticides, heavy metals and sewage. It is cheap and natural but slow, and works only for some pollutants. In-situ methods treat the soil or water where it is (adding nutrients or microbes, pumping air); ex-situ methods move it to a treatment site (landfarming, biopiles, bioreactors).
  • Examples: Ananda Mohan Chakrabarty created an oil-eating Pseudomonas ('superbug'); its US patent (1980) was the first patent on a GM organism. 'Oilzapper' (developed by TERI with Indian Oil) is a mix of bacteria used to clean oil spills and oily sludge. Phytoremediation: Indian mustard takes up heavy metals; sunflowers were used to absorb radioactive elements after Chernobyl; water hyacinth and constructed wetlands clean sewage. Biomining of old dumps (Day 29) also uses microbes.
