GEOGRAPHY · India: drainage system

Understand it first

What is it? Drainage means the flow of water through rivers and their tributaries. The area drained by a river and its tributaries is its basin (catchment); the high land that separates two basins is a water divide. India's rivers fall into two families: the Himalayan rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra) and the Peninsular rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Mahanadi, Narmada and others). Rivers give water, fertile soil, power and transport, and are the basis of inter-State water disputes. Learn it as drainage patterns → two families compared → Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra → east- and west-flowing Peninsular rivers → lakes → river linking and disputes → AP.

1. The Himalayan rivers

SystemFacts
IndusRises in Tibet near Lake Mansarovar, enters India in Ladakh, then flows through Pakistan to the Arabian Sea. Tributaries (the 'five rivers' of Punjab): Jhelum (from Verinag; flows through Wular lake), Chenab (largest tributary), Ravi, Beas (from near Rohtang) and Sutlej (from Rakas Tal; enters India at Shipki La; Bhakra Nangal dam). The Indus Waters Treaty (1960, World Bank) gave the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) mainly to Pakistan; India put the treaty 'in abeyance' in April 2025.
GangaThe Bhagirathi (from Gangotri glacier, Gaumukh) and the Alaknanda meet at Devprayag to form the Ganga; India's longest river (about 2,525 km) and National River (2008). Left-bank tributaries: Ramganga, Gomti, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi (the 'sorrow of Bihar' for its floods). Right-bank: the Yamuna (from Yamunotri; with Chambal, Betwa, Ken from the plateau) and the Son (from Amarkantak). The Farakka barrage feeds the Hooghly; the Ganga enters Bangladesh as the Padma and forms the Sundarbans delta with the Brahmaputra.
BrahmaputraRises at the Chemayungdung glacier near Mansarovar; called the Tsangpo in Tibet, the Dihang (Siang) in Arunachal, the Brahmaputra in Assam (after joining the Dibang and Lohit) and the Jamuna in Bangladesh. A braided river that carries huge silt and causes floods; Majuli is one of the world's largest river islands. China began a very large dam on the Yarlung Tsangpo (lower reaches, 2025), raising concerns downstream.

2. The Peninsular rivers

RiverFacts
GodavariThe longest Peninsular river (about 1,465 km), called the 'Dakshin Ganga'; rises at Trimbakeshwar (Nashik); tributaries Pranhita (Wardha + Wainganga), Indravati, Sabari, Manjira; forms a large delta in AP; Polavaram project; Papikonda gorge.
KrishnaRises at Mahabaleshwar (Maharashtra); about 1,400 km; tributaries Koyna, Bhima, Ghataprabha, Malaprabha, Tungabhadra, Musi; dams Almatti, Srisailam, Nagarjuna Sagar, Prakasam barrage (Vijayawada); delta in AP.
Mahanadi, Kaveri, PennarMahanadi: from Sihawa (Chhattisgarh); Hirakud dam (Odisha). Kaveri: from Talakaveri (Brahmagiri hills, Karnataka); gets rain from both monsoons, so it flows almost all year; Shivanasamudra falls; KRS and Mettur dams. Pennar: from the Nandi hills (Karnataka); flows through Anantapur, Kadapa and Nellore. Vamsadhara and Nagavali drain north coastal AP.
West-flowingNarmada (from Amarkantak; flows west through a rift valley between the Vindhyas and Satpuras; Dhuandhar falls at Jabalpur; Sardar Sarovar dam) and Tapi (from Multai, Betul) form estuaries; also Sabarmati, Mahi (crosses the Tropic of Cancer twice), Sharavati (Jog falls), Periyar, Mandovi, Zuari; the Luni ends in the Rann of Kutch.

3. Lakes, river linking and disputes

  • Lakes: Wular (largest freshwater lake, J&K), Dal; Loktak (Manipur; floating vegetation called phumdis; Keibul Lamjao, the only floating national park); Chilika and Pulicat (lagoons); Kolleru (AP); Sambhar (salt); Vembanad (Kerala; longest lake); Hussain Sagar (Hyderabad).
  • River linking: the National Perspective Plan (1980); the Ken–Betwa link (first under the national plan; foundation laid December 2024); AP's Pattiseema lift (2016) moved Godavari water to the Krishna through the Polavaram right canal. Disputes: Article 262 and the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956; tribunals for the Krishna (Bachawat, 1970s; Brijesh Kumar, 2010), Godavari (Bachawat, 1980), Vamsadhara (AP–Odisha, award 2017) and Kaveri (award 2007; Supreme Court verdict 2018; Cauvery Water Management Authority).

ECONOMY · Money supply: functions and measures

Understand it first

What is it? Money is anything generally accepted to buy goods and settle debts. Before money, people used barter (goods for goods), which needed a 'double coincidence of wants': the other person had to want exactly what you had. Money supply is the total stock of money held by the public at a time. Too much money pushes up prices (inflation); too little slows the economy. The RBI measures and manages it. Learn it as barter problems → functions of money → kinds of money and who issues it → measures M0 to M4 → money multiplier → recent events.

1. Functions and kinds of money

FunctionMeaning
PrimaryMedium of exchange (removes the need for double coincidence of wants) and measure of value / unit of account (prices of all goods stated in rupees).
SecondaryStore of value (savings can be kept for the future) and standard of deferred payments (loans repaid later in money).
ContingentBasis of credit, distribution of national income, and a guarantee of solvency.
  • Kinds: commodity money (cattle, grain), metallic money (coins), paper money, which today is fiat money (backed by the government's order, not by gold), and bank (credit) money (cheques, cards, UPI). Legal tender must be accepted in payment: notes are unlimited legal tender; coins are legal tender only up to a limit.
  • Who issues: the RBI has the sole right to issue banknotes (Section 22, RBI Act 1934), except the ₹1 note, which with all coins is issued by the Government of India (Ministry of Finance). Since 1956, India follows the Minimum Reserve System: the RBI must keep at least ₹200 crore in gold and foreign exchange (of which at least ₹115 crore in gold) against notes issued. The digital rupee (e₹), a central bank digital currency, was piloted from late 2022.

2. Measures of money supply

Measures of money supply
  • These measures were set by the RBI's Second Working Group (1977). M1 (narrow money) = currency with the public + demand deposits with banks + other deposits with the RBI; it is the most liquid. M3 (broad money) = M1 + net time deposits with banks; it is also called aggregate monetary resources and is the measure most watched. M2 and M4 add post office deposits; M4 is the least liquid. (The Reddy Working Group (1998) suggested new measures NM1–NM3, but M1 and M3 remain the main ones.)
  • M0 (reserve money), also called high-powered money, is created by the RBI: currency in circulation + banks' deposits with the RBI + other deposits with the RBI. Banks lend out part of each deposit, which is deposited again and lent again, so M3 is several times M0. The money multiplier = M3 ÷ M0; it falls when the CRR rises or when people hold more cash. Fisher's quantity theory: MV = PT (money × velocity = price level × transactions): more money without more output means higher prices.
  • Recent events: demonetisation of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes on 8 November 2016 (about 86% of currency by value); the ₹2,000 note was withdrawn from circulation in May 2023 but remains legal tender; UPI (2016, NPCI) made India a world leader in digital payments.

SCIENCE · Global warming, climate change, acid rain, ozone depletion, ocean acidification

Understand it first

What is it? These are global environmental problems that no country can solve alone. Global warming is the rise in the Earth's average temperature caused by extra greenhouse gases from human activity; it drives wider climate change (heatwaves, floods, droughts, sea-level rise). Ozone depletion is the thinning of the protective ozone layer by man-made chemicals. Acid rain and ocean acidification are both caused by gases we release dissolving in water. Learn it as greenhouse effect and gases → warming and its effects → global agreements and India → acid rain → ozone depletion → ocean acidification.

1. Greenhouse effect and global warming

How the greenhouse effect warms the Earth
GasSources and strength (global warming potential, GWP, over 100 years; CO₂ = 1)
Carbon dioxide (CO₂)Burning coal, oil and gas, deforestation, cement; the largest contributor overall; now over 420 ppm (about 280 ppm before industry).
Methane (CH₄)Paddy fields, cattle, landfills, coal mines, gas leaks; about 28 times CO₂ (much more over 20 years).
Nitrous oxide (N₂O)Nitrogen fertilisers, industry; about 270 times CO₂; also depletes ozone.
F-gases (HFCs, PFCs, SF₆)Refrigerants, aluminium making, electrical switchgear; SF₆ has the highest GWP (over 20,000). Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas but follows temperature.
  • How much warming: the IPCC (AR6) found the Earth about 1.1°C warmer (2011–2020) than pre-industrial times; 2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5°C (WMO). Effects: melting glaciers (Himalayan rivers at risk), sea-level rise (about 20 cm since 1900), stronger heatwaves, cyclones, floods and droughts, coral bleaching, lower crop yields, spread of diseases, and climate refugees. Coastal AP faces erosion (e.g. Uppada) and storm surges.

2. Global agreements and India

Milestones of global climate action
  • IPCC (1988), set up by WMO and UNEP, assesses the science. The UNFCCC (1992, Rio) is the main treaty; its annual meeting is the COP; it rests on 'common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities' (CBDR-RC). The Kyoto Protocol (1997; in force 2005) set binding cuts only for developed (Annex I) countries, covering six gases, with market tools (CDM, joint implementation, emissions trading). The Paris Agreement (2015; in force 2016) covers all countries: keep warming well below 2°C and try for 1.5°C; each country sets its own Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), updated every five years; a global stocktake every five years (first in 2023). The USA left Paris again in 2025.
  • Recent COPs: COP26 Glasgow (2021): India's Panchamrit and net zero by 2070; COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh (2022): a Loss and Damage Fund; COP28 Dubai (2023): first global stocktake; call to 'transition away from fossil fuels'; COP29 Baku (2024): a new finance goal of at least $300 billion a year by 2035 for developing countries; COP30 Belém, Brazil (November 2025). India: the National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) with eight missions (solar, energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, Himalayan ecosystem, Green India, sustainable agriculture, strategic knowledge); updated NDC (2022): 45% cut in emissions intensity and 50% non-fossil power capacity by 2030; Mission LiFE; the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (2023).

3. Acid rain, ozone depletion and ocean acidification

ProblemCause, effects and response
Acid rainSO₂ (coal, smelters) and NOx (vehicles, power plants) form sulphuric and nitric acids; rain with pH below 5.6; harms lakes, fish, forests, soil and marble buildings (Taj Mahal); controlled by flue-gas desulphurisation, cleaner fuels and BS-VI.
Ozone depletionStratospheric ozone (15–35 km) absorbs UV-B; measured in Dobson Units (about 300 DU normal; an ozone hole is below 220 DU). CFCs, halons (bromine), carbon tetrachloride and methyl bromide release chlorine and bromine when UV breaks them; one chlorine atom can destroy about 100,000 ozone molecules. Over Antarctica, polar stratospheric clouds speed this up, so an ozone hole forms each spring (September–October); it was discovered in 1985. Effects: skin cancer, cataracts, weaker immunity, harm to crops and phytoplankton. The Montreal Protocol (1987) has worked: the layer is expected to recover by about 2066 over Antarctica. World Ozone Day: 16 September.
Ocean acidificationThe oceans absorb about a quarter of the CO₂ we emit; CO₂ + water forms carbonic acid, lowering pH from about 8.2 to 8.1 since industry began (about 30% more acidic). It makes it hard for corals, shellfish, oysters and tiny plankton to build calcium carbonate shells, threatening fisheries and reefs; it is called the 'evil twin' of global warming.
The Antarctic ozone hole (blue) in September 2000, one of the largest recorded. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
