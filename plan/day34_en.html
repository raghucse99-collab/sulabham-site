GEOGRAPHY · India: soils and natural vegetation

Understand it first

What is it? Soil is the thin top layer of the land, made of broken rock (minerals), humus (decayed plants and animals), water and air. It forms very slowly from the parent rock, shaped by climate, relief, plants and animals, and time. Natural vegetation is the plant cover that grows on its own, without human planting; it depends mainly on rainfall and temperature, and on altitude in the hills. Soils decide which crops grow where; forests give timber, fuel, medicine and protect soil and water. Learn it as how soil forms → the major soil types → soil erosion and conservation → forest types by rainfall → mangroves and forest cover → AP.

1. Soils of India

Major soils of India

A laterite quarry at Angadipuram, Kerala: blocks are cut and used as bricks. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Classification: ICAR classifies India's soils into 8 major groups (the five above plus arid, saline and peaty/forest). The US (USDA) taxonomy is also used (e.g. Inceptisols, Entisols, Alfisols, Vertisols = black soils).
  • Soil erosion is the removal of topsoil by water (sheet, rill and gully erosion; gullies make ravines like the Chambal badlands) and wind (Rajasthan). Causes: deforestation, overgrazing, shifting cultivation (jhum), wrong ploughing. Conservation: contour ploughing, terrace farming (hills), strip cropping, shelter belts (rows of trees against wind), check dams, afforestation. The Soil Health Card scheme (2015) tests soil nutrients and advises fertiliser use; 5 December is World Soil Day.

2. Natural vegetation

Natural vegetation and rainfall
  • Tropical evergreen (rain) forests (rain over 200 cm): dense, tall, layered; trees shed leaves at different times so the forest is always green; Western Ghats, NE India, Andaman and Nicobar; ebony, mahogany, rosewood, rubber. Tropical deciduous (monsoon) forests (70–200 cm): the most widespread type in India; trees shed leaves in the dry season; moist (sal, teak, bamboo; eastern India, Eastern Ghats) and dry (teak, sandalwood, red sanders); Eastern Ghats of AP (Nallamala, Seshachalam, Paderu–Araku hills) are mostly deciduous.
  • Thorn forests and scrub (below 70 cm): Rajasthan, Gujarat, rain-shadow parts of the Deccan; acacia, babul, khejri, cacti, with long roots and thick, small leaves. Montane forests change with height like climate zones: wet temperate (oak, chestnut) at 1,000–2,000 m, then pine, deodar, fir, then alpine meadows and shrubs; sholas (patches of evergreen forest with grassland) in the Nilgiris, Anamalai and Palani hills. Mangroves (tidal forests) grow in salty, muddy deltas; breathing roots (pneumatophores); Sundarbans (sundari tree, Royal Bengal tiger), Bhitarkanika (Odisha), Coringa (Godavari delta), Krishna delta mangroves, Pichavaram (TN).
  • Forest cover (India State of Forest Report 2023, released Dec 2024): forest and tree cover about 25.2% of India's area (forest cover about 21.8%, tree cover about 3.4%); largest forest area: Madhya Pradesh, then Arunachal Pradesh and Chhattisgarh; highest share of area under forest: Mizoram (among States) and Lakshadweep (among UTs); mangrove cover about 4,992 sq km, with West Bengal having the largest share. The National Forest Policy 1988 aims for 33% of the land under forest (two-thirds in the hills). The report is published every two years by the Forest Survey of India (Dehradun). AP's red sanders (Pterocarpus santalinus) is endemic to the Seshachalam–Lankamala–Palakonda hills.
Mangrove forest in the Sundarbans, the world's largest mangrove area. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)

ECONOMY · RBI: functions, monetary policy and credit control

Understand it first

What is it? The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is India's central bank: the bank of the Government and of all other banks. Its main job is to keep prices stable while supporting growth. It does this through monetary policy: changing the cost and amount of money and credit in the economy. When prices rise too fast, it makes loans costlier and scarcer (tight money); when growth is weak, it makes them cheaper (easy money). Learn it as history → functions → the Monetary Policy Committee → quantitative tools → qualitative tools → current rates.

1. History and functions

  • Set up on the advice of the Hilton Young Commission (Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance, 1926) under the RBI Act, 1934; began work on 1 April 1935 in Calcutta, moved to Bombay (1937); started as a private shareholders' bank and was nationalised on 1 January 1949. First Governor Sir Osborne Smith; first Indian Governor C. D. Deshmukh; present Governor Sanjay Malhotra (26th, from December 2024). It is run by a Central Board of Directors.
Functions of the RBI
  • Besides these, the RBI does developmental work: it helped build NABARD (1982), SIDBI, EXIM Bank, the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) (deposits insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank), and promotes financial inclusion and digital payments (the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007; NPCI, which runs UPI, was set up with RBI support). It also manages the foreign exchange reserves under FEMA 1999.

2. The Monetary Policy Committee

  • On the Urjit Patel Committee (2014) report, India adopted flexible inflation targeting (Monetary Policy Framework Agreement, February 2015). The RBI Act was amended in 2016 to create a statutory Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): six members: three from the RBI (Governor as chair, a Deputy Governor, one officer) and three external members appointed by the Government for four years. Each has one vote; the Governor has a casting vote in a tie. It meets at least four times a year (in practice six). The Government, in consultation with the RBI, sets the inflation target every five years: CPI inflation 4%, with a band of 2% to 6%. If inflation stays outside the band for three quarters in a row, the RBI must explain in writing to the Government.

3. Tools of monetary policy (credit control)

Tools of monetary policy
ToolMeaning
Repo rateRate at which the RBI gives short-term (overnight) loans to banks against government securities, with an agreement to buy them back ('repurchase'). The main policy rate; set by the MPC.
SDF and reverse repoThe Standing Deposit Facility (April 2022) lets banks park surplus money with the RBI without giving securities; it is the floor of the rate corridor (repo minus 0.25%). The reverse repo (RBI borrowing against securities) still exists but is no longer the floor.
MSF and bank rateThe Marginal Standing Facility (2011) gives banks emergency overnight money at a penalty rate (repo plus 0.25%), even by dipping into their SLR securities; it is the ceiling of the corridor. The bank rate is the rate for longer loans without repurchase; now kept equal to the MSF and used for penalties.
CRR and SLRCash Reserve Ratio: share of a bank's net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) kept as cash with the RBI; earns no interest. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (under the Banking Regulation Act 1949): share of NDTL the bank keeps itself in cash, gold or approved government securities. Raising either reduces lending.
Open market operations (OMO)The RBI sells government securities to absorb money (fights inflation) and buys them to inject money. Also variable rate repo/reverse repo auctions and forex swaps manage day-to-day liquidity under the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF, 2000).
Qualitative (selective) toolsControl where credit goes, not how much: margin requirements (share of a loan the borrower must bring), credit rationing (ceilings), regulation of consumer credit, moral suasion (persuasion, advice), direct action (penalties on erring banks), priority sector lending (40% of credit for agriculture, small units, weaker sections).
  • Current settings (check the latest): the MPC raised the repo rate by 0.25% to 5.50% at its October 2026 meeting and changed its stance from 'neutral' to 'calibrated tightening'; SDF 5.25%, MSF and bank rate 5.75%. The CRR was cut to 3% in 2025 (phased, Sept–Nov 2025) and the SLR is 18%. (Before this, the repo rate had come down from 6.50% in steps through 2025.)

SCIENCE · International environmental initiatives; India's role; sustainable development

Understand it first

What is it? Since pollution, biodiversity loss and climate change cross borders, countries sign conventions (treaties) and protocols (detailed agreements under a convention) to act together. Sustainable development is the idea that ties them: growth that meets today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Day 33 covered the climate treaties (UNFCCC, Kyoto, Paris); today covers the big summits → sustainable development and the SDGs → biodiversity, chemical and ozone treaties → wetlands and wildlife → India's own global initiatives.

Milestones of global environmental action

1. Summits, sustainable development and the SDGs

  • Stockholm (1972): the UN Conference on the Human Environment, the first global environment meeting; created UNEP (HQ Nairobi); 5 June became World Environment Day; Indira Gandhi spoke of poverty as the worst polluter. The Brundtland Commission report 'Our Common Future' (1987) defined sustainable development; its three pillars are economic growth, social equity and environmental protection (people, planet, prosperity).
  • Rio Earth Summit (1992; UN Conference on Environment and Development): Agenda 21 (action plan for the 21st century), the Rio Declaration (27 principles), Forest Principles, and two conventions opened for signature: the UNFCCC and the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD); the UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) followed in 1994 (India hosted its COP14 in 2019). Rio+20 (2012), 'The Future We Want', started the process for the SDGs.
  • The Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000): 8 goals for 2015. The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), adopted in September 2015 as Agenda 2030: 17 goals, 169 targets, for all countries, with the promise to 'leave no one behind' (e.g. Goal 1 no poverty, 2 zero hunger, 6 clean water, 7 clean energy, 13 climate action, 14 life below water, 15 life on land). In India, NITI Aayog coordinates the SDGs and publishes the SDG India Index (first 2018; in the 2023–24 edition Kerala and Uttarakhand topped; check the latest edition). MoSPI tracks the national indicators.

2. Other key conventions and protocols

AreaAgreements
BiodiversityCBD (1992): conservation, sustainable use, fair sharing of benefits. Cartagena Protocol (2000): biosafety of GM (living modified) organisms. Nagoya Protocol (2010): access and benefit-sharing (ABS) from genetic resources. Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022): '30x30': protect 30% of land and sea by 2030. India: Biological Diversity Act 2002 (National Biodiversity Authority, Chennai); India hosted CBD COP11 (Hyderabad, 2012).
Chemicals and wasteBasel (1989): control of cross-border movement of hazardous wastes. Rotterdam (1998): prior informed consent (PIC) before trading dangerous chemicals and pesticides. Stockholm (2001): eliminate persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as DDT, dioxins ('dirty dozen'). Minamata (2013): mercury (named after the mercury poisoning at Minamata, Japan).
OzoneVienna Convention (1985) → Montreal Protocol (1987) to phase out CFCs, halons and other ozone-depleting substances; the most successful treaty, ratified by every UN member. Kigali Amendment (2016) to phase down HFCs, which do not harm ozone but are powerful greenhouse gases; India's schedule: freeze by 2028, 85% cut by 2047.
Wetlands and wildlifeRamsar Convention (1971, Iran): wetlands of international importance; India joined in 1982; first sites Chilika and Keoladeo (1981); India now has over 90 Ramsar sites (the number keeps rising; check); Kolleru (AP) is one. CITES (1973): controls trade in endangered plants and animals (Appendix I, II, III). CMS / Bonn Convention (1979): migratory species; India hosted CMS COP13 (Gandhinagar, 2020).

3. India's role and initiatives

  • India argues for equity and 'common but differentiated responsibilities': rich countries polluted first and must give finance and technology; India's per-capita emissions are well below the world average. India's global initiatives: the International Solar Alliance (ISA), launched by India and France at COP21 Paris (2015), HQ Gurugram, the first international body headquartered in India; 'One Sun One World One Grid' (OSOWOG); the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI, 2019), HQ New Delhi; Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment, announced at COP26, 2021; launched 2022): mindful use instead of wasteful consumption.
  • At the G20 New Delhi summit (September 2023) India launched the Global Biofuels Alliance and the Green Development Pact; the Green Credit Programme (2023) rewards voluntary actions such as tree planting and water conservation with tradable green credits; the International Big Cat Alliance (IBCA), for seven big cats (tiger, lion, leopard, snow leopard, cheetah, jaguar, puma), was launched in 2023 (HQ in India). At home: Environment (Protection) Act 1986, the National Green Tribunal (2010), and schemes like Nagar Van Yojana and 'Ek Ped Maa Ke Naam' (2024).
