GEOGRAPHY · Andhra Pradesh: physiography, climate and drainage

Understand it first

What is it? Physiography means the shape of the land: plains, hills, plateaus. For Andhra Pradesh it explains why the deltas are rice bowls, why Rayalaseema faces drought, and why the coast is hit by cyclones. AP lies on the east coast between 12°N and 19°N, bordered by Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Telangana, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and the Bay of Bengal. Learn it as location and size → three physical divisions → hills, caves and lakes → seasons and monsoons → cyclones → rivers north to south → irrigation projects.

1. Location and physical divisions

  • Size: about 1.63 lakh sq km (about 5% of India; 7th largest State by area). Coastline: officially about 974 km, the second longest after Gujarat (a newer survey measured about 1,053 km; check the figure your source uses). Districts: 26 from April 2022; Polavaram and Markapuram districts were added on 31 December 2025, making 28 (check for later changes). Capital: Amaravati.

2. Climate

Seasons and rainfall in Andhra Pradesh
  • AP has a tropical monsoon climate: hot summers, mild winters. Normal annual rain is roughly 950 mm. The south-west monsoon (June–September) brings most of the rain to the State, especially the north coast and Agency hills. The north-east (retreating) monsoon (October–December) is the main rainy season for south coastal AP (Nellore, Prakasam, Tirupati) and southern Rayalaseema, because winds from the Bay of Bengal reach them directly.
  • Anantapur is the driest district (about 550 mm; among the lowest in India after western Rajasthan) because it lies in the rain-shadow of the Western Ghats and is far from the Bay; it is drought-prone. Summer temperatures cross 45°C in Rayalaseema, Palnadu and Prakasam; heatwaves kill people most years. Cyclones form in the Bay mainly in October–December (also May): the Diviseema cyclone (1977) with a huge storm surge, Hudhud (2014, Visakhapatnam), Titli (2018, Srikakulam), Michaung (2023, near Bapatla) and Montha (October 2025, near Kakinada). The AP State Disaster Management Authority issues warnings; cyclone shelters and mangroves reduce damage.

ECONOMY · Indian banking: structure, development and reforms

Understand it first

What is it? A bank accepts deposits from people who save and gives loans to people who need money, earning the difference in interest. Banks carry savings to farms, factories and homes, so a sound banking system is the backbone of growth. India's banks grew from a few colonial banks to a vast network; nationalisation took them to villages, and the 1991 reforms made them efficient and competitive. Learn it as structure → history up to nationalisation → rural banking → reforms after 1991 → recent changes.

1. Structure

Structure of Indian banking
  • Scheduled banks are listed in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act, 1934 (paid-up capital and reserves of at least ₹5 lakh, and sound affairs); they can borrow from the RBI and must keep CRR with it. Commercial banks: public sector (12 since the April 2020 mergers: SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda, Canara, Union, Indian Bank, Bank of India, Central Bank, Indian Overseas, UCO, Bank of Maharashtra, Punjab & Sind), private (HDFC Bank, ICICI, Axis and others), foreign banks, and RRBs. Differentiated banks (on the Nachiket Mor Committee, 2013; licences 2015): small finance banks (small loans to farmers, small units; 75% priority sector) and payments banks (accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer, cannot lend; e.g. India Post Payments Bank).
  • Regional Rural Banks (RRBs): set up on the Narasimham Working Group (1975), first on 2 October 1975 (Prathama Bank, Moradabad), under the RRB Act 1976; owned Centre 50%, sponsor bank 35%, State 15%; serve small farmers, labourers and artisans. Under 'One State, One RRB', they were amalgamated into 28 RRBs from 1 May 2025; AP's four RRBs became one Andhra Pradesh Grameena Bank. Co-operative banks: urban co-operative banks, and a rural three-tier structure: State Co-operative Bank → District Central Co-operative Banks → Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS); regulated by both the RBI and State Registrars; the Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act 2020 brought them more firmly under the RBI.

2. Development up to nationalisation

Milestones of Indian banking
  • Early banks: Bank of Hindustan (1770), the first; General Bank of India (1786). The three Presidency Banks, Bank of Bengal (1806, as Bank of Calcutta), Bank of Bombay (1840) and Bank of Madras (1843), merged into the Imperial Bank of India (1921). Allahabad Bank (1865); Punjab National Bank (1894), the first bank run wholly with Indian capital, linked with Lala Lajpat Rai; the Swadeshi movement inspired more Indian banks. Andhra Bank was founded in 1923 at Machilipatnam by Dr Bhogaraju Pattabhi Sitaramayya (merged into Union Bank in 2020).
  • After Independence: the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 gave the RBI wide powers. On the All India Rural Credit Survey (Gorwala Committee, 1954), the Imperial Bank became the State Bank of India (1 July 1955); its associate banks were merged into SBI in 2017. Social control (1967), then nationalisation: 14 banks with deposits over ₹50 crore on 19 July 1969, and 6 more with deposits over ₹200 crore on 15 April 1980. Aims: take banks to villages, end control by big business, lend to agriculture and small industry (priority sector). The Lead Bank Scheme (1969) (Gadgil Study Group, Nariman Committee) gave each district a lead bank. NABARD (12 July 1982) became the apex bank for rural credit.

SCIENCE · Health: disease burden, epidemics and pandemics in India

Understand it first

What is it? Disease burden measures how much illness, disability and early death a disease causes in a population. It is counted in DALYs (Disability-Adjusted Life Years) = years lost to early death + years lived with disability. Diseases are communicable (spread by germs: TB, malaria, COVID-19) or non-communicable (NCDs) (not spread person to person: heart disease, diabetes, cancer). An epidemic is a sudden rise in cases; a pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across the world. Learn it as how burden is measured → India's health transition → major communicable diseases → successes → NCDs and nutrition → epidemics and pandemics → laws and preparedness.

1. India's health transition

  • India has gone through an epidemiological transition: in 1990, communicable, maternal, neonatal and nutritional diseases caused most of the burden; today NCDs cause about two-thirds of deaths (ICMR–PHFI India State-Level Disease Burden Initiative). Leading causes now: heart disease (ischaemic heart disease), COPD (lung disease), stroke, diabetes, with air pollution, high blood pressure, poor diet, tobacco and high blood sugar as main risk factors. India still carries a 'double burden': old infections plus new lifestyle diseases. States in earlier transition (e.g. Bihar, UP) still have more infections; Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Goa have more NCDs.

3. Epidemics and pandemics

  • Past pandemics in India: cholera pandemics in the 19th century spread from the Bengal delta; bubonic plague from Bombay (1896) led to the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897; the 1918 influenza ('Spanish flu') killed an estimated 12–20 million Indians, the worst hit of any country; H1N1 'swine flu' (2009); the 1994 Surat plague outbreak.
  • COVID-19: caused by the virus SARS-CoV-2; India's first case on 30 January 2020 (Thrissur, Kerala), the same day the WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC); the WHO called it a pandemic on 11 March 2020; national lockdown from 25 March 2020 under the Disaster Management Act, 2005; the Epidemic Diseases Act was amended (2020) to protect health workers; vaccination began 16 January 2021 (Covishield, Covaxin), over 220 crore doses through CoWIN; the WHO ended the emergency on 5 May 2023.
  • Preparedness: Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP, 2004) and its digital IHIP; National Centre for Disease Control (NCDC); ICMR–National Institute of Virology, Pune; PM Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission (PM-ABHIM, 2021) for labs and critical-care blocks; the 'One Health' approach links human, animal and environmental health (most new diseases are zoonotic). Globally, the WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2025 (check its later progress).

Assertion (A): Antibiotics are the main treatment that cures dengue.
Reason (R): Dengue is caused by a virus.
Choose the correct answer:

Tap an option to check your answer

  1. A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
  2. B. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
  3. C. A is true but R is false
  4. D. A is false but R is true

Answer: D. A is false: antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses; dengue is treated with fluids, rest and monitoring of platelets. R is true.

Practice

In some tribal forest belts, malaria cases occur at a fairly steady level year after year. How is the disease described in these areas?

Tap an option to check your answer

  1. A. Endemic
  2. B. Epidemic
  3. C. Pandemic
  4. D. Sporadic

Answer: A. A disease constantly present at a steady level in an area is endemic; a sudden rise above that level would be an epidemic.
