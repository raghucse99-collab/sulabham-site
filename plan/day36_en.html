GEOGRAPHY · Andhra Pradesh: soils and vegetation

Understand it first

What is it? Day 34 covered India's soils and forests; today applies them to Andhra Pradesh. AP's soils follow its rocks and rain: old granite gives red soils in Rayalaseema and the uplands, basalt-like and clayey deposits give black soils in the west and Palnadu, and the rivers lay rich alluvium in the Godavari–Krishna deltas. Forests follow the Eastern Ghats and rainfall: wetter, taller forests in the north, dry deciduous and thorn forests in the south-west, and mangroves on the deltas. Learn it as soil groups → soil problems → forest cover → forest types → trees and forest produce → protected areas → rare species.

  • Soil problems in AP: erosion on the Ghats and in Rayalaseema (bare slopes, heavy bursts of rain); salinity spreading in the delta lowlands as brackish-water aquaculture ponds and sea-water intrusion spread (around Kolleru, Machilipatnam, Bhimavaram); waterlogging in over-irrigated canal areas; desertification risk in Anantapur (part of India's drought-prone belt). Remedies: watershed development, farm ponds, contour bunds, afforestation, gypsum, drip irrigation; Soil Health Cards; AP's push for natural farming (APCNF, Rythu Sadhikara Samstha, since 2016), which builds soil organic matter.

2. Forests and vegetation

  • Forest cover: AP's forest cover is a little under one-fifth of its area (roughly 18–19% in the India State of Forest Report), and its recorded forest area is a little over one-fifth (about 23%); both are well below the 33% national goal. The largest forest areas are in the Nallamala belt and the northern Agency districts. (Use the latest ISFR figures for exact numbers.)

ECONOMY · Inflation: causes and remedies

Understand it first

What is it? Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, so each rupee buys less than before. One costly item does not make inflation; the average price of a basket of goods and services must keep rising. It is measured as a percentage change in a price index over a year: Inflation rate = (Index this year − Index last year) ÷ Index last year × 100. Mild, steady inflation (around 4%) is normal in a growing economy; high or very low inflation both hurt. Learn it as meaning → kinds by cause and speed → related terms → how India measures it → who gains and who loses → remedies → India's target and trend.

1. Kinds and causes

Inflation: kinds and causes
KindWhat causes it
Demand-pull'Too much money chasing too few goods': demand rises faster than output because of higher incomes, government spending and deficits, cheap credit, rising money supply, or a sudden rush (festivals, panic buying).
Cost-pushCosts of production rise and firms pass them on: crude oil prices, wages, raw materials, indirect taxes, a weaker rupee (costlier imports). A wage-price spiral (built-in inflation) happens when workers demand higher wages to match prices, which raises costs again.
Structural (India)Supply-side weaknesses: monsoon-dependent farming, poor storage and cold chains, middlemen and hoarding, weak transport. This is why food items (vegetables like tomato, onion, potato, and pulses) cause sharp price spikes in India.
  • By speed: creeping (below about 3% a year), walking/trotting (3–10%), galloping/running (double digits), hyperinflation (prices rise 50% or more a month; e.g. Germany 1923, Zimbabwe 2008, Venezuela). Related terms: deflation = a sustained fall in prices (inflation below zero; harms output, as in Japan); disinflation = the rate of inflation falls but prices still rise (e.g. from 7% to 4%); reflation = deliberate policy to lift prices and output after a slump; stagflation = high inflation + stagnant growth + high unemployment (1970s oil shocks); skewflation = prices of a few items (e.g. onions) rise sharply while others stay steady; headline inflation covers the whole basket; core inflation excludes volatile food and fuel.

2. Measuring inflation in India

How inflation is measured in India
  • WPI (Wholesale Price Index; Office of the Economic Adviser, DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce and Industry; base 2011–12) covers goods only at the first point of bulk sale: primary articles, fuel and power, manufactured products (the largest weight). CPI (Consumer Price Index; National Statistics Office, MoSPI; base 2012; a revised series with base 2024 is being introduced in 2026, so check the base year in your source) covers retail prices of goods and services that households buy; food and beverages have the largest weight. The RBI moved from WPI to CPI-Combined as its main measure in 2014 (Urjit Patel Committee), since CPI reflects what people actually pay.
  • Who gains and who loses: losers: people on fixed incomes (pensioners, salaried without DA), savers and creditors (money repaid is worth less), the poor (most of their income goes on food); exports become less competitive. Gainers: debtors (including the government), businesses whose prices rise faster than costs, holders of real assets (land, gold). Real interest rate = nominal rate − inflation. The Phillips curve shows a short-run trade-off: lower unemployment comes with higher inflation. Inflation also works as a hidden 'inflation tax' on cash holders.

3. Remedies

MeasureHow it works
Monetary (RBI)Raise the repo rate, CRR or SLR; sell government securities (OMO); tighter selective credit controls. Loans become costlier and scarcer, so demand cools. Works best on demand-pull inflation, with a time lag.
Fiscal (Government)Cut unproductive spending, reduce the fiscal deficit, raise direct taxes, cut indirect taxes/excise on fuel to lower costs, public borrowing from the market rather than the RBI.
Supply-side (direct)Release buffer stocks (FCI's Open Market Sale Scheme for wheat and rice); imports and lower import duty (pulses, edible oil); export bans/limits (e.g. on onion, wheat, non-basmati rice at times); stock limits on traders against hoarding (Essential Commodities Act, 1955); the Price Stabilisation Fund (2014–15) for onion and pulses buffers; Operation Greens (TOP) for tomato, onion, potato; PDS and subsidised sales (Bharat atta, Bharat dal); better storage and cold chains in the long run.
  • India's target and trend: under the flexible inflation targeting framework (2016), CPI inflation is to be kept at 4% within a 2–6% band. Inflation was high in 2022 (above 7% at times, after the Ukraine war pushed up fuel and food). It then fell sharply through 2025 (helped by cheap food and the GST rate cuts of September 2025), reaching record lows; by late 2026 price pressures had returned enough for the RBI to raise the repo rate in October 2026. Check the latest monthly CPI release (published around the 12th of each month).
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Base effect: a low index last year makes this year's inflation rate look high, and vice versa, even if prices barely moved. (2) GDP deflator is the widest measure (all goods and services, including exports and investment goods) but is quarterly, not monthly. (3) WPI and CPI can diverge: e.g. WPI was negative in parts of 2023 while CPI stayed around 5%, because WPI has no services and more fuel and metals. (4) Dearness Allowance for Central employees is linked to CPI-IW; MGNREGA wages to CPI-AL. (5) Inflation-indexed bonds and inflation targeting are favourite statement questions. (6) Laffer curve is about taxes, not inflation; do not confuse it with the Phillips curve.

Exam trap

SCIENCE · Healthcare delivery, outcomes and recent public health programmes

Understand it first

What is it? Healthcare delivery is how care reaches people: who gives it (government, private, AYUSH), where (sub-centre to super-speciality hospital) and who pays (government, insurance or the patient). Health outcomes are the results, measured by indicators such as infant mortality, maternal mortality and life expectancy. Health is a State subject (Seventh Schedule, State List, entry 6), but the Centre funds major missions; the Supreme Court reads the right to health into Article 21, and Article 47 asks the State to improve nutrition and public health. Learn it as the tiers → National Health Mission → Ayushman Bharat and digital health → outcome indicators → spending → programmes for mothers and children → newer missions → AP.

1. How care is delivered

Tiers of public health care
A Primary Health Centre complex in rural West Bengal: the PHC is the first doctor-level point of government care. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • The tiers follow the Bhore Committee (1946) idea of primary health centres and are set by the Indian Public Health Standards (IPHS). The National Rural Health Mission (NRHM, 2005) and National Urban Health Mission (2013) together form the National Health Mission (NHM): it created the ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist; a local woman, about one per 1,000 people, who links families to services), Rogi Kalyan Samitis, the 108 ambulance network and free drugs and diagnostics. AYUSH (Ayurveda, Yoga, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, Homoeopathy) has had its own ministry since 2014. The National Medical Commission (2020), under the NMC Act 2019, replaced the Medical Council of India.
Ayushman Bharat and digital healthDetails
Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (earlier Health and Wellness Centres, 2018)Sub-centres and PHCs upgraded to give comprehensive primary care: screening for BP, diabetes, cancers; maternal and child care; free drugs and tests; yoga; run by a Community Health Officer (CHO); renamed December 2023.
PM Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY, September 2018)The world's largest government-funded health assurance scheme: ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary hospital care, cashless and paperless, at empanelled public and private hospitals; families chosen from SECC 2011 data plus later additions; run by the National Health Authority. Since October 2024, all people aged 70 and above, whatever their income, are covered with an extra ₹5 lakh top-up via the Ayushman Vay Vandana card.
Digital and medicinesAyushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM, 2021): a 14-digit ABHA health ID and linked health records; e-Sanjeevani (free telemedicine); PM Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana (cheap generic medicines at Jan Aushadhi Kendras); PM-ABHIM (2021) for critical-care blocks and labs.

2. Outcomes and spending

IndicatorMeaning and recent level (check the latest SRS/NFHS)
Infant Mortality Rate (IMR)Deaths of infants under 1 year per 1,000 live births: about 26–27 (SRS 2021–22), down from about 80 in 1990; lowest in Kerala, highest in States like MP, UP, Chhattisgarh.
Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR)Maternal deaths per 1,00,000 live births: 93 (2019–21), down from 130 (2014–16); the SDG target is below 70 by 2030; Kerala, Maharashtra, Telangana, AP and others have already met it.
OthersUnder-5 mortality about 30, neonatal mortality about 19 (per 1,000); TFR 2.0 (NFHS-5; replacement level is 2.1); life expectancy about 70 years; institutional deliveries 88.6% and full immunisation 76.4% (NFHS-5).
SpendingThe National Health Policy 2017 set a goal of government health spending at 2.5% of GDP by 2025; actual government spending is under 2%. The out-of-pocket share of total health spending fell from about 64% (2013–14) to about 39–40% (2021–22) (National Health Accounts), still high: illness pushes many families into debt and poverty.

3. Programmes

  • Mothers and children: Janani Suraksha Yojana (2005): cash for institutional delivery; Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (2011): free delivery, C-section, drugs and transport; PM Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan (2016): free antenatal check-up by a specialist on the 9th of every month; LaQshya (2017): quality labour rooms; SUMAN (2019): assured respectful maternity care; PM Matru Vandana Yojana (2017): cash for the first child (and a second if a girl, since 2022); Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK, 2013): screens children for the '4 Ds': defects at birth, diseases, deficiencies, developmental delays; Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (2014) for adolescents; Mission Indradhanush (2014), U-WIN for vaccines.
  • Newer missions: PM National Dialysis Programme (2016): free dialysis at district hospitals; Tele-MANAS (October 2022): a 24×7 mental health helpline (14416), under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 spirit (which also decriminalised suicide attempts); National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission (July 2023, launched at Shahdol, MP): screen 7 crore people in tribal areas, eliminate as a public health problem by 2047; TB Mukt Bharat, NP-NCD screening (Day 35); Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan with Ni-kshay Mitras (community donors).
  • Andhra Pradesh: the State's own health assurance scheme began as Aarogyasri (2007, in united AP), later renamed Dr NTR Vaidya Seva (2024); it works alongside PM-JAY. AP also runs village/ward health clinics linked to the secretariats, 104 mobile medical units and 108 ambulances, and has met the SDG target for MMR. Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) IMR is per 1,000 live births; MMR is per 1,00,000 live births; mixing the bases is a classic trap. (3) Health is a State subject, but population control and family planning is in the Concurrent List. (4) Alma-Ata Declaration (1978): 'Health for All' through primary health care.
