GEOGRAPHY · Natural hazards and disasters and their management

Understand it first

What is it? A hazard is a natural event that can cause harm (a cyclone at sea, a moving fault). It becomes a disaster only when it hits people who cannot cope and causes heavy loss of life, property or livelihood beyond the community's capacity. So Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability ÷ Capacity: the same cyclone kills thousands where there are no shelters and very few where there is warning and evacuation (compare Odisha 1999 with Phailin 2013). Disaster management is everything done before, during and after to reduce that risk. Learn it as India's exposure → each hazard (cause, area, warning) → the management cycle → law and institutions → global frameworks → AP's experience.

1. India's exposure and the main hazards

  • India is one of the most disaster-prone countries. Commonly quoted figures (NDMA): about 59% of the land is prone to moderate to very high earthquakes; over 40 million hectares (about 12%) to floods; about 5,700 km of the roughly 7,500 km coastline to cyclones and tsunamis; about 68% of cultivable land to drought; and hilly areas to landslides and avalanches. Hazards are natural (geological, hydro-meteorological, biological) or man-made (industrial, chemical, nuclear, fire, stampedes).

2. Managing disasters

The disaster management cycle
Disaster management institutions
  • Law and plans: after the Odisha super cyclone (1999), the Gujarat earthquake (2001) and the 2004 tsunami, India moved from relief to risk reduction: the Disaster Management Act, 2005 created NDMA (chaired by the Prime Minister), SDMAs (Chief Ministers), DDMAs (Collector; the elected zilla parishad chairperson is co-chair), the NDRF (raised 2006, under the Ministry of Home Affairs; battalions drawn from the central armed police forces) and NIDM. The National Policy on Disaster Management (2009) and the first National Disaster Management Plan (2016, revised 2019) followed. Money: National Disaster Response Fund and State Disaster Response Funds (Centre pays 75%, or 90% for NE and Himalayan States), plus mitigation funds since the 15th Finance Commission.
  • Disaster Management (Amendment) Act, 2025: gives the NDMA and SDMAs the direct job of preparing the national and State plans; creates Urban Disaster Management Authorities for State capitals and large cities with municipal corporations; makes the State Disaster Response Force statutory; and sets up national and State disaster databases. Global frameworks: the Yokohama Strategy (1994) → Hyogo Framework (2005–2015) → Sendai Framework (2015–2030): 4 priorities (understand risk; strengthen risk governance; invest in resilience; better preparedness and 'build back better') and 7 global targets (fewer deaths, fewer people affected, lower losses, less damage to infrastructure, more national strategies, more cooperation, better early warning). India's PM gave a 10-point agenda on disaster risk reduction (2016) and set up the CDRI (2019).
  • Andhra Pradesh: the APSDMA runs a State Emergency Operations Centre with early warnings by SMS for cyclones, floods and lightning (lightning kills many farm workers in AP); hundreds of multi-purpose cyclone shelters along the coast; lessons from Diviseema (1977), Hudhud (2014), Titli (2018), Michaung (2023), Montha (2025); the Krishna and Budameru floods in Vijayawada (September 2024); heatwaves in Rayalaseema and Palnadu; drought in Anantapur; the LG Polymers gas leak (Visakhapatnam, May 2020) as a man-made disaster.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Slow-onset vs rapid-onset: drought and desertification are slow; earthquakes, cyclones, flash floods are rapid. (2) Storm surge, not wind, causes most cyclone deaths. (3) Cyclone names are given in turn from the list; a used name is never repeated. (4) 'Disaster' is not in the Seventh Schedule as a separate entry; the DM Act was passed using Entry 23 of the Concurrent List (social security and social insurance). (5) Ministry of Home Affairs is the nodal ministry for most disasters, but Agriculture for drought and Health for epidemics. (6) Odisha is often cited as a model for cyclone preparedness (zero-casualty approach).

ECONOMY · Fiscal policy: fiscal imbalance, deficit finance, FRBM

Understand it first

What is it? Fiscal policy is the Government's use of taxes, spending and borrowing to steer the economy (from Latin *fiscus*, 'treasury'). It is run by the Finance Ministry through the Budget, while monetary policy (Day 34) is run by the RBI. When the Government spends more than it earns, it has a deficit and must borrow; too much borrowing raises interest costs, can push up prices and leaves less money for the private sector. The FRBM Act sets rules to keep this in check. Learn it as what fiscal policy does → receipts and expenditure → kinds of deficit → deficit financing and its effects → FRBM and its journey → latest numbers → States.

1. Tools, aims and the Budget's parts

  • Aims: growth, price stability, employment, reducing inequality (progressive taxes, welfare spending), balanced regional development. Expansionary (loose) fiscal policy = more spending and/or lower taxes, used in a slowdown (Keynes: government spending boosts demand; the multiplier effect). Contractionary (tight) = less spending and/or higher taxes, used when the economy overheats or debt is too high. Counter-cyclical policy leans against the cycle; automatic stabilisers (tax revenue falls and welfare spending rises by itself in a slump) help.
  • Budget (Article 112, 'Annual Financial Statement') has a revenue account and a capital account. Revenue receipts do not create a liability or reduce assets: tax revenue (income tax, corporation tax, GST, customs) and non-tax revenue (RBI dividend, interest, fees). Capital receipts either create a liability (borrowings, small savings) or reduce assets (disinvestment, recovery of loans). Revenue expenditure does not create assets: interest payments (the biggest item), salaries, pensions, subsidies, grants. Capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities: roads, railways, defence equipment, loans to States, repayment of debt. Since about 2020 the Centre has sharply raised capital expenditure (about ₹11–12 lakh crore, over 3% of GDP) because it has a higher multiplier.

2. Kinds of deficit and deficit financing

Kinds of deficit
  • The fiscal deficit is the most watched: it equals the Government's total borrowing in a year. A high revenue deficit is worse than a high capital deficit, because it means borrowing to pay day-to-day bills (salaries, interest) rather than to build assets; the 'golden rule' says borrow only for investment. A primary deficit near zero means new borrowing is only paying interest on old debt. Fiscal imbalance also has a vertical side (Centre collects more, States spend more) and a horizontal side (rich vs poor States), handled by the Finance Commission (Day 32).
  • Deficit financing means covering a deficit by borrowing, especially by creating new money (borrowing from the central bank). In India's early plans it funded development but also fed inflation. Until 1997, the Government issued ad hoc treasury bills that the RBI automatically bought (automatic monetisation); an RBI–Government agreement (1994) ended this from April 1997, replacing it with limited Ways and Means Advances. The FRBM Act later barred the RBI from buying government securities in the primary market (except in special cases such as the escape clause). Effects of large deficits: inflation, higher interest rates, crowding out of private investment (Government borrowing absorbs savings), a growing interest burden, and a possible twin deficit (fiscal plus current account).

Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Fiscal deficit = borrowing, so 'total borrowing and other liabilities' is the standard Budget definition. (2) Interest payments take about two-fifths of the Centre's revenue receipts. (3) Disinvestment is a non-debt capital receipt, so it reduces the fiscal deficit. (4) Off-budget borrowing (through public sector bodies) hides the true deficit; the Centre now discloses it. (5) The Fiscal Council idea (an independent watchdog) was suggested by the N.K. SCIENCE · General science in everyday life: physics, chemistry, biology

Understand it first

What is it? APPSC asks everyday science: why the sky is blue, why ice floats, what baking soda is, why a vitamin shortage causes a disease. These questions look easy but carry traps in units, formulas and pairs. This page collects the most-asked basics in three parts. Learn it as physics (units, motion, light, sound, electricity) → chemistry (acids and bases, common compounds, metals) → biology (cells, vitamins, blood, body facts), and always link a fact to an everyday example.

1. Physics

TopicKey facts with everyday examples
Units (SI)Seven base units: metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), ampere (current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount), candela (light intensity). Derived: newton (force), joule (work, energy), watt (power), pascal (pressure), hertz (frequency), ohm (resistance), volt, coulomb. 1 kWh = 1 'unit' of electricity on your bill. Light year is a unit of distance.
Motion and forceNewton's 1st law (inertia): passengers jerk forward when a bus brakes. 2nd law: F = ma: a cricketer pulls his hands back to catch (more time, less force). 3rd law (action–reaction): rockets, swimming, a gun's recoil. Gravity: g ≈ 9.8 m/s²; weight on the Moon is about one-sixth; mass stays the same everywhere. Archimedes' principle: ice floats because it is less dense than water; ships float by displacing water.
Sound and heatSound needs a medium (no sound in space); fastest in solids, then liquids, slowest in gases (about 343 m/s in air). Echo needs at least about 17 m distance; ultrasound (above 20,000 Hz) used in scans and by bats; infrasound (below 20 Hz) by elephants. Decibel measures loudness. Heat moves by conduction (metal spoon), convection (sea breeze, boiling water) and radiation (sunlight; no medium needed). Water is densest at 4°C, so lakes freeze from the top.
Electricity and magnetismOhm's law: V = IR. Household supply in India: 220–230 V, 50 Hz AC; home wiring is in parallel (each appliance gets full voltage). A fuse (low melting point wire) or MCB breaks the circuit on overload; earthing protects from shocks. Electric motor: electrical → mechanical energy; generator (dynamo): mechanical → electrical (Faraday's electromagnetic induction). Transformers work only on AC. LED bulbs save energy.
Light in everyday life
A glass prism splits white light into its colours (dispersion); violet bends the most and red the least. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • More on light: light travels fastest in a vacuum (about 3 lakh km/s) and slows in water and glass, which is why it bends. Convex lens (converging) for long-sightedness (hypermetropia), magnifying glasses; concave lens (diverging) for short-sightedness (myopia).
