GEOGRAPHY · Natural resources of India and Andhra Pradesh and their distribution

Understand it first

What is it? A resource is anything in nature that people can use with available technology: land, water, forests, minerals, energy. Resources are renewable (sun, wind, water, forests: replaced naturally if used wisely) or non-renewable (coal, oil, minerals: formed over millions of years and used up). Minerals are found where the rocks are right: most of India's metallic minerals lie in the old Peninsular rocks, coal in Gondwana basins, and oil in young sedimentary basins. Learn it as kinds of resources → mineral belts → each mineral with its States and mines → energy → water and land → Andhra Pradesh.

2. Energy, water and land

  • Petroleum and gas: Digboi (Assam; oil struck 1889, one of the world's oldest working refineries), Mumbai High (offshore, 1974; largest field), Gujarat (Ankleshwar, Cambay), Rajasthan (Barmer, Mangala), Krishna–Godavari basin (gas). India imports about 85% of its crude oil. Nuclear: Tarapur (1969), Rawatbhata, Kalpakkam, Kudankulam (largest), Kaiga, Narora, Kakrapar. Renewables: solar (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Karnataka, AP), wind (Gujarat, TN, Karnataka); in June 2025 India reached 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources, five years ahead of its 2030 NDC goal; the target is 500 GW non-fossil by 2030.
  • Water: India has about 18% of the world's people but only about 4% of its fresh water; per-capita availability is about 1,500 m³ a year (below 1,700 = water-stressed; below 1,000 = scarce); agriculture uses about 80%; groundwater is over-used in Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan; Jal Jeevan Mission (2019), Atal Bhujal Yojana (2020), Amrit Sarovar (2022). Land: about 43% of the area is net sown, about a quarter under forest and tree cover; land degradation affects about 30% (ISRO atlas).

3. Andhra Pradesh

Andhra Pradesh: main resources
  • Minerals: barytes at Mangampet (YSR Kadapa; among the world's largest single deposits; AP supplies most of India's output; used in oil-well drilling mud); limestone (Nandyal–Kurnool, YSR Kadapa, Palnadu around Piduguralla, and the Yerraguntla cement area; AP is a major cement State); granite (Chimakurthy 'galaxy' granite, Prakasam; also Srikakulam, Chittoor); mica (Gudur–Nellore belt); uranium at Tummalapalle (YSR Kadapa; mined by UCIL); heavy-mineral beach sands (ilmenite, monazite, zircon) along the coast from Srikakulam to Nellore; manganese in Vizianagaram–Srikakulam; bauxite in the Eastern Ghats (Alluri Sitharama Raju; not mined because of tribal and forest concerns); diamond-bearing kimberlite at Wajrakarur (Anantapur) and the historic Golconda diamonds from the Kollur mines on the Krishna (the Koh-i-Noor); quartz, feldspar, steatite (Kadapa).
  • Energy and water: KG basin oil and gas (ONGC, Reliance's KG-D6); Visakhapatnam has the HPCL refinery and the Vizag Steel Plant (RINL); large solar parks in Kurnool and Anantapur (NP Kunta) and wind farms in Rayalaseema; pumped-storage projects (e.g. Pinnapuram, integrated renewable project); thermal plants at Vijayawada (Dr NTTPS), Krishnapatnam, Simhadri; hydel at Srisailam and Lower Sileru. Water resources come from the Godavari and Krishna systems (see Day 35) plus thousands of tanks, especially in Rayalaseema.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Gondwana vs Tertiary coal: about 98% of India's coal is Gondwana; Tertiary coal is in the NE (Assam, Meghalaya). (2) Anthracite has the highest carbon, then bituminous, lignite, peat. (3) Mineral rights: the Supreme Court (2024, Mineral Area Development Authority case) held that States can tax mineral rights; royalty is not a tax. (4) MMDR Act 1957 (amended 2015: auction of mines, District Mineral Foundations). (5) Rare earths are handled by IREL (Indian Rare Earths Ltd). (6) Copper and gold: India depends heavily on imports, so 'largest producer' traps are common.

ECONOMY · Indian tax structure and GST

Understand it first

What is it? A tax is a compulsory payment to the Government without a direct return for the payer; it funds public services. Under Article 265, no tax can be levied or collected except by authority of law. Taxes are direct (the person on whom it is levied bears it: income tax) or indirect (the burden is passed on to the buyer: GST). The Seventh Schedule divides taxing powers between the Union and States, and GST (2017) pooled most indirect taxes into one. Learn it as principles and kinds → who taxes what → direct taxes → GST in detail → reforms and numbers → AP's own taxes.

1. Kinds of taxes and who levies them

India's tax structure
  • By rate: progressive (rate rises with income: income tax slabs; promotes equity), proportional (same rate for all), regressive (takes a larger share from the poor: many indirect taxes on necessities). Direct taxes are progressive and harder to evade but cover fewer people; indirect taxes are broad-based but can be regressive. Tax buoyancy = % change in tax revenue ÷ % change in GDP (includes effect of rate changes); elasticity excludes rate changes. The Laffer curve says very high rates can reduce revenue.
  • Seventh Schedule: Union List: taxes on income other than agricultural income, corporation tax, customs, excise on tobacco and some goods, and CGST/IGST. State List: agricultural income tax, land revenue, excise on alcohol for human consumption, stamp duty (except on financial documents), taxes on vehicles, electricity consumption, sales tax on petroleum products and liquor, and SGST. Local bodies collect property tax and professional tax (Article 276, cap ₹2,500 a year). India's tax-to-GDP ratio (Centre + States) is about 18%; the Centre's gross tax is about 11–12% of GDP; direct taxes now bring in more than indirect taxes for the Centre.
Direct taxKey facts
Personal income taxTwo regimes: the new regime (lower rates, few deductions; default since 2023–24) and the old regime (with deductions like 80C). From Budget 2025, no tax on income up to ₹12 lakh under the new regime (₹12.75 lakh for salaried people with the ₹75,000 standard deduction). The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026 with simpler language (it introduces a single 'tax year').
Corporation tax and othersSeptember 2019 cut: 22% for domestic companies giving up exemptions (about 25% with surcharge and cess). Securities Transaction Tax (2004); TDS/TCS; faceless assessment (2020); Vivad se Vishwas schemes for settling disputes. Abolished: estate duty (1985), gift tax (1998), wealth tax (Budget 2015), the equalisation levy on online ads (from April 2025).

2. Goods and Services Tax (GST)

How GST works
  • Basis: the 101st Constitutional Amendment (2016) added Article 246A (both Parliament and State legislatures can make GST laws), 269A (IGST on inter-State supply, levied and collected by the Centre and shared), and 279A (GST Council). GST began on 1 July 2017 (midnight session of Parliament), replacing about 17 taxes (central excise, service tax, VAT, entry tax, octroi and others). It is a destination-based consumption tax: tax goes to the State where goods are consumed. Input tax credit removes the cascading ('tax on tax') effect. The idea came from the Kelkar Task Force (2003); France was the first country to adopt VAT/GST (1954).
  • GST Council: chaired by the Union Finance Minister; all State Finance Ministers are members; the Centre has 1/3 of the votes and all States together 2/3; a decision needs 3/4 of weighted votes; the Supreme Court (Mohit Minerals, 2022) said its recommendations are persuasive, not binding. GST 2.0 (56th Council meeting, effective 22 September 2025): mainly two slabs, 5% and 18%, a special 40% rate on sin and luxury goods (e.g. pan masala, tobacco, large cars), and 0% on many essentials; the compensation cess to States (promised for five years till June 2022, later used to repay COVID loans) was wound down. Outside GST for now: alcohol for drinking, petrol, diesel, ATF, crude oil, natural gas (in GST law but the Council has not fixed a date), electricity. Systems: GSTN (IT backbone), e-way bill (2018), e-invoicing, and the GST Appellate Tribunal (began functioning in 2025).
  • Andhra Pradesh's own taxes: the largest is SGST, followed by sales tax/VAT on petroleum products and liquor, State excise on liquor, stamps and registration, and motor vehicle tax; AP also gets a share of central taxes (about 4% under the Finance Commission) and grants. A registration threshold applies to small businesses (about ₹40 lakh turnover for goods, ₹20 lakh for services in most States), and a composition scheme (turnover up to ₹1.5 crore) lets small traders pay a flat low rate.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Cess and surcharge are not shared with States (Article 270/271), so their rising share is a federal complaint. (2) Customs duty stays outside GST; IGST is also charged on imports. (3) GST on alcohol for human consumption needs a constitutional amendment; petroleum needs only a Council decision. (4) 'One Nation, One Tax' is the slogan; the GST Council is a constitutional body, the GSTN is a not-for-profit company. (5) Tax expenditure = revenue forgone due to exemptions. (6) Pigouvian taxes (on pollution, tobacco) correct harmful behaviour; GST 2.0's 40% slab is an example.

Exam trap
