GEOGRAPHY · Major industries and industrial regions

Understand it first

What is it? Industry (manufacturing) turns raw materials into more valuable goods: iron ore into steel, cotton into cloth, sand into chips. It creates jobs and exports and is the secondary sector of the economy (about a quarter of India's GVA, with manufacturing about 15–17%). An industry is located where its costs are lowest: near raw materials (if they lose weight in processing, like steel and sugar), near markets (if the product is bulky or perishable), or where power, water, labour, transport, capital and government support are available. Learn it as location factors → kinds and policies → each major industry and its centres → industrial regions and corridors → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Kinds and policies

  • Kinds: by raw material (agro-based: cotton, jute, sugar, food; mineral-based: steel, cement, aluminium), by ownership (public, private, joint, co-operative like Amul), by size (large, MSME), by role (basic/key industries like steel that feed others; consumer industries). MSME definition (revised 2020, raised again in Budget 2025): based on investment and turnover together; micro up to about ₹2.5 crore investment and ₹10 crore turnover, small up to ₹25 crore / ₹100 crore, medium up to ₹125 crore / ₹500 crore (check the latest). MSMEs employ the most people after agriculture.
  • Policies: Industrial Policy Resolution 1948 (mixed economy); 1956 (the 'economic constitution': Schedule A 17 industries for the State, Schedule B mixed, Schedule C private; heavy-industry focus of the Mahalanobis Second Plan); licence-permit raj; New Industrial Policy 1991 (licensing abolished except a few; public-sector reservation cut; see Day 31). Later: Make in India (2014), Production Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes in 14 sectors (2020), National Manufacturing Mission (Budget 2025), PM Gati Shakti (2021) for logistics, National Logistics Policy (2022). Index of Industrial Production (IIP), base 2011–12, by NSO; the eight core industries (coal, crude oil, natural gas, refinery products, fertilisers, steel, cement, electricity) make up about 40% of IIP, with refinery products the heaviest.

ECONOMY · Balance of Payments (BOP) and FDI

Understand it first

What is it? The Balance of Payments is a record of all economic transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world in a year: goods, services, income, gifts and capital flows. Like a household's accounts, money coming in (exports, remittances, foreign investment) is a credit and money going out (imports, investment abroad) a debit. Overall, the BOP always balances: any gap is met by changes in foreign exchange reserves. Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) is long-term investment by foreigners with a say in management, and is the most stable way to finance a deficit. Learn it as the accounts → India's pattern → the 1991 crisis and convertibility → exchange rate and reserves → latest numbers → FDI vs FPI → FDI rules and trends.

1. The accounts and India's pattern

Balance of Payments: the accounts
  • Current account = trade in goods (visible) + invisibles: services (software, travel, transport), primary income (interest, dividends, wages) and secondary income (transfers, mainly remittances). Capital (and financial) account = FDI, FPI, external commercial borrowing (ECB), external aid, NRI deposits, trade credit. Errors and omissions cover gaps. A current account deficit (CAD) must be financed by capital inflows or by using reserves.
  • India's pattern: a large merchandise trade deficit (imports of crude oil, gold, electronics, coal) is partly offset by a big services surplus (IT and business services) and remittances (India is the world's largest recipient, over $130 billion a year). So the CAD is usually small: about 0.6% of GDP in both 2024–25 ($22.9 bn) and 2025–26 ($25.2 bn); India even ran a current account surplus in some quarters (e.g. $7.1 billion in January–March 2026). In 2025–26, FPI saw a net outflow of about $16 billion and net FDI was modest, so reserves fell on a BOP basis. A CAD above about 2.5–3% of GDP is seen as risky.

2. Crisis, convertibility, exchange rate and reserves

  • 1991 crisis (Day 31): reserves fell to about two weeks of imports; gold was pledged; the rupee was devalued. Reforms: the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS, 1992) with dual rates, then a market-determined exchange rate (1993); full current account convertibility (August 1994), when India accepted IMF Article VIII; capital account convertibility only partial (Tarapore Committees, 1997 and 2006). FERA (1973) gave way to FEMA (1999): foreign exchange offences became civil, not criminal.
  • Exchange rate: India has a managed float: the market sets the rupee's value, but the RBI buys or sells dollars to smooth sharp swings. Depreciation (rupee weaker) makes exports cheaper abroad and imports costlier; it can raise inflation via oil. Devaluation is a deliberate cut under a fixed system. REER/NEER compare the rupee with a basket of currencies. Forex reserves (foreign currency assets, gold, SDRs, IMF reserve position) were around $650–700 billion in 2025–26 (check the RBI's weekly data); India is among the top five holders. Rupee trade settlement (2022) and UPI links abroad promote the rupee's international use.

3. Foreign Direct Investment

FDI in India: routes and limits
PointDetails
FDI vs FPIAn investment of 10% or more of a listed company's equity is FDI (lasting interest, management say, brings technology and jobs); below 10% is FPI (portfolio: shares and bonds, 'hot money' that can leave quickly). FPIs are regulated by SEBI; FDI policy by DPIIT (Commerce Ministry) and FEMA rules by the RBI.
TrendsGross FDI inflows are about $80 billion a year, but net FDI has fallen sharply because of repatriation by foreign investors and Indian firms investing abroad (net FDI about $1 bn in 2024–25 and $6.9 bn in 2025–26). Top sources: Singapore, Mauritius, USA, Netherlands, Japan, UAE. Top sectors: services, computer software and hardware, trading, telecom, autos, non-conventional energy. Top States: Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat, Delhi, Tamil Nadu.
Other flowsECB (foreign loans to Indian firms, regulated by the RBI); NRI deposits (FCNR, NRE); Overseas Direct Investment by Indian firms; Bilateral Investment Treaties (model BIT 2016); India's largest trading partners are the USA (largest export market), China (largest source of imports), UAE.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Remittances are secondary income in the current account, not capital account. (2) Sterilisation: when the RBI buys dollars it releases rupees, then absorbs them via OMOs. (3) Impossible trinity: a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows and independent monetary policy all at once. (4) J-curve: after depreciation, the trade balance first worsens, then improves. (5) Participatory notes (P-notes) are offshore derivatives used by FPIs. (6) 'Twin deficit' = fiscal deficit + CAD. (7) SDR basket: US dollar, euro, Chinese yuan, yen, pound.
