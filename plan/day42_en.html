GEOGRAPHY · Tourism and trade

Understand it first

What is it? Tourism is people travelling for leisure, pilgrimage, health, business or study and spending money where they go; it is a service export when foreigners spend in India. Trade is the exchange of goods and services: internal trade within India and international trade across borders. In exams, trade is asked through three ideas: volume (how much), composition (what we buy and sell) and direction (with whom). The balance of trade = exports − imports of goods; India has a large goods deficit but a big services surplus. Learn it as tourism types and numbers → schemes → AP tourism → trade basics → India's trade figures → policy and agreements → AP's exports.

India's trade in 2025–26 at a glance

1. Tourism in India

  • Numbers: Foreign Tourist Arrivals (FTAs) were about 9.02 million in 2025 (provisional; about 9.95 million in 2024); foreign exchange earnings about ₹2.74 lakh crore (2025). Domestic tourist visits are far larger, close to 300 crore a year; domestic tourists are the backbone of the sector. Top source countries of FTAs: Bangladesh, USA, UK. Tourism's share of GDP is about 5% (Tourism Satellite Account) and it gives about 7–8 crore jobs. India ranked 39th in the WEF Travel and Tourism Development Index 2024.
  • Kinds of tourism: heritage (India has 44 UNESCO World Heritage Sites; the 44th, the Maratha Military Landscapes, was added in 2025), spiritual/pilgrimage (Tirumala, Varanasi, Bodh Gaya), eco and wildlife, adventure (Himalaya, rafting), medical and wellness ('Heal in India', AYUSH visa), MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences, exhibitions: Bharat Mandapam, Delhi), rural (UNWTO Best Tourism Villages: Pochampally 2021, Dhordo 2023), cruise (Cruise Bharat Mission, 2024), film and culinary tourism.
  • Schemes and bodies: Ministry of Tourism; ITDC (1966); 'Incredible India' campaign (2002); e-Tourist Visa (2014); Swadesh Darshan (2014–15) → Swadesh Darshan 2.0 (2023) for theme circuits and destinations; PRASHAD (2014–15) for pilgrimage sites; Dekho Apna Desh (2020); Adopt a Heritage – Apni Dharohar Apni Pehchaan (2017); Iconic tourist sites and Challenge-Based Destination Development. Days: National Tourism Day 25 January, World Tourism Day 27 September; UN Tourism (formerly UNWTO, HQ Madrid).
  • Andhra Pradesh tourism: Tirumala–Tirupati (one of the world's most visited shrines), Srisailam, Srikalahasti, Simhachalam, Annavaram; Buddhist circuit: Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda, Salihundam, Thotlakonda, Bavikonda, Sankaram; nature: Araku, Lambasingi, Borra caves, Belum caves, Gandikota, Horsley Hills, Papikondalu, Konaseema, Talakona; beaches RK Beach (Vizag), Rushikonda (Blue Flag); heritage Lepakshi (on UNESCO's tentative list). The AP Tourism Policy 2024–29 gives tourism industry status and promotes homestays, caravans and PPP projects.

2. Trade: basics and India's numbers

  • Basic terms: Balance of trade = goods exports − goods imports (favourable if positive). Balance of payments also counts services, income, transfers and capital (Day 40). Terms of trade = export prices ÷ import prices. Internal trade: within the country (wholesale, retail, GST made India 'one market'); international trade: bilateral (two countries) or multilateral (WTO). Ports, roads and rail (Day 41) are its arteries.
  • India's trade, 2025–26: total exports (goods + services) about $860 billion (up from $825 billion); merchandise exports about $442 billion, merchandise imports about $776 billion → goods deficit about $333 billion; services exports about $418 billion with a services surplus about $214 billion; overall trade deficit about $119 billion. Services exports may soon overtake goods exports. India's share of world goods exports is under 2%; of world services exports about 4–5%.

3. Trade policy, agreements and AP

  • Policy: Foreign Trade Policy 2023 (from 1 April 2023, no end date; goal $2 trillion exports by 2030); DGFT administers it; Districts as Export Hubs; RoDTEP (2021) refunds hidden taxes on exports (WTO-compatible); Advance Authorisation, EPCG; SEZ Act 2005 and EOUs; Export Promotion Mission (Budget 2025); ECGC insures exporters; EXIM Bank (1982) finances trade. The Foreign Trade (Development and Regulation) Act, 1992 is the base law.
  • Trade agreements: SAFTA (2006); India–ASEAN FTA (2010); CEPAs with Singapore (2005), South Korea, Japan (2011); India–UAE CEPA (2022); India–Australia ECTA (2022); India–EFTA TEPA (signed March 2024, in force 1 October 2025; EFTA pledged $100 billion investment); India–UK CETA (signed 24 July 2025); India–EU FTA concluded 27 January 2026 (called the 'mother of all deals'; tariffs cut on most Indian exports). India stayed out of RCEP (2019). WTO (1995, Geneva): India opposes limits on food stockholding (public stockholding, 'peace clause' 2013). High US tariffs on many Indian goods in 2025 pushed India to diversify markets.
  • Andhra Pradesh in trade: AP is a big exporter of marine products (mainly shrimp; India's largest aquaculture State), pharma (Visakhapatnam's Pharma City), rice (Kakinada port), chillies and spices, steel (Vizag), tobacco, granite and iron ore transit via Vizag. Kakinada port is a major rice-export hub. The AP Export Promotion Policy and the State's ports aim to raise AP's share in India's exports.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Invisible trade = services, remittances, income; India is the largest recipient of remittances in the world (about $135 billion in 2024–25). (2) Dumping and anti-dumping duty (DGTR); safeguard duty; countervailing duty. (3) Rupee trade settlement (RBI, 2022) and Special Rupee Vostro Accounts. (4) CBAM (EU carbon tax on imports from 2026) worries Indian steel and aluminium exporters. (5) GI tags help exports (Darjeeling tea, the first GI, 2004; AP's Banganapalle, Araku coffee). (6) Tourism terms: ecotourism must benefit locals; carrying capacity; over-tourism in hill towns. (7) 'Vocal for local' and ODOP (One District One Product). (8) The India–Middle East–Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC, 2023) and INSTC (via Iran) are new trade routes.

ECONOMY · Agricultural finance and marketing

Understand it first

What is it? A farmer needs money before harvest (seeds, fertiliser, labour) and a fair price after harvest. Agricultural finance is how that money reaches the farmer: short-term loans (up to 15 months, for a crop season), medium-term (1–5 years: pump-sets, cattle) and long-term (over 5 years: land development, tractors). It comes from non-institutional sources (moneylenders, traders, relatives: costly and exploitative) or institutional ones (cooperatives, commercial banks, RRBs, NABARD refinance, SHGs). Agricultural marketing is everything from the field to the buyer: assembling, grading, storage, transport, selling. A weak market means distress sales and middlemen's cuts. Learn it as credit needs → sources and their history → KCC, interest and insurance → marketing problems → APMC, e-NAM, MSP and procurement → storage and reforms → AP.

How credit reaches the farmer

1. Agricultural credit

  • History: Cooperative Credit Societies Act 1904 began cooperatives; the All India Rural Credit Survey (1951–54, A.D. Gorwala) found moneylenders gave about 70% of rural credit and cooperatives only about 3%; it said 'cooperation has failed, but cooperation must succeed' and led to SBI (1955). Bank nationalisation (1969, 1980), Lead Bank Scheme (1969), priority-sector lending, RRBs (1975) and NABARD (12 July 1982, on the CRAFICARD / B. Sivaraman Committee) followed. The SHG–Bank Linkage Programme (1992) by NABARD brought small loans to rural women.
  • Size today: institutional farm credit grew from about ₹6 lakh crore (2012–13) to about ₹25 lakh crore (2023–24). Banks must lend 18% of adjusted net bank credit to agriculture, with 10% for small and marginal farmers. Commercial banks now give the largest share; cooperatives follow a three-tier short-term structure (StCB → DCCB → PACS); NABARD refinances banks and runs the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund (RIDF, 1995–96). A Ministry of Cooperation (2021) and computerisation of PACS aim to revive cooperatives.
  • Kisan Credit Card (KCC, 1998, R.V. Gupta Committee): one card for crop loans, flexible withdrawal; extended to animal husbandry and fisheries (2018–19); about 7.7 crore operational cards (March 2025). Under the Modified Interest Subvention Scheme (MISS) short-term crop loans are at 7%, with 3% extra for prompt repayment → effective 4%; the subsidised limit was raised from ₹3 lakh to ₹5 lakh (Budget 2025–26). Collateral-free farm loans up to ₹2 lakh (RBI, 2025).
  • Risk and income support: PMFBY (2016): farmer premium only 2% (kharif), 1.5% (rabi), 5% (commercial/horticulture); about 28% of gross cropped area insured (2022); RWBCIS for weather-based cover. PM-KISAN (February 2019): ₹6,000 a year in three instalments to landholding families by DBT. Agriculture Infrastructure Fund (2020, ₹1 lakh crore): loans with interest relief for storage and post-harvest units. Loan waivers by States give short relief but hurt credit culture.

2. Agricultural marketing

From field to buyer: the marketing chain
  • Problems: distress sale right after harvest (no storage, debt), too many middlemen, lack of grading, poor storage and transport, little market information, unregulated village sales, malpractices in weighing. The National Commission on Farmers (Swaminathan) wanted a market within 5 km; India has only about 7,085 regulated APMC markets and about 22,000 rural haats (to be upgraded as GrAMs, Gramin Agricultural Markets).
  • Regulated markets: States pass APMC Acts (agriculture marketing is a State subject); the Centre gave the Model APMC Act (2003) and the Model APLM Act (2017) (one State one market licence, private markets, e-trading). e-NAM (14 April 2016, run by SFAC): an online national market; about 1,522 mandis in 23 States and 4 UTs (January 2026). The three farm laws (2020) were repealed in December 2021 after farmer protests. Agmark grading (Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act, 1937); NAFED (1958) for cooperative marketing and procurement.

Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Scale of finance = loan per acre fixed by a district-level committee for each crop. (2) Microfinance: MFIs, JLGs (joint liability groups); NABARD's SHG model vs Bangladesh's Grameen Bank (Muhammad Yunus). (3) Farm debt: NSO's Situation Assessment Survey (2019) found about 50% of agricultural households in debt, average about ₹74,121; AP and Telangana among the highest. (4) MSP cost terms: A2 (paid-out costs), A2+FL (+ family labour), C2 (+ rent and interest on own land and capital). (5) Marketable surplus vs marketed surplus: what can be sold vs what is actually sold. (6) Cold chain gaps cause large post-harvest losses (fruits and vegetables about 5–15%). (7) ONDC for farmers, e-NAM–ONDC links. (8) Agricultural income is exempt from Union income tax; agriculture is a State subject, but 'trade and commerce' and food items sit in the Concurrent List (entry 33).
