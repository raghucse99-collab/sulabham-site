GEOGRAPHY · Human development and demographics

Understand it first

What is it? Demography is the study of population: how many people there are, how they are spread, and how they change through births, deaths and migration. Key rates are counted per 1,000 people: birth rate (CBR), death rate (CDR), infant mortality (IMR, deaths under one year per 1,000 live births). Total Fertility Rate (TFR) is the average number of children a woman would have in her life; 2.1 is the replacement level (population stays stable in the long run). Human development asks whether people live long, healthy, educated lives with a decent income (the HDI). India is the most populous country and is now in a phase of falling fertility and a large working-age population (the demographic dividend). Learn it as census history → Census 2011 numbers → Census 2027 → demographic transition and SRS rates → population policy → HDI → Andhra Pradesh.

1. The Census of India

  • History: first census 1872 (non-synchronous, under Lord Mayo); first synchronous census 1881 (Lord Ripon, Census Commissioner W.C. Plowden); held every 10 years since. 1921 is the 'year of the great divide': the only decade (1911–21) when population fell (influenza 1918, famines). 1951: first census of free India. The Census Act 1948 is the legal base; Census is a Union List subject (entry 69); done by the Registrar General and Census Commissioner (Home Ministry). Census 2011 was the 15th census.
  • Phases of growth: 1901–21 stagnant; 1921–51 steady growth; 1951–81 rapid growth ('population explosion'), falling death rate but high birth rate; 1981–2011 high growth with a definite slowing. Decadal growth 24.8% (1961–71, highest) → 17.7% (2001–11).
  • Census 2027 (16th; 8th after Independence): India's first digital census (mobile app, self-enumeration portal in 16 languages) and the first to count caste (jati) since 1931 (decided 30 April 2025). Phase 1, Houselisting: 1 April–30 September 2026 (a 30-day window in each State, completed in stages). Phase 2, Population Enumeration: 9–28 February 2027 with reference date 1 March 2027; snow-bound areas (Ladakh, J&K, HP, Uttarakhand) in September 2026 (reference date 1 October 2026). Provisional results are expected late 2027. The fresh data will also be used for the delimitation of seats (frozen till the first census after 2026).

2. Demographic transition, current rates and policy

The demographic transition model
  • Demographic transition: Stage 1 high birth and death rates, slow growth; Stage 2 death rate falls (medicine, food), birth rate stays high → population explosion; Stage 3 birth rate also falls (education, urbanisation, family planning); Stage 4 both low, stable; (Stage 5 births below deaths, population shrinks, e.g. Japan). India is in late stage 3, moving to stage 4; southern States are already there.
  • Latest rates (SRS 2023, Registrar General): TFR 1.9 (below replacement; Bihar 2.8 highest, Delhi 1.2 lowest); birth rate 18.4; death rate 6.4; IMR 25 (Kerala 5; MP, UP, Chhattisgarh 37); sex ratio at birth 917 (2021–23). NFHS-5 (2019–21): TFR 2.0, and for the first time more women than men (1,020 per 1,000) in the surveyed population. Life expectancy about 70 years. India became the most populous country in 2023, overtaking China (UN estimate about 146 crore in 2025); population is expected to peak around 170 crore in the early 2060s.
  • Population policy: India started the world's first national family planning programme (1952); the Emergency (1975–77) saw coercive sterilisation, which set the programme back; renamed Family Welfare (1977). National Population Policy 2000: immediate aim contraception and health infrastructure; medium-term TFR 2.1 by 2010; long-term stable population by 2045; National Commission on Population (2000, PM chairs). The 42nd Amendment froze Lok Sabha seats on the 1971 census (extended by the 84th to after 2026) so that States controlling population are not penalised. Mission Parivar Vikas (2016) for high-TFR districts.
  • Demographic dividend and ageing: about two-thirds of Indians are of working age (15–64); median age about 28–29; the dividend window lasts roughly till the 2040s if jobs, skills and health keep pace. The share of people 60+ is about 10–11% and rising (India Ageing Report 2023: about 20% by 2050); southern States age first. Migration (Census 2011): about 45 crore migrants (37%); marriage is the main reason for women, work for men; the biggest flows are from UP and Bihar to Maharashtra, Delhi and Gujarat.

3. Human development and Andhra Pradesh

  • HDI (UNDP, 1990, Mahbub ul Haq with Amartya Sen): life expectancy, education (mean and expected years of schooling) and GNI per capita (PPP), combined as a geometric mean. HDR 2025: India 0.685, rank 130 of 193 (medium human development); life expectancy 72 years. Inequality cuts India's HDI by about 30%; India's Gender Inequality Index rank is about 102. Within India, Kerala leads; Bihar is lowest. India's multidimensional poverty fell from about 29% (2013–14) to about 11% (2022–23) (NITI Aayog).
  • Andhra Pradesh (Census 2011, present 13 old districts): population about 4.94 crore (10th); sex ratio about 996; literacy about 67% (below the national average; West Godavari highest, Vizianagaram lowest); urban about 30%; density about 303; STs about 5.5%, SCs about 17%. AP's TFR is about 1.5 (well below replacement), so the State worries about ageing: in 2024 it removed the two-child rule for contesting local body elections and now encourages larger families. AP ranks near the middle on State HDI, ahead of the national average on health indicators like IMR.

Other angles asked: (1) Sex ratio in India is females per 1,000 males; internationally it is males per 100 females. (2) Literacy in the Census counts age 7+ who can read and write with understanding; first Census literacy (1951) was about 18%. (3) Dependency ratio = (0–14 + 65+) ÷ (15–64). (4) Census town = population ≥ 5,000, density ≥ 400/km² and ≥ 75% of male workers in non-farm work; statutory town = has a municipality. (5) NPR (National Population Register) is updated with the census (Citizenship Rules 2003). (6) Civil Registration System: Registration of Births and Deaths (Amendment) Act 2023 makes the birth certificate a single document for many services (from 1 October 2023). (7) Missing women and the PCPNDT Act 1994 (sex-selection ban); Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (2015, Panipat). (8) Tribal populations: Bhils largest tribe, Gonds second; PVTGs 75.

ECONOMY · Agricultural pricing and food policy: MSP, procurement, PDS

Understand it first

What is it? Farm prices swing because harvests depend on the weather and crops come to market all at once. If prices crash, farmers lose; if they shoot up, poor consumers suffer. So the government runs an agricultural price policy: it announces a Minimum Support Price (MSP) before sowing, procures grain at that price through the Food Corporation of India (FCI) and the States, stores it as a buffer stock, and distributes it cheaply (now free) through the Public Distribution System (PDS) under the National Food Security Act. This one chain links farmer income, food security, inflation and the fiscal deficit (food subsidy). Yesterday (Day 42) we saw markets and MSP basics; today we learn how MSP is fixed, who buys, how much is stored, who gets it, what it costs and how it should be reformed.

1. How MSP is fixed

  • Objectives of price policy: (1) incentive price to farmers to adopt new technology and raise output; (2) protect farmers from distress sale after harvest; (3) protect consumers from high prices; (4) build buffer stocks for food security; (5) guide the crop pattern (e.g. higher MSP for pulses and oilseeds to cut imports).
  • CACP: set up in 1965 as the Agricultural Prices Commission (on the L.K. Jha Committee's advice, 1964); renamed Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices in 1985. It is an attached office of the Ministry of Agriculture, an advisory body (not statutory). It sends five price-policy reports a year (kharif, rabi, sugarcane, raw jute, copra). The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA), chaired by the Prime Minister, takes the final decision.
  • 22 mandated crops: 14 kharif (paddy, jowar, bajra, maize, ragi, tur, moong, urad, groundnut, soybean, sunflower, sesamum, nigerseed, cotton), 6 rabi (wheat, barley, gram, masur, rapeseed-mustard, safflower) and 2 commercial (jute, copra). For sugarcane the Centre fixes the Fair and Remunerative Price (FRP) (since 2009, under the Sugarcane (Control) Order 1966); some States add a higher State Advised Price (SAP).
  • Factors CACP weighs: cost of production, demand and supply, domestic and international prices, inter-crop price parity, terms of trade between farm and non-farm sectors, a minimum 50% margin over cost, and the effect on consumers and the economy. Since the Budget 2018–19, MSP is fixed at at least 1.5 times the A2+FL cost. Recent MSPs: paddy (common) ₹2,369 per quintal (kharif 2025–26); wheat ₹2,585 (rabi 2026–27).
Cost concepts used by CACP
  • Is MSP a legal right? No. MSP is an administrative announcement; no law makes buying below MSP illegal. The 2020–21 farmers' protest demanded a legal guarantee of MSP; after the three farm laws were repealed (Dec 2021), a committee under Sanjay Agrawal (2022) was set up on MSP and crop diversification. Exception: sugarcane FRP is legally binding on mills.
  • Who really gets MSP? Effective procurement is mostly of paddy and wheat, from a few States (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Telangana, Chhattisgarh, AP, Odisha). The Shanta Kumar Committee found only about 6% of farm households sold to a procurement agency. For pulses and oilseeds, MSP works through PM-AASHA. This tilt pushes the wheat-rice cycle, overuse of groundwater (Punjab) and stubble burning.

  • Food subsidy = economic cost of grain (MSP + procurement incidentals + distribution cost) minus the issue price (now zero). It is about ₹2 lakh crore a year, one of the largest subsidies in the Union Budget. Economic cost of rice and wheat is far above the market price because of storage losses, interest and transport.
  • Shanta Kumar High Level Committee (set up 2014, report January 2015) on restructuring FCI: FCI should hand procurement in Punjab, Haryana, AP etc. to States and move to eastern States where farmers get below MSP; cut NFSA coverage to about 40%; move to cash transfers of food subsidy gradually; negotiable warehouse receipts; outsource storage; and a clear liquidation policy when stocks exceed norms.
  • DBT in food: cash in place of grain was piloted in Chandigarh, Puducherry and Dadra–Nagar Haveli (2015). Critics argue cash can be eaten up by inflation and does not reach remote areas; supporters say it cuts leakages (the Shanta Kumar Committee put PDS leakage at about 40–50%).

3. Other price tools, trade policy and Andhra Pradesh

  • PM-AASHA (2018) has three parts: PSS (Price Support Scheme: NAFED/NCCF physically buy pulses, oilseeds, copra at MSP); PDPS (Price Deficiency Payment: pay farmers the gap between MSP and market price without buying, modelled on MP's Bhavantar Bhugtan Yojana 2017); MIS (Market Intervention Scheme: perishables not under MSP such as onion, tomato, chillies, at a State's request, losses shared 50:50). Price Stabilisation Fund (2014–15) keeps buffers of onion and pulses.
  • Trade policy tools: export bans or duties when domestic prices rise (wheat export ban May 2022; non-basmati white rice ban July 2023, lifted 2024); import duty cuts on edible oils and pulses; stock limits on traders (Essential Commodities Act 1955). India is the world's largest rice exporter (~40% of world rice trade) and the largest importer of edible oil. Self-reliance drives: NMEO-Oil Palm (2021), NMEO-Oilseeds (2024), Mission for Aatmanirbharta in Pulses (Budget 2025–26, six years: tur, urad, masur).
  • WTO Agreement on Agriculture (1995): subsidies are put in boxes: Amber (trade-distorting, such as MSP-based price support; limit de minimis 10% of the value of production for developing countries, 5% for developed); Blue (with production limits); Green (minimal distortion: research, extension, PM-KISAN-type income support, no limit). The Bali 'peace clause' (2013) protects public stockholding for food security; India became the first country to invoke it, for rice (2020).
  • Andhra Pradesh: AP is a big paddy procurement State (Godavari and Krishna deltas); the AP State Civil Supplies Corporation buys paddy through Rythu Seva Kendras with e-Crop booking and pays into bank accounts; AP distributes rice (plus subsidised sugar, red gram) through about 29,000 fair price shops and once used mobile dispensing units (MDUs) for doorstep delivery; State-level market intervention for crops like chillies, onions, tomatoes, mangoes when prices crash.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) MSP is for grain of Fair Average Quality (FAQ); poorer grain gets a value cut. (2) Issue price = price at which FCI gives grain to States (now zero under NFSA); Central Issue Price (CIP) was ₹3/2/1 per kg for rice/wheat/coarse grains. (3) Food Security Mission (NFSM, 2007) raises output of rice, wheat, pulses, coarse cereals, nutri-cereals. (4) International Year of Millets 2023 (India's proposal); millets renamed Shree Anna. (5) Fortified rice (iron, folic acid, B12) in all schemes by 2024. (6) Price Stabilisation Fund is under the Department of Consumer Affairs. (7) Swaminathan formula = C2 + 50%; the Government uses A2+FL + 50%. (8) Cobweb theory: this year's high price → more sowing → next year's glut → price crash, a reason for pre-sowing MSP.
