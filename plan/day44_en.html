GEOGRAPHY · Urbanisation and migration

Understand it first

What is it? Urbanisation means a rising share of people living in towns and cities. It is not just towns getting bigger: it is a shift from farm work to industry and services, from villages to dense settlements with municipal government. Towns grow in three ways: natural increase (births over deaths in towns), migration from villages, and reclassification (a big village becomes a census town, or a city's limits are widened). Migration is the movement of people from one place to another to live. It changes both ends: the place people leave and the place they reach. Yesterday we met the Census and demography; today we learn how India urbanised, its big cities and problems, urban missions, and why and where Indians migrate, with Andhra Pradesh at the end.

1. How India urbanised

India's urban population share, 1901 to 2025
  • Trend: the urban share was only about 11% in 1901, rose slowly to 17.3% in 1951, 25.7% in 1991 and 31.2% in 2011 (about 37.7 crore people). It is estimated at about 38% (about 52 crore) in 2025. 2011 was the first census in which the absolute increase in urban population (9.1 crore) was larger than the rural increase. India is still less urbanised than China (~65%) or the world average (~57%).
  • Kinds of towns (Census 2011): 7,933 towns = 4,041 statutory towns (with a municipality, corporation, cantonment or notified area) + 3,892 census towns (rules learnt on Day 43). Census towns nearly tripled from 1,362 in 2001, showing much 'hidden' urbanisation in big villages. Class I city = 1 lakh+ people (468 in 2011); million-plus urban agglomerations (UAs) = 53 (35 in 2001). An urban agglomeration is a town plus its continuous outgrowths (e.g. a railway colony or university campus).
  • Biggest UAs (2011): Greater Mumbai (~1.84 crore), Delhi (~1.63 crore), Kolkata (~1.41 crore), then Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad. Most urbanised State: Goa (62%); among big States Tamil Nadu (48%), Kerala, Maharashtra; least: Himachal Pradesh (10%), then Bihar (11%), Assam, Odisha. Among UTs Delhi (97.5%). Largest urban population (number): Maharashtra, then UP.
  • Classifying cities by function: administrative (Chandigarh, Amaravati), industrial (Jamshedpur, Bhilai), port (Visakhapatnam, Kandla), religious (Tirupati, Varanasi), educational (Pilani, Roorkee), hill/tourist (Shimla, Ooty), garrison (Mhow, Ambala), mining (Jharia, Singrauli). New planned towns after 1947: Chandigarh (Le Corbusier), Bhubaneswar, Gandhinagar, Durgapur.

2. Urban problems, governance and missions

Dharavi, Mumbai: one of Asia's largest slums shows the housing gap in cities. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Problems: slums (Census 2011: about 6.5 crore slum dwellers, ~17% of urban people; most in Maharashtra; slums reported in about 65% of towns; Dharavi, Mumbai, among Asia's largest); housing shortage; water, sewage and solid waste; traffic and air pollution; urban flooding (Chennai 2015, Mumbai 2005); urban heat islands; informal jobs; weak municipal finances (property tax under ~0.2% of GDP).
  • Governance: the 74th Amendment (1992) added Part IXA (Articles 243P–243ZG) and the 12th Schedule (18 functions, e.g. urban planning, water supply, slum improvement, fire services). Three bodies: Nagar Panchayat (area in transition), Municipal Council (smaller urban area), Municipal Corporation (larger urban area). State Election Commission holds elections; State Finance Commission every 5 years; District Planning Committee (243ZD) and Metropolitan Planning Committee (243ZE, 10 lakh+).
  • Urban missions: JNNURM (2005) was the first big one. Smart Cities Mission (June 2015, 100 cities, ended 31 March 2025, ~94% projects done); AMRUT (2015) → AMRUT 2.0 (Oct 2021, water for all towns); PMAY-Urban (2015) → PMAY-U 2.0 (2024: 1 crore more houses); Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban (2 Oct 2014) → SBM-U 2.0 (2021, garbage-free cities); HRIDAY (2015, heritage cities, incl. Amaravati); DAY-NULM (2013); PM SVANidhi (2020, street-vendor loans). Budget 2026–27 raised the Urban Challenge Fund to ₹10,000 crore and planned City Economic Regions. India's metro rail network (over 1,000 km) is among the world's top three.

3. Migration and Andhra Pradesh

Why people move: push and pull
AspectKey facts
How the Census countsPlace of birth or place of last residence (if different from where enumerated). Census 2011: about 45.6 crore migrants by last residence (37%); about two-thirds are women
StreamsIntra-district and intra-State moves are the large majority; rural → rural is the biggest stream (women after marriage); rural → urban is the main stream for men seeking work
Inter-StateBiggest senders: UP, Bihar; biggest receivers: Maharashtra, Delhi, Gujarat, also Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala (Kerala sends to the Gulf and receives workers from the east)
InternationalIndia has the largest diaspora (~3.5 crore NRIs + PIOs); largest remittance receiver (~$135 bn, 2024–25); main destinations UAE, USA, Saudi Arabia; Emigration Act 1983 (ECR countries); Pravasi Bharatiya Divas 9 January (Gandhi's return, 1915)
TheoriesRavenstein's laws (1885): most migrants go short distances, step by step, women more over short distances, each stream has a counter-stream; Everett Lee (1966): push–pull factors with intervening obstacles; Todaro: people move on expected (not actual) urban income
ProtectionInter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979 now part of the OSH Code 2020 (labour codes in force from 21 Nov 2025); e-Shram portal (Aug 2021) for unorganised workers (~30 crore registered); One Nation One Ration Card; COVID lockdown 2020 exposed migrants' distress
  • Andhra Pradesh: urban share about 29.5% (2011), below the national average; big cities: Visakhapatnam (largest city, GVMC), Vijayawada, Guntur, Nellore, Kurnool, Tirupati, Kakinada, Rajahmundry, Anantapur. Smart Cities in AP: Visakhapatnam, Kakinada, Tirupati, Amaravati. Amaravati is being built as the capital city (CRDA). Migration: seasonal labour migration from north coastal districts (Srikakulam, Vizianagaram) and drought-hit Rayalaseema to cities and other States; Gulf migration from the Godavari districts and Kadapa; skilled migration to the USA. The AP Non-Resident Telugu Society (APNRTS, 2016) helps overseas Telugus.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Over-urbanisation = city growth faster than jobs and services; pseudo-urbanisation = growth driven by rural push, not urban pull. (2) Primate city (Mark Jefferson) = a city far bigger than the next one (e.g. Bangkok); India has no single primate city. (3) Counter-urbanisation = people moving out of big cities to small towns or villages. (4) Rural-urban continuum and RURBAN Mission (2016): urban amenities in village clusters. (5) Peri-urban areas = the fringe where city meets village. (6) Gentrification = richer people moving into poor areas, pushing old residents out. (7) Brain drain vs brain gain; 'circular migration' = people moving back and forth seasonally. (8) Sex ratio of migrants: men dominate in Mumbai and Delhi, so these cities have low sex ratios.

ECONOMY · Industrial development in India: patterns and problems

Understand it first

What is it? Industry turns raw materials into goods (manufacturing), and also covers mining, electricity, gas, water and construction in national accounts. Countries usually grow rich by moving workers from farms to factories (more output per worker), then to services. India took an unusual path: services grew faster than manufacturing, so factory jobs stayed limited. Learn it as policy phases (1948 → 1956 → control era → 1991 → Make in India) → growth pattern → how industry is measured (IIP, core sector) → MSMEs → problems → corridors and new schemes → Andhra Pradesh. Tomorrow (Day 45) covers the 1991 policy, disinvestment and Ease of Doing Business in depth.

1. Policy phases and the growth pattern

India's industrial policies at a glance
  • Industrial Policy Resolution 1948: a mixed economy; arms and ammunition, atomic energy, railways for the Centre only; basic industries (coal, iron and steel, aircraft, ships, telecom equipment, mineral oils) to be new State enterprises. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act 1951 (IDRA) started industrial licensing.
  • Industrial Policy Resolution 1956 (called India's 'economic constitution'), with the Second Plan (Mahalanobis model: heavy and capital-goods industry first): Schedule A: 17 industries exclusively for the State; Schedule B: 12 industries progressively State-owned, private sector may supplement; Schedule C: the rest for the private sector. Goal: 'socialist pattern of society' (Avadi session 1955). Steel plants of this era: Bhilai (USSR), Rourkela (West Germany), Durgapur (UK); Bokaro (USSR, 1964).
  • Control era (1965–1980): MRTP Act 1969 (to curb monopolies; on the Dutt Committee findings), bank nationalisation 1969, FERA 1973 (foreign exchange; foreign equity capped at 40%), reservation of items for small-scale industry (1967 onwards), 1977 policy (Janata: District Industries Centres, small and tiny sector focus). This was the 'Licence-Permit-Quota Raj'. 1980 and 1985 policies began partial liberalisation (broad-banding, delicensing of some industries).
  • Growth pattern: 1951–65 fast growth (~7% a year, heavy industry); 1965–80 deceleration (~4%: wars, droughts, falling public investment, controls; debated by K.N. Raj, Isher Ahluwalia); 1980s recovery (~6–7%); after 1991 faster but uneven. The share of manufacturing in GVA has stayed around 14–17% for decades (target was 25% under the National Manufacturing Policy 2011 and Make in India 2014). Manufacturing employs only about 11–12% of workers. Industry (incl. construction) GVA grew about 7% in H1 2025–26.

2. Measuring industry, MSMEs and the problems

Measure / sectorKey facts
IIP (Index of Industrial Production)Monthly, by the NSO (MoSPI), released about 6 weeks after the month. New base 2022–23 (first release in 2026, replacing 2011–12): 463 item groups, manufacturing ~76% weight, plus mining and electricity (now split into renewable and non-renewable). Also by use: primary, capital, intermediate, infrastructure/construction, consumer durables and non-durables

ASI (Annual Survey of Industries)Main source on organised (registered) factories (Factories Act): output, employment, capital. Tamil Nadu has the most factories; Maharashtra, Gujarat lead in output
PMIPurchasing Managers' Index (private, S&P Global/HSBC): above 50 = expansion; a quick monthly signal
MSME limits from 1 April 2025
  • MSMEs: governed by the MSMED Act 2006; since July 2020 a composite test of investment in plant and machinery + turnover, with limits raised from 1 April 2025 (Budget 2025–26): micro ≤ ₹2.5 crore / ₹10 crore, small ≤ ₹25 crore / ₹100 crore, medium ≤ ₹125 crore / ₹500 crore. Registration on Udyam (2020). MSMEs give about 30% of GDP, ~45% of exports, and are the largest non-farm employer. Support: CGTMSE (collateral-free credit guarantee, 2000), PMEGP (2008, KVIC), MUDRA (2015: Shishu, Kishor, Tarun, Tarun Plus up to ₹20 lakh), TReDS (bill discounting), 45-day payment rule (Section 43B(h), Income Tax), PM Vishwakarma (2023, 18 trades), SAMADHAAN portal for delayed payments. Reservation of items for small industry ended in 2015.
  • Problems of Indian industry: (1) low share of manufacturing and premature deindustrialisation (Dani Rodrik's term: manufacturing peaks at a low income level); (2) jobless growth: capital-intensive output, few jobs; (3) the 'missing middle': many tiny firms, few medium firms (rigid labour laws and size-based benefits discourage growth); (4) costly land, power and logistics (logistics ~8–9% of GDP, NLP 2022); (5) credit gaps and delayed payments for MSMEs; (6) sick units and twin-balance-sheet problem (handled first by SICA 1985 / BIFR, now the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016, NCLT); (7) regional imbalance: industry concentrated in the west and south; (8) import dependence in electronics, solar cells, APIs (China); (9) low R&D (~0.65% of GDP).

3. New push: schemes, corridors and Andhra Pradesh

Sri City SEZ (Tirupati district): a major industrial hub of AP. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Make in India (25 September 2014): 25 sectors (later 27), DPIIT (renamed from DIPP in 2019); Production Linked Incentive (PLI, 2020): 14 sectors (mobile phones, pharma, autos, ACC batteries, solar modules, textiles, food processing, drones, specialty steel...) with about ₹1.97 lakh crore; India Semiconductor Mission (Dec 2021, ₹76,000 crore): first plants at Sanand (Micron), Dholera (Tata–PSMC fab), Assam (Jagiroad); National Logistics Policy (Sept 2022) and PM Gati Shakti (Oct 2021); labour codes in force 21 November 2025; National Manufacturing Mission (Budget 2025–26).
  • Industrial corridors (under NICDC): Delhi–Mumbai (DMIC, Japan-aided, along the Western DFC; Dholera smart city), Amritsar–Kolkata, Chennai–Bengaluru (CBIC, node Krishnapatnam), East Coast / Visakhapatnam–Chennai (VCIC, ADB-aided; nodes Visakhapatnam, Machilipatnam, Donakonda, Srikalahasti–Chittoor), Hyderabad–Bengaluru (HBIC) and others: 11 corridors. In August 2024 the Cabinet approved 12 new industrial smart cities, two of them in AP: Orvakal (Kurnool) and Kopparthy (YSR Kadapa). Other tools: SEZs (Act 2005), National Investment and Manufacturing Zones (NIMZ, 2011), PM MITRA textile parks (7), bulk drug and medical device parks.
  • Andhra Pradesh: major units and hubs across regions: Visakhapatnam Steel Plant (RINL, coastal shore-based plant; revival package ₹11,440 crore, Jan 2025), HPCL refinery and pharma city (Visakhapatnam), Sri City (Tirupati district: multi-product SEZ, Japanese and global firms), Kia Motors (Penukonda, Sri Sathya Sai district), Isuzu (Sri City), electronics cluster at Tirupati/Renigunta, Krishnapatnam and Kakinada SEZs, cement belt (Nandyal–Kadapa–Guntur), aqua and food processing in the Godavari–Krishna region. AP's Industrial Development Policy 4.0 (2024–29) and MSME policy aim at 'one family, one entrepreneur'; AP has ranked near the top in Business Reforms Action Plan (Ease of Doing Business) rankings.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Weber's theory of industrial location (1909): industries locate to minimise transport cost; weight-losing raw materials (iron ore, sugarcane) → near the source; pure materials → near the market. (2) Sugar mills sit near cane fields because cane loses sucrose fast. (3) Footloose industries (electronics, software) can locate almost anywhere. (4) Navratna (1997), Maharatna (2009), Miniratna PSU status. (5) Hindu rate of growth (~3.5%, Raj Krishna). (6) Industrial sickness definition under SICA: accumulated losses ≥ net worth. (7) Make in India lion logo; Startup India (16 Jan 2016), Stand-Up India (2016: loans ₹10 lakh–₹1 crore to SC/ST and women). (8) ESDM: India is the second-largest mobile phone maker.

TIMED PRACTICE · Polity: 30 questions in APPSC pattern (60 minutes)

Understand it first

How to take this test: set a timer for 60 minutes. Close all notes. Write your choice on paper first, then tap to check. Score: +1 correct, −1/3 wrong. Target: 22 or more. The questions cover the whole Polity block: making of the Constitution, Preamble, rights and duties, DPSP, Union and State executive, Parliament, judiciary, federalism and emergency, local bodies, constitutional and statutory bodies, and amendments.
