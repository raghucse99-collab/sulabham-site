GEOGRAPHY · Racial, tribal, religious and linguistic groups

Understand it first

What is it? India is called a 'museum of peoples': over thousands of years groups came through the north-west passes, the north-east and the sea, and mixed. Geography asks who lives where: (1) racial groups (old classification by physical features; now replaced by genetics), (2) tribal groups (communities with distinct culture, mostly in hills and forests), (3) religious groups, (4) language groups. Exams ask classifications, largest/highest States, Schedules and Articles. A word of caution: 'race' is an outdated, disputed idea; all humans are one species. Learn it as racial classification → tribes → religions → languages → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Racial groups (B.S. Guha's classification) and the genetic view

  • Other classifications: H.H. Risley (1901 Census) gave seven types (Turko-Iranian, Indo-Aryan, Scytho-Dravidian, Aryo-Dravidian, Mongolo-Dravidian, Mongoloid, Dravidian), based on nose index; criticised as colonial. Guha (Census 1931 work, published 1935–37) is the most quoted in exams.
  • Genetic view: studies (Reich and others, 2009) show most Indians descend from a mix of 'Ancestral North Indians' (ANI) and 'Ancestral South Indians' (ASI), plus Austro-Asiatic and Tibeto-Burman inputs. The Rakhigarhi skeleton DNA (published 2019) suggests the Harappans were mostly of local ancestry with Iranian-farmer links. Andamanese groups preserve some of the oldest lineages outside Africa.

2. Tribal groups

A traditional barrel-shaped Toda hut in the Nilgiris (a PVTG). (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
  • Numbers (Census 2011): STs 10.4 crore (8.6%); about 705 notified tribal communities; ~90% live in rural areas. Most STs by number: Madhya Pradesh, then Maharashtra, Odisha; highest share: Lakshadweep (~95%), Mizoram (~94%), Nagaland, Meghalaya. No STs notified in Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Chandigarh, Puducherry. Largest tribes: Bhil (~1.7 crore), Gond (~1.3 crore), then Santhal, Mina, Oraon.
  • Tribal regions: (1) North and north-east (Himalaya, NE: Nagas, Khasis, Garos, Bhutias, Lepchas); (2) Central belt (largest: Gonds, Santhals, Mundas, Oraons, Hos, Baigas in MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha); (3) Western (Bhils, Minas, Warlis in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra); (4) Southern (Todas, Kotas, Badagas, Kurumbas in Nilgiris; Chenchus, Koyas in AP/Telangana); (5) Islands (Onge, Jarawa, Sentinelese, Shompen, Nicobarese).
  • Safeguards (recap from Polity): Article 342 (notification), Fifth Schedule (Scheduled Areas, Tribes Advisory Council), Sixth Schedule (Autonomous District Councils in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram: 10 councils), Article 275(1) grants, 338A NCST (2004), PESA 1996, Forest Rights Act 2006, Ministry of Tribal Affairs (1999). PVTGs: 75 (most in Odisha); PM-JANMAN (2023), Dharti Aaba Janjatiya Gram Utkarsh Abhiyan (2024). Janjatiya Gaurav Divas: 15 November (Birsa Munda's birth anniversary, from 2021).

3. Religious and linguistic groups

Religious composition of India (Census 2011)
  • Religion, where concentrated (2011): Muslims: largest number in UP; highest share Lakshadweep (~96%), then J&K (~68%), Assam (~34%) and West Bengal (~27%) among States. Christians: Nagaland (~88%), Mizoram (~87%), Meghalaya (~75%); largest number in Kerala. Sikhs: Punjab (~58%). Buddhists: largest number in Maharashtra (Navayana, after Ambedkar's 1956 conversion); highest share Sikkim (~27%), Arunachal. Jains: largest number in Maharashtra, then Rajasthan. Hindus are a minority in Lakshadweep, J&K (old State), Punjab, Mizoram, Nagaland, Meghalaya, Arunachal, Manipur (plurality).
  • Minorities: six notified national minorities under the NCM Act 1992: Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Parsis (Zoroastrians) (all 1993) and Jains (2014). Parsis are the smallest (~57,000; Jiyo Parsi scheme, 2013). Articles 25–28 (religious freedom), 29–30 (cultural and educational rights of minorities). T.M.A. Pai (2002): minority status is decided State-wise.
India's four main language families
  • Language numbers (Census 2011): 19,569 raw mother-tongue returns → 121 languages (22 scheduled + 99 non-scheduled) with 10,000+ speakers. Speakers: Hindi ~43.6%, Bengali ~8.0%, Marathi ~6.9%, Telugu ~6.7% (4th; it was 3rd in 2001), Tamil ~5.7%, Gujarati, Urdu, Kannada, Odia, Malayalam. Bilingualism ~26%, trilingualism ~7%. Smallest scheduled language by speakers: Sanskrit (~25,000).
  • Eighth Schedule: originally 14; Sindhi (21st Amendment, 1967); Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali (71st, 1992); Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santali (92nd, 2003) → 22. Official language: Article 343 Hindi in Devanagari; Official Languages Act 1963; Article 350A primary education in the mother tongue; 350B Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (7th Amendment 1956). Classical languages: 11: Tamil (2004), Sanskrit (2005), Telugu and Kannada (2008), Malayalam (2013), Odia (2014), and Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali (October 2024).
  • Andhra Pradesh: Telugu is spoken by nearly 90%; Urdu made the second official language (2022 amendment to the AP Official Language Act 1966); tribal languages: Koya, Savara (Sora), Kuvi (Kui), Gadaba, Konda (Kubi), and Lambadi (Banjara); Tamil and Kannada near the southern and western borders, Odia in the north. AP's STs (about 5.5%) include the Sugali/Lambada, Koya, Yanadi, Yerukula, Konda Dora, Savara, Jatapu, Bagata, Gadaba, Valmiki, Chenchu, Konda Reddi; the Agency (Scheduled) Areas run through Parvathipuram Manyam, Alluri Sitharama Raju and parts of the Godavari, Eluru and Srikakulam districts, while Chenchus live in the Nallamala hills and Yanadis in the Nellore–Tirupati plains. AP was the first State formed on a linguistic basis (1 October 1953) after Potti Sriramulu's fast.

Other angles asked: (1) Language vs dialect: the Census groups mother tongues under languages; e.g. Bhojpuri, Rajasthani are counted under Hindi. (2) Three-language formula (Kothari Commission 1964–66; NEP 1968; NEP 2020 keeps it flexible, no language imposed). (3) States Reorganisation Commission (1953, Fazl Ali; Pannikar, Kunzru) → SR Act 1956: 14 States, 6 UTs. (4) Dar Commission (1948) and JVP Committee (1948: Nehru, Patel, Pattabhi) first rejected linguistic States. (5) Toda buffalo herders of the Nilgiris; Jarawa and Sentinelese are protected by the Andaman Aboriginal Tribes Regulation 1956. (6) Khasi and Garo societies are matrilineal. (7) Brahui (Balochistan) is a Dravidian island in the north-west. (8) Script: Santali uses Ol Chiki (Raghunath Murmu).

ECONOMY · New Industrial Policy 1991, disinvestment and Ease of Doing Business

Understand it first

What is it? In 1991 India faced a balance of payments crisis: it had money for only about two weeks of imports. Instead of only borrowing, the Government changed the economy's direction: Liberalisation (remove controls and licences), Privatisation (reduce the State's role in business) and Globalisation (open to foreign trade and investment): LPG. Three linked ideas follow: the New Industrial Policy (24 July 1991) removed the licence raj; disinvestment means the Government selling its shares in public sector enterprises; Ease of Doing Business means making it simple and quick to start and run a business. Yesterday we saw the policies up to 1991; today we see how 1991 changed them and what came after.

1. The 1991 crisis and the New Industrial Policy

The 1991 crisis and the New Industrial Policy
  • Causes of the crisis: high fiscal deficit (~8% of GDP) and current account deficit (~3%); rising short-term foreign debt; the Gulf War (1990–91) raised oil prices and cut remittances; the collapse of the USSR (a key trade partner); political instability; credit rating downgrades. Forex reserves fell to about $1 billion. India pledged about 47 tonnes of gold with the Bank of England and Bank of Japan (July 1991) and took an IMF loan; the rupee was devalued about 18–19% in two steps (1 and 3 July 1991).
  • NIP, 24 July 1991 (same day as Dr Manmohan Singh's Budget; PM P.V. Narasimha Rao, from Andhra Pradesh, held the Industry portfolio): (1) industrial licensing abolished except for 18 industries (now 5: alcoholic drinks, tobacco products, defence/aerospace electronics, industrial explosives, hazardous chemicals); (2) public sector reservation cut from 17 to 8 (now only 2: atomic energy and railway operations); (3) MRTP asset threshold removed (pre-entry approval for big firms ended); (4) automatic approval for FDI up to 51% in 34 high-priority industries; FIPB for other cases; (5) foreign technology agreements automatic within limits; (6) phased manufacturing programmes ended; (7) location: no approval needed except within 25 km of cities over 1 million; (8) start of disinvestment and MoUs for PSU autonomy.
  • Other 1991-era reforms: trade: peak import duty cut from over 300% to about 10% over time, quantitative restrictions removed by 2001; rupee made convertible on the current account (August 1994); financial sector: Narasimham Committee (1991); tax: Raja Chelliah Committee; FERA → FEMA 1999 (in force 1 June 2000); MRTP → Competition Act 2002 (CCI); FIPB abolished in 2017 (approvals now by ministries through the Foreign Investment Facilitation Portal). India joined the WTO on 1 January 1995.
  • Results and critique: GDP growth rose (about 6% in the 1990s, ~8%+ in 2003–08); forex reserves crossed $600 billion (2021 onwards); services and IT boomed; FDI rose. Critics point to jobless growth, rising inequality, agrarian distress and slow manufacturing. Reforms are called 'first-generation' (1991: delicensing, trade, exchange rate) and 'second-generation' (factor markets: land, labour, capital; GST, IBC, PSU reform).

2. Disinvestment and privatisation

Disinvestment: from minority sales to privatisation
  • Meaning and aims: disinvestment = Government sells part of its equity in a CPSE (Central Public Sector Enterprise). If the Government keeps 51% or more, it is minority disinvestment; if it sells control (usually to a strategic buyer), it is strategic disinvestment / privatisation. Aims: raise non-debt capital receipts to cut the fiscal deficit, improve efficiency through market discipline, unlock value, widen share ownership. Critics call it 'selling the family silver' (a phrase first used by Harold Macmillan about Thatcher's sales).
  • History: 1991–92: minority shares sold in bundles to financial institutions; C. Rangarajan Committee (1993): disinvest up to 49% in general (up to 74% in some), above that for non-priority units; Disinvestment Commission (1996, G.V. Ramakrishna); Department of Disinvestment (1999) → Ministry (2001–04) → Department under Finance → renamed DIPAM (April 2016). Strategic sales: Modern Food Industries (2000, to HLL; first), BALCO (2001, Sterlite), VSNL (Tata), Hindustan Zinc (Sterlite), Maruti (Suzuki), IPCL; later Air India to Tata (Talace, completed 27 January 2022), NINL to Tata Steel (2022). National Investment Fund (2005) holds proceeds.
  • Methods: IPO (first listing; LIC IPO May 2022, ~₹21,000 crore, 3.5%), FPO, Offer for Sale (OFS) through the stock exchange, ETFs (CPSE ETF 2014, Bharat 22 ETF 2017), strategic sale, share buyback by the CPSE, cross-holding. New PSE Policy (Budget 2021–22): 4 strategic sectors (atomic energy, space and defence; transport and telecom; power, petroleum, coal and minerals; banking, insurance and financial services) keep a bare minimum of CPSEs; in non-strategic sectors CPSEs are to be privatised or closed. NITI Aayog recommends candidates.
  • Recent status: the Budget no longer shows a separate disinvestment target; 2026–27 projects about ₹80,000 crore from disinvestment and asset monetisation together (FY26 disinvestment was only about ₹17,000 crore). In FY27 a 6.5% LIC stake was sold, and in October 2026 the Cabinet approved Fairfax as buyer of 60.72% of IDBI Bank (Government + LIC), worth about ₹52,900 crore: the first bank privatisation in decades. National Monetisation Pipeline (Aug 2021, ~₹6 lakh crore, 2022–25) leases assets without selling ownership; NMP 2.0 (Budget 2025–26) targets about ₹10 lakh crore (2025–30). Maharatna (2009): about 14 CPSEs; Navratna (1997); Miniratna I and II.
