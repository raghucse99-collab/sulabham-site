INDIAN SOCIETY · Family, marriage and kinship

Understand it first

What is it? Every society has three basic building blocks. Family is the smallest group: people related by blood, marriage or adoption who usually live together, share income and raise children. Marriage is the socially approved union that creates a family and decides who the children belong to. Kinship is the whole web of relatives created by blood and marriage, with rules about whom you can marry, who inherits and how you behave with each relative. Sociology asks what forms these take in India, why they are changing and what the law says. This is the first of nine Indian Society days (46–54). Learn it as family (meaning, types, joint family, change) → marriage (forms, rules, laws) → kinship (types, terms, usages, north vs south) → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Family

  • Definitions: G.P. Murdock (1949): a social group with common residence, economic cooperation and reproduction; he called the nuclear family universal. Burgess and Locke: a group united by marriage, blood or adoption, forming a single household. Functions: reproduction, socialisation (first school of the child), economic support, emotional security, status placing, care of the old. Many functions have shifted to schools, hospitals, the State (the family is said to be 'losing functions'; Ogburn).
  • Joint family: Iravati Karve: a group who generally live under one roof, eat food cooked at one hearth, hold property in common, take part in common worship and are related as kindred. Features: depth of generations, common property, karta (eldest male manager), cooperation, social security. I.P. Desai (Mahuva, Gujarat) stressed 'functional jointness': relatives living apart still share duties. A.M. Shah noted that even in the past many households were nuclear in residence.
  • Hindu law schools: Mitakshara (Vijnaneshwara's commentary on the Yajnavalkya Smriti; most of India): sons get a share by birth; Dayabhaga (Jimutavahana; Bengal, Assam): rights only after the father's death. The Hindu Succession Act 1956, amended in 2005, made daughters coparceners by birth; Vineeta Sharma (2020): this applies even if the father died before 2005.
Types of family: five ways to classify
  • Change in the family: causes: industrialisation, urbanisation, migration, education, women's employment, new laws, smaller family size (TFR 1.9). Trends: nuclearisation (average household size about 4.8 in 2011, falling), single-person and elderly-only households, live-in relationships (recognised by courts: D. Velusamy 2010), 'empty nest' parents, rising divorce in cities, care of the elderly (Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007). Yet kin networks remain strong in crises, marriages and migration.

3. Kinship

  • Kinds and degrees: consanguineal kin (by blood: parents, siblings, children) and affinal kin (by marriage: in-laws, spouse). Primary kin (Murdock: 8, e.g. father, mother, brother, sister, husband, wife, son, daughter), secondary (kin of primary kin: 33, e.g. father's brother) and tertiary (151, e.g. father's brother's wife).
  • Descent groups and terms: descent may be patrilineal, matrilineal, bilateral or double. Lineage (traceable common ancestor), clan (believed common ancestor, often a totem), phratry (group of clans), moiety (society split into two halves). L.H. Morgan: kinship terms are classificatory (one word for many relatives, e.g. 'chacha' for many) or descriptive (one word for one relative).
  • North vs south (Iravati Karve, 'Kinship Organisation in India', 1953): the north forbids marriage among close kin, practises village exogamy, and the bride goes to a distant, 'superior' family; the south allows and prefers cross-cousin marriage (mother's brother's daughter, father's sister's daughter) and even uncle–niece marriage, keeping property and ties within a close circle. Karve described four kinship zones: northern, central, southern and eastern.
Kinship usages: rules of behaviour between relatives
  • Andhra Pradesh: menarikam (cross-cousin marriage) and marriage with the elder sister's daughter are common across communities; Gurajada Apparao's 'Kanyasulkam' (1892) attacked bride price and old men marrying girls; Kandukuri Veeresalingam held the first widow remarriage (Rajahmundry, 11 December 1881); illatom adoption (a son-in-law brought into the wife's home as heir) is a recognised custom among some Andhra communities; the menamama (maternal uncle) has a key ritual role. Joint families remain more common in delta farming families; migration from Uttarandhra and Rayalaseema and to the Gulf and USA has produced many split households.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Family of orientation (into which you are born) vs family of procreation (which you create by marriage). (2) Coparcenary = joint heirs to ancestral property. (3) Gotra exogamy was upheld by tradition; the Hindu Marriage Act forbids marriage within sapinda (3 generations on the mother's side, 5 on the father's) and prohibited degrees unless custom allows (this saves menarikam). (4) Stridhan = woman's own property (gifts) — distinct from dowry. (5) Neolocal residence is rising in cities. (6) Talcott Parsons: the isolated nuclear family fits industrial society. (7) Panchayats and khap decisions against sagotra marriages are illegal. (8) Matrilineal Meghalaya: among Khasis the youngest daughter (ka khadduh) inherits the ancestral house.

ECONOMY · Industrial sickness: causes, consequences, remedies

Understand it first

What is it? A firm is 'sick' when it keeps making losses, cannot repay its loans and its own capital (net worth) is eaten up, so it cannot survive without outside help. Like a sick person, it shows early symptoms (late payments), then a crisis (default), and then either recovers (restructuring, new owner) or dies (closure, liquidation). Sickness matters because it locks up bank money (NPAs), destroys jobs and wastes capital. India first tried to 'nurse' sick units (SICA and BIFR, 1985–2016), which mostly failed; since 2016 the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code aims at quick revival or exit. Learn it as meaning → causes → consequences → old remedies → IBC and new tools → MSMEs → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Meaning and causes

  • Definitions: SICA 1985 (Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act): a company registered for at least 5 years (originally 7) whose accumulated losses equal or exceed its entire net worth; 'potentially sick' if losses wipe out 50% of peak net worth. RBI (MSMEs, 2012): a unit is sick if any borrowal account stays NPA for over 3 months, or accumulated losses erode 50% or more of net worth. Since 2016, the RBI Framework for Revival of MSMEs uses early stress tags: SMA-0 (1–30 days overdue), SMA-1 (31–60), SMA-2 (61–90); beyond 90 days = NPA.
  • Internal causes (within the firm, usually the bigger share): poor management and planning; wrong project choice or location; outdated technology; over-capitalisation or too much debt; diversion of funds and fraud; poor marketing; labour unrest; family disputes in management.
  • External causes (outside the firm): demand recession; shortage or high cost of raw materials and power; credit squeeze and delayed payments by buyers (often Government); policy changes (sudden duty or tax changes, bans); cheap imports after liberalisation; natural disasters; infrastructure gaps. Sectors with chronic sickness: textiles (cotton and jute mills), sugar, engineering, steel (2015–18), power projects, airlines, telecom.
Industrial sickness: from causes to cure
  • Consequences: loss of jobs and wages; bank NPAs that cut fresh lending (the twin balance sheet problem: stressed companies and stressed banks, Economic Survey 2016–17); loss of tax revenue; idle capital and machinery; sick suppliers and buyers (chain effect, especially for MSMEs); regional decline (e.g. old textile towns); fiscal cost of bailouts; industrial unrest.

2. Remedies: from BIFR to the IBC

ToolWhat it does / key facts
Tiwari Committee (1981) → SICA 1985Set up BIFR (1987) and the appellate AAIFR to revive or wind up sick companies; slow (years per case), the reference itself gave a legal shield from creditors; SICA repealed and BIFR dissolved on 1 December 2016

IBC 2016Based on the Bankruptcy Law Reforms Committee (T.K. How a case moves under the IBC
  • IBC in practice: creditors had realised about ₹4.3 lakh crore through approved resolution plans by March 2026; recovery is about one-third of admitted claims (haircuts of 60–70% are common) but well above liquidation value; IBC gave about 52% of banks' recoveries in 2024–25. Cases often exceed the 330-day limit. Big cases: Essar Steel (ArcelorMittal, 2019; Supreme Court upheld creditor primacy), Bhushan Steel (Tata Steel), Jet Airways (failed, liquidation ordered 2024). Pre-packaged insolvency for MSMEs (April 2021): debtor-in-possession, 120 days.
  • IBC (Amendment) Act 2026 (assent 6 April 2026; most parts in force 26 May 2026): 14- and 30-day time limits for NCLT orders; NCLAT to decide appeals in 3 months; a new Creditor-Initiated Insolvency Resolution Process (out-of-court start by financial institutions with 51% creditor approval; 150 + 45 days) and a group insolvency framework (both await rules); power to frame cross-border insolvency rules.
  • MSME-specific support: Emergency Credit Line Guarantee Scheme (ECLGS, May 2020); Credit Guarantee Scheme for Subordinate Debt (2020) for stressed MSMEs (promoters get loans to put into the unit); Fund of Funds (₹50,000 crore equity); one-time restructuring of MSME loans (2019–21); Committees for Stressed MSMEs at banks; MSE Facilitation Councils and SAMADHAAN for delayed payments; 45-day payment rule (43B(h)); TReDS.
  • Andhra Pradesh: sickness has hit jute mills of Uttarandhra (e.g. Nellimarla, Chittivalasa), cooperative sugar factories (several closed or under revival plans), spinning mills in Guntur–Prakasam, and ferro-alloy units dependent on power prices; the Visakhapatnam Steel Plant (RINL) needed a ₹11,440 crore revival package (2025); the State's MSME policy offers power-tariff and interest subsidies and a revival and rehabilitation support for stressed small units; APSFC and the AP MSME Development Corporation help with credit.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Operational creditor (supplier, worker) vs financial creditor (lender): only financial creditors vote in the Committee of Creditors (66% for a plan). (2) Homebuyers were made financial creditors (2018). (3) Waterfall (Section 53): insolvency costs → workmen (24 months' dues) and secured creditors → employees → unsecured → Government dues → equity. (4) Wilful defaulter = able to pay but does not, or diverts funds (RBI master direction). (5) Moratorium under Section 14 stops all suits. (6) Individual insolvency: Part III of IBC applies to personal guarantors (2019). (7) Cross-border: India has not adopted the UNCITRAL Model Law (1997) yet. (8) Clean exit: IBC moved India's 'resolving insolvency' rank from 136 (2017) to 52 (2020) in Doing Business.
