INDIAN SOCIETY · Caste and tribe in India

Understand it first

What is it? Caste (jati) is a group you are born into, which traditionally fixed whom you marry (endogamy), what work you do, whom you eat with and your rank in a hierarchy based on ideas of purity and pollution. Varna is the old four-fold scheme in texts (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra); jatis are the thousands of real local groups. A tribe is a community with its own territory, dialect, culture and a strong sense of kinship, usually outside the caste hierarchy, often living in hills and forests. The Constitution abolished untouchability and gave safeguards; still, caste shapes marriage, politics and inequality, and tribes face land loss and displacement. Day 46 covered family and kinship; today we learn how sociologists explain caste, how it is changing, what tribes face, and the policies.

1. Caste: meaning, theories and features

Five ways of looking at caste
  • Origin theories: traditional (Purusha Sukta of the Rig Veda, 10th mandala: four varnas from the body of the cosmic being); racial (Risley: Aryan–native contact); occupational (Nesfield: caste from occupations); political (Abbé Dubois: Brahmin design); Hutton ('Caste in India', 1946: tribal taboos, mana); Ambedkar ('Castes in India', 1916: caste began when endogamy was imposed on classes; 'superimposition of endogamy on exogamy').
  • Jajmani system (W.H. Wiser, Karimpur village, UP, 1936): hereditary exchange of services between landowning patron (jajman) families and service castes (kamins) such as barber, potter, washerman, paid in grain at harvest; it gave security but kept people in place. In Andhra this was the 'balutedari / ayagar' village-servant system (12 ayagars). It declined with money wages, markets and migration.
  • Srinivas's concepts: Sanskritisation ('Religion and Society among the Coorgs', 1952): a lower caste adopts the customs, rituals and beliefs of a higher ('twice-born') caste to claim higher status (positional change, not structural); Westernisation (changes from British rule); dominant caste (Rampura, 1955): a caste that is numerically strong, owns much land and has political power, Western education and jobs (e.g. Jats, Reddys, Kammas, Lingayats, Vokkaligas, Marathas). De-Sanskritisation and 'Sanskritisation of tribes' also occur.

2. Change in caste and the policies

  • Forces of change: British rule (census classification 1871 onwards, courts, railways, education), reform movements (Phule, Narayana Guru, Periyar, Ambedkar), Constitution (Arts 15, 16, 17, 46, 330–342), land reforms, urbanisation, migration and education. Caste is weakening in ritual and occupation (commensality, pollution rules) but strengthening in politics and identity: caste associations, vote banks, caste-based parties ('politicisation of caste', Rajni Kothari). Inter-caste marriage is still under 10%.
  • OBCs and reservation: Kaka Kalelkar Commission (1953–55): 2,399 castes; Mandal Commission (1979–80, B.P. Mandal): 3,743 castes, about 52% of population, 27% reservation (implemented August 1990 by V.P. Singh); Indra Sawhney (1992): upheld 27%, creamy layer (now ₹8 lakh income), 50% ceiling, no reservation in promotions; NCBC made constitutional (102nd Amendment, 2018; 105th, 2021 restored States' power to list OBCs); Rohini Commission on OBC sub-categorisation (report 2023). State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (1 August 2024): a 7-judge bench allowed sub-classification of SCs (overruling E.V. Chinnaiah, 2004).
  • Caste counts: the last full caste census was 1931; the SECC 2011 caste data were not released; Bihar's caste survey (2023) found EBCs ~36% and OBCs ~27%; Census 2027 will enumerate caste for all. Andhra Pradesh: on the Rajiv Ranjan Mishra Commission's report, AP issued an ordinance (17 April 2025) splitting its 59 SC castes into three groups with 1% (12 most backward castes, e.g. Relli), 6.5% (18 castes incl. Madiga, Chamar) and 7.5% (29 castes incl. Mala) of the 15% SC quota. Historic caste atrocities in AP include Karamchedu (1985, Prakasam) and Tsundur (1991, Guntur).

3. Tribes

AspectKey points
Definition and featuresD.N. Majumdar: a collection of families with a common name, living in a common territory, speaking a common language, observing taboos on marriage and occupation, with a system of reciprocity. CommitteesDhebar Commission (1960–61): 'primitive tribes' (now PVTGs); Lokur Committee (1965): ST criteria; Tribal Sub-Plan (5th Plan, 1974), ITDAs; Bhuria Commission (2002–04); Xaxa Committee (2014): health, education, displacement
Tribal problems and the policy response
  • Tribal problems: land alienation to moneylenders and settlers; displacement by dams, mines and industry (tribals are about 8.6% of people but an estimated 40% of the displaced); indebtedness; loss of forest access; shifting cultivation pressures; malnutrition, sickle cell anaemia (National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission, July 2023, target 2047); low literacy (~59% for STs in 2011); identity and language loss; Left-wing extremism in central India. Schemes: Eklavya Model Residential Schools (1997–98; one in every block with 50%+ ST population and 20,000+ STs), Van Dhan Vikas Kendras (2018), MSP for Minor Forest Produce (2013–14), PM-JANMAN (2023), DAJGUA (2024).
  • Tribal movements: Santhal Hul (1855–56, Sidhu and Kanhu), Munda Ulgulan (1899–1900, Birsa Munda), Rampa rebellions (1879 and 1922–24, the latter led by Alluri Sitarama Raju in the Godavari Agency), Gond uprising under Komaram Bheem (1930s–40, 'Jal, Jangal, Zameen'), the Jharkhand movement (State formed 15 November 2000), and Bodoland and other autonomy movements in the north-east.
  • Andhra Pradesh: the AP Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation 1959, as amended by Regulation 1 of 1970 ('1/70'), bars transfer of land in Scheduled Areas to non-tribals; the Girijan Cooperative Corporation (GCC, 1956, Visakhapatnam) buys minor forest produce (honey, tamarind, adda leaves) and runs fair-price depots; ITDAs at Paderu, Rampachodavaram, Parvathipuram, Seethampeta, K.R. Puram, Srisailam (Chenchus) and for plains Yanadis; the Polavaram project displaces many Koya and Konda Reddi families (R&R packages); Araku coffee (tribal cooperative brand) and podu land pattas under FRA.

(8) Ghotul (Muria Gond youth dormitory, Chhattisgarh); Morung (Naga).

ECONOMY · Services sector: growth, role of IT and ITES

Understand it first

What is it? The services (tertiary) sector sells work, skills and convenience rather than goods: trade, hotels, transport, communication, banking, insurance, real estate, IT, health, education, public administration, tourism. Most countries grew from farms to factories and then to services; India jumped from farms to services, so services now make more than half of output but employ only about a third of workers. IT (software) and ITES/BPM (call centres, back-office, finance and accounting, analytics) made India the 'back office of the world' and now host Global Capability Centres (GCCs). Learn it as meaning and classification → growth and share → sub-sectors → IT and ITES story → services exports → problems → policies → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Size, growth and structure

Output vs jobs: the services puzzle
  • Share and growth: services' share of GVA rose from about 30% (1950–51) to over 50% (about 53–55% now); services grew about 7.5% a year since 2000 (Economic Survey 2025–26). They employ about 30% of workers (PLFS) — nearly two-fifths in some estimates — so output per worker is high in IT and finance but low in petty trade and informal services. Services also take the largest share of FDI (the 'services sector' is the top FDI recipient, followed by computer software and hardware).
  • Classification (national accounts): (1) trade, repair, hotels and restaurants; (2) transport, storage, communication and broadcasting; (3) financial services, real estate, ownership of dwellings and professional services (largest group; includes IT); (4) public administration, defence and other services (health, education, personal). Construction is counted in industry, not services.
  • Why India's services grew early: English-speaking, low-cost engineers and graduates; time-zone advantage with the US (24-hour work); telecom reforms (NTP 1994, 1999) and cheap data; liberalisation 1991; the Y2K work (1999–2000) that built trust; STPI (1991) with tax holidays; diaspora networks; and growing domestic demand (finance, retail, health). The Kuznets/Fisher–Clark pattern says services expand as income rises; critics warn of 'premature' services-led growth and Baumol's cost disease (low productivity growth in some services).

3. Problems, policies and Andhra Pradesh

  • Problems: (1) jobless growth: high output, limited jobs, mostly for the educated; (2) dualism: a small modern segment and a huge informal, low-wage segment (street vendors, small shops, domestic work); (3) concentration in a few metros; (4) skill mismatch and low employability of graduates; (5) AI and automation threaten routine BPM and coding jobs; (6) protectionism abroad: the US $100,000 fee on new H-1B petitions (September 2025), visa curbs, data-localisation rules; (7) dependence on the US and Europe (about 80% of IT exports); (8) weak data on services.
  • Policies: STPI (1991) and SEZ Act 2005; IT Act 2000 (amended 2008); National Policy on Software Products (2019); Digital India (1 July 2015); India Stack (Aadhaar, UPI 2016, DigiLocker, ONDC 2022); Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (rules notified 2025); IndiaAI Mission (March 2024, ₹10,372 crore: compute, models, datasets); Skill India, PMKVY; 'Champion Services Sectors' action plan (2018, 12 sectors); services trade deals (India–UK CETA 2025, India–EFTA TEPA in force 2025).
  • Andhra Pradesh: services give roughly two-fifths of AP's GSVA (agriculture is larger than the national share). IT hubs: Visakhapatnam (Rushikonda IT SEZ, TCS campus, Google's $15 bn AI data-centre hub announced October 2025), Tirupati (electronics and IT), Vijayawada–Amaravati (planned 'Quantum Valley' with IBM); AP IT & GCC Policy 4.0 (2024–29) offers incentives for GCCs and 'work from home towns' (co-working spaces in smaller towns). Tourism draws visitors across regions: Tirumala (one of the world's most-visited shrines), Srisailam, Araku, Lambasingi, Papikondalu, Gandikota, Amaravati Buddhist sites, beaches of Visakhapatnam and Suryalanka. Other services: ports and logistics (Visakhapatnam, Krishnapatnam, Kakinada, Gangavaram), aqua exports processing, education (IITs at Tirupati, IIM Visakhapatnam, AIIMS Mangalagiri).
