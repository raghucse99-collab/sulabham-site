INDIAN SOCIETY · Ethnicity, religion and women in society

Understand it first

What is it? India's diversity comes in many layers: ethnic (groups with a shared sense of descent, language and culture), religious, linguistic and regional. Diversity is a strength, but when one identity is used against another, we get ethnic conflict, communalism and regionalism. Secularism is the State's answer for religion. The second big theme is women: half the population, yet facing gaps in health, work, safety and power. Sociologists call the system that keeps men in control patriarchy; feminism and women's movements push back. Learn it as ethnicity and regionalism → religion, communalism and secularism → status of women (indicators, problems) → laws and schemes → women's movements → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Ethnicity, regionalism, communalism and secularism

Diversity and its challenges
  • Ethnicity: an ethnic group shares a belief in common descent, language, customs and homeland. Ethnic movements in India range from autonomy (Bodoland, Gorkhaland, Karbi) to secession (Naga, Mizo insurgencies, now settled: Mizo Accord 1986, Naga Framework Agreement 2015) and ethnic violence (Manipur Meitei–Kuki clashes from May 2023). Tools of accommodation: Sixth Schedule, Article 371 special provisions (371A Nagaland, 371G Mizoram, 371D Andhra Pradesh), new States, peace accords (Bodo Accord 2020).
  • Regionalism: loyalty to a region over the nation or demands for regional development. Causes: uneven development, language, ethnicity, political mobilisation. Forms: demands for new States (Andhra 1953, Gujarat–Maharashtra 1960, Punjab–Haryana 1966, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand 2000, Telangana 2 June 2014), sons-of-the-soil movements (job quotas for locals), inter-State river disputes. Healthy regionalism seeks development; unhealthy regionalism breeds hostility.
  • Communalism: using religious identity for political ends, treating followers of a religion as one group whose interests are opposed to others. Bipan Chandra's three stages: (1) people of one religion have common secular interests; (2) these interests differ from other religions' (liberal communalism); (3) they are hostile to others (extreme communalism). Roots: colonial 'divide and rule' (separate electorates, 1909), Partition (1947), political mobilisation, economic competition, rumours and social media. Major riots: 1984 (Delhi), 1992–93 (Mumbai), 2002 (Gujarat), 2013 (Muzaffarnagar), 2020 (Delhi).
  • Secularism, Indian style: the Western model is a wall of separation between State and religion; the Indian model is 'principled distance' (Rajeev Bhargava) and equal respect for all religions (sarva dharma samabhava): the State may reform religious practices (temple entry, untouchability) and support minority institutions. 'Secular' added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976); S.R. Bommai (1994): secularism is part of the basic structure. Articles 25–28 (freedom of religion), 29–30 (minority rights). Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act 1991 freezes the character of places of worship as on 15 August 1947 (except Ayodhya).

2. Women in Indian society

A women's self-help group meeting: SHGs bring savings, credit and voice. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
Women in India: where things stand
  • Status over time: relatively better in the early Vedic age (women scholars Gargi, Maitreyi, Lopamudra; education, choice in marriage); declined in the later Vedic and Smriti period (child marriage, no property, sati); Bhakti saints (Meerabai, Akka Mahadevi, Andal, Molla in Telugu) opened space; 19th-century reformers (Raja Ram Mohan Roy: sati abolished 1829; Vidyasagar: widow remarriage 1856; Savitribai Phule: first girls' school 1848, Pune; Veeresalingam in Andhra) and the freedom struggle brought women into public life.
  • Problems today: adverse sex ratio and sex-selective abortion (SRB 917); anaemia (57% of women, NFHS-5); maternal deaths (MMR 93, 2019–21; SDG target 70); low workforce participation (female LFPR about 35%, PLFS September 2026; urban about 26%) and unpaid care work; gender pay gap; violence (domestic violence, dowry deaths, rape, acid attacks, cybercrime; NCRB records 4 lakh+ crimes against women a year); under-representation in legislatures (Lok Sabha ~14% in 2024); digital divide. Global Gender Gap Index 2026 (WEF): India 131st, score 64.5%; best in political empowerment (67th), worst in economic participation. Iceland is 1st; full parity is ~120 years away worldwide.

Women leaders: Durgabai Deshmukh (Constituent Assembly, Andhra Mahila Sabha 1937), Duvvuri Subbamma, Sarojini Naidu (born in Hyderabad).

ECONOMY · Poverty, unemployment and inclusive growth (Indian economy revision)

Understand it first

What is it? Poverty means being unable to afford a minimum standard of living (food, clothing, shelter, health, education). It is absolute when measured against a fixed poverty line, and relative when compared with others' incomes. Unemployment means people who are willing and able to work at current wages cannot find work. Inclusive growth means growth whose benefits reach all, especially the poor, women, SCs/STs and backward regions, with jobs, skills, health and social protection. This day closes the Indian economy block, so it also revises earlier ideas. Learn it as measuring poverty → trends → causes and programmes → measuring unemployment → types and data → employment programmes → inclusive growth → Andhra Pradesh.

1. Poverty: measurement, trends and programmes

How India measured poverty
  • Measures: Head Count Ratio (HCR) = share of people below the line; poverty gap = how far below the line they are; Sen index combines both with inequality; MPI (Alkire–Foster method) counts deprivations in health, education and living standards (NITI's national MPI uses 12 indicators). Consumption data come from the Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) by NSO: 2022–23 and 2023–24 (average MPCE rural ₹4,122, urban ₹6,996 in 2023–24; the urban–rural gap fell to about 70%).
  • Trends: Tendulkar: 37.2% (2004–05) → 21.9% (2011–12); NITI MPI: 29.2% (2013–14) → 11.3% (2022–23), about 24.8 crore people escaped; World Bank (new $3.00 a day, 2021 PPP): extreme poverty 27.1% (2011–12) → 5.3% (2022–23) (about 26.9 crore fewer); at the $4.20 lower-middle-income line, 57.7% → 23.9%. Consumption Gini about 0.255 (World Bank calls India among the most equal by consumption), but income and wealth inequality is high (top 1% hold about 40% of wealth, World Inequality Lab). Poorest States by MPI: Bihar, Jharkhand, Meghalaya, UP; lowest: Kerala, Goa, Tamil Nadu.
  • Causes: low productivity in farming, disguised unemployment, population pressure, unequal land and assets, low human capital, caste and gender discrimination, regional imbalance, inflation, health shocks (medical costs push about 5–6 crore people into poverty yearly in some estimates), and informal jobs without social security.
  • Anti-poverty programmes (timeline): Garibi Hatao (5th Plan, 1971); IRDP (1978–80) → SGSY (1999) → NRLM/DAY-NRLM (2011); NREP (1980), RLEGP (1983) → JRY (1989) → SGRY (2001) → MGNREGA (2005) → VB-G RAM G (2025 Act, from 1 July 2026); food: PDS → TPDS (1997) → AAY (2000) → NFSA (2013) → free grain (2023); housing: Indira Awaas Yojana (1985) → PMAY-G (2016); financial inclusion: PMJDY (28 August 2014); social security: NSAP (1995), PMJJBY, PMSBY, APY (2015); health: Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY (2018, ₹5 lakh cover; all aged 70+ from 2024).

3. Inclusive growth and Andhra Pradesh

  • Inclusive growth: first a central goal in the 11th Plan (2007–12, 'faster and more inclusive growth') and the 12th Plan ('faster, more inclusive and sustainable'). Pillars: jobs, agriculture, education and skills, health, financial inclusion (JAM trinity: Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile), social protection, regional balance (Aspirational Districts Programme, January 2018; 112 districts; Aspirational Blocks 2023), gender equity and sustainability. Viksit Bharat @2047 aims at a developed nation by the centenary of Independence. Debates: trickle-down vs direct redistribution, jobless growth, K-shaped recovery after COVID, and freebies vs welfare.
  • Andhra Pradesh: AP's MPI fell to about 6% (2019–21), well below the national figure; P4 ('Public–Private–People Partnership') launched on Ugadi, 30 March 2025 links the top 10% of families ('margadarsis') with the poorest 20% ('bangaru kutumbam') to end poverty under Swarna Andhra @2047; NTR Bharosa pensions (₹4,000 a month for the elderly; higher for disabled, from 2024); 'One Family, One Entrepreneur'; Skill census (2024–25); strong SHG network; aspirational districts in AP include Alluri Sitharama Raju (old Visakhapatnam), Vizianagaram and YSR Kadapa.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Poverty line basis: Lakdawala used calorie norms with separate State lines; Tendulkar moved to a consumption basket with MRP (mixed reference period) and dropped calories. (2) Okun's law: higher unemployment ↔ lower output. (3) Phillips curve: short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment. (4) Lorenz curve and Gini (0 = equal, 1 = unequal). (5) Kuznets curve: inequality first rises, then falls with growth. (6) Lewis model: surplus farm labour moves to industry. (7) Vicious circle of poverty (Ragnar Nurkse). (8) Universal Basic Income was discussed in the Economic Survey 2016–17.
