INDIAN SOCIETY · Casteism, communalism and regionalism

Understand it first

What is it? Caste, religion and region are identities; they are natural and can be a source of pride. They become '-isms' when people put loyalty to their own group above fairness and the nation, treat other groups as rivals or enemies, and use the identity to win power, jobs or votes. Casteism = discrimination or mobilisation on the basis of caste; communalism = hostility between religious communities; regionalism = putting one region's interest above others (its milder form, demanding fair development for a backward region, is legitimate). Days 47 and 48 explained caste, religion and diversity; today we learn how these become divisive, their causes and effects, the laws and remedies, and Andhra Pradesh's own regional history.

Three 'isms': how identity turns divisive

1. Casteism

  • Forms: caste-based vote banks and ticket distribution; caste associations and caste-based parties; nepotism in jobs and contracts; social boycott; atrocities against Dalits (NCRB records about 50,000+ crimes against SCs a year; UP, Rajasthan, MP report the most); honour killings over inter-caste marriage; manual scavenging deaths; caste slurs on social media.
  • Causes: endogamy keeps groups closed; economic competition for land, jobs and seats; politicians mobilise caste for votes (Kothari's 'politicisation of caste' also brought democratic participation of lower castes — the 'silent revolution', Christophe Jaffrelot); weak enforcement of laws; lack of inter-caste contact in housing and schools.
  • Laws and judgments: Article 17; PCR Act 1955; SC/ST (PoA) Act 1989 (amended 2015: new offences, special courts; 2018: no preliminary inquiry or prior approval before arrest, restoring the law after Subhash Kashinath Mahajan, March 2018; upheld in Prithvi Raj Chauhan, 2020); Lata Singh (2006) and Shakti Vahini (2018): adults may marry by choice, khap diktats illegal; Abhiram Singh (2017): appeals for votes in the name of religion, race, caste, community or language are a corrupt practice under Section 123(3) of the RPA 1951; Dr Ambedkar Scheme for Social Integration through Inter-Caste Marriages (₹2.5 lakh).

2. Communalism

  • Causes: colonial 'divide and rule' (separate electorates 1909, Communal Award 1932); memory of Partition; political mobilisation and vote-bank politics; economic competition and unemployment; ghettoisation of housing; religious fundamentalism; rumours, fake news and social media; slow justice after riots; foreign-sponsored propaganda.
  • Remedies and institutions: National Integration Council (set up 1961 after a Nehru-called conference; last met 2013); Communal Harmony awards and the National Foundation for Communal Harmony (1992); PM's New 15-Point Programme for minorities (2006); Sachar Committee (2006); laws: BNS Section 196 (promoting enmity between groups; old IPC 153A), 197 (imputations against national integration), 299 (outraging religious feelings; old 295A), RPA 123(3); Tehseen Poonawalla (2018) guidelines against mob lynching (nodal officers, fast-track courts); BNS Section 103(2) makes mob lynching on grounds of caste, religion or community punishable with death or life; community policing, peace committees, inter-faith dialogue, media and education.

3. Regionalism and regional imbalance

  • Kinds: (1) supra-State (e.g. north–south debates on delimitation and tax devolution); (2) inter-State (river waters: Krishna, Cauvery, Mahadayi; borders: Belagavi); (3) intra-State (demands from backward regions: Vidarbha, Bundelkhand, Saurashtra, Gorkhaland, and in AP Rayalaseema and Uttarandhra); (4) sons-of-the-soil (local job quotas, e.g. AP Employment of Local Candidates in Industries Act 2019: 75% private jobs for locals). Causes: uneven development, neglect, language and culture, historical grievances, political entrepreneurs.
  • Tools to reduce imbalance: Finance Commission formula (income distance, area, population, forest, demographic performance); special category status (Gadgil–Mukherjee; hilly, border, low-density States; 11 States; the distinction faded after the 14th FC, 2015); Article 371 (A–J) special provisions; Backward Regions Grant Fund (2007); Aspirational Districts (2018) and Aspirational Blocks (2023); Zonal Councils (SR Act 1956: 5, chaired by the Union Home Minister; AP is in the Southern Zonal Council); North Eastern Council (1971); Inter-State Council (Article 263, 1990).
Regional questions in Andhra history
  • Andhra Pradesh's regional story: the Sri Bagh Pact (16 November 1937) promised Rayalaseema a fair share in university, capital, High Court and Krishna–Tungabhadra waters; Andhra State (1953) had Kurnool as capital and Guntur the High Court; the Gentlemen's Agreement (1956) merged Telangana with safeguards; the Mulki rules dispute led to Jai Telangana (1969) and Jai Andhra (1972); the Six-Point Formula (1973) and the 32nd Amendment added Article 371D (local cadres, zones, Administrative Tribunal) and 371E (Central University); the Telangana movement (2009–14) led to bifurcation on 2 June 2014. After 2014: the AP Reorganisation Act promised a special development package for seven backward districts (four of Rayalaseema and three of north coastal Andhra; ₹50 crore per district per year), Polavaram as a national project and tax incentives; the demand for Special Category Status was met with a 'special assistance' package (2016). Today's balance agenda: Amaravati as capital, Visakhapatnam as the economic hub, Rayalaseema industrial nodes (Orvakal, Kopparthy) and irrigation, and Uttarandhra projects (Bhogapuram airport, Polavaram left canal, Vamsadhara).
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Casteism vs caste: caste is a social structure; casteism is its partisan misuse. (2) 'Caste-ification' of politics and politicisation of caste (Kothari). (3) Communalism vs secularism vs fundamentalism (fundamentalism = return to 'pure' scripture; not always political). (4) Communal Award (1932) and the Poona Pact (24 September 1932) (joint electorates with reserved seats for Depressed Classes). (5) Article 51A(e): duty to promote harmony transcending religious, linguistic and regional diversities. (6) Article 19(2): reasonable restrictions on speech for public order and incitement. (7) Regionalism vs federalism: healthy regional demands strengthen federalism (e.g. Finance Commission share debates). (8) 'Sons of the soil' first rose with Shiv Sena (1966) in Maharashtra.

AP ECONOMY · Structure and growth: GSDP and sectoral contribution

Understand it first

What is it? GSDP (Gross State Domestic Product) is the value of all final goods and services produced within a State in a year — the State-level version of GDP. GSVA (Gross State Value Added) is GSDP minus product taxes plus product subsidies, and is used to measure sector shares. Estimates are made by the State's Directorate of Economics and Statistics (DES) using the national method (base year 2011–12 in the series used for these figures) and published in the Socio-Economic Survey before the State Budget. Andhra Pradesh's economy is unusual: it depends more on agriculture and allied activities (especially fisheries and horticulture) than India does. This starts the AP economy block (Days 49–54). Learn it as concepts → size and growth → sector shares → sub-sectors → strengths and weaknesses → vision and targets.

1. Size and growth

  • Concepts: current prices (nominal, includes inflation) vs constant prices (real, base-year prices); GSDP vs GSVA; NSDP = GSDP − depreciation; per capita income (PCI) = NSDP at current prices ÷ population (Day 50 covers PCI in detail). Quarterly GSDP estimates are now released by AP's DES.
  • Size: AP's GSDP (current prices) was about ₹15.9 lakh crore in 2024–25, about ₹17.6 lakh crore in 2025–26 (revised estimate) and is projected at about ₹19.75 lakh crore in 2026–27 (Budget, +12%). The economy has roughly tripled since bifurcation (2014–15). AP produces about 4.7–5% of India's GDP with about 4% of its population.
  • Growth: real (constant-price) GSDP growth was about 8% in 2024–25 (India 6.5%). In Q2 (July–September) 2025–26, AP's GSDP grew about 11.3% at current prices (India 8.7%), with agriculture and allied ~10.7% (India 1.8%), industry ~12.2% and services ~11.3%. The State aims for 15% growth a year to reach Swarna Andhra @2047 goals.

Inside AP's economy

3. Strengths, weaknesses, finances and vision

  • Strengths: long coastline (~974 km by the older measure) and ports; fertile deltas; aquaculture leadership; industrial corridors (VCIC, CBIC, HBIC nodes); young workforce; strong SHG and e-governance networks (village/ward secretariats). Weaknesses: loss of Hyderabad's service base in 2014 (revenue deficit at birth); high debt (outstanding liabilities about 36% of GSDP); regional gaps (Rayalaseema drought, Uttarandhra backwardness); low manufacturing share; cyclones and floods (e.g. Budameru floods, Vijayawada, September 2024; Cyclone Montha 2025); low per-farm incomes despite high output.
  • State finances (Budget 2026–27): total expenditure about ₹3.32 lakh crore; fiscal deficit 3.8% of GSDP (4.6% in 2025–26 RE); revenue deficit 1.1%; outstanding liabilities about 36% of GSDP; guarantees about ₹1.77 lakh crore. Merchandise exports about ₹1.59 lakh crore (FY26), led by engineering goods (~21%) and marine products (~18%).
  • Vision: Swarna Andhra @2047 (released December 2024) aims at a $2.4 trillion economy and per capita income of about $42,000 by 2047, with 15% annual growth, ten principles (zero poverty, jobs, water security, farm tech, logistics, energy, product perfection, Swachh Andhra (cleanliness), deep tech, human resources), and district and constituency-level action plans; P4 for zero poverty; sector policies (Industrial Development Policy 4.0, IT & GCC Policy 4.0, MSME, food processing, clean energy 2024–29).
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Structural transformation: AP's primary share is high because of high-value fisheries and horticulture, not just low productivity. (2) 'Growth engines' listed by the State: aquaculture, horticulture, livestock, manufacturing, logistics, tourism. (3) GSDP at constant prices removes inflation; compare growth only at constant prices. (4) District GDP (GDDP): AP estimates GSDP for each district; highest per capita income districts are usually Krishna/NTR, Visakhapatnam and West Godavari, and the lowest Srikakulam, Alluri Sitharama Raju and Parvathipuram Manyam. (5) Debt sustainability: FRBM limit 3% of GSDP (+0.5% for power reforms). (6) Bifurcation effect: AP got 58.32% of assets/liabilities on a population basis. (7) Revenue-deficit grant under the 15th FC helped AP. (8) Polavaram: a national multipurpose project on the Godavari (irrigation for ~7.2 lakh acres, 960 MW power, Vizag drinking water).
