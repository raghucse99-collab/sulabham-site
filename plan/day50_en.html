INDIAN SOCIETY · Crime against women

Understand it first

What is it? Crimes against women are offences in which women are targeted because they are women: violence at home (cruelty by husband and in-laws, dowry deaths), sexual violence (rape, assault, harassment, stalking), trafficking, acid attacks, honour crimes and cybercrime (morphing, online abuse). Their roots lie in patriarchy, unequal power and social tolerance of violence. The National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB, 1986) publishes the 'Crime in India' report every year; a higher number may mean more crime or better reporting. Day 48 covered the status of women; today we learn the data, the laws (old and new criminal codes), institutions, schemes, gaps and Andhra Pradesh's measures.

1. What the data show (NCRB 2024)

Crimes against women, 2024 (NCRB)
  • Crime in India 2024 (released May 2026): 4,41,534 cases of crimes against women (−1.4% from 4,48,211 in 2023); rate 64.6 per lakh women. Largest heads: cruelty by husband or relatives (~27%), kidnapping and abduction (~15%), POCSO cases involving girls (~15%), assault to outrage modesty (~11%); rape 29,536 (highest in Rajasthan); dowry deaths 5,737. Uttar Pradesh reports the most cases (66,396); Delhi has the most among UTs. Overall IPC/BNS crime fell about 6%, but cybercrime keeps rising.
  • Southern States (2024): Telangana 24,495, Andhra Pradesh 19,952, Karnataka 19,945, Kerala 15,037, Tamil Nadu 11,363 cases. AP recorded 441 rapes and 71 dowry deaths. Under-reporting is huge: NFHS-5 found about 29% of ever-married women aged 18–49 faced spousal violence, but only about 14% of those who faced violence sought help.
  • Causes: patriarchal attitudes and acceptance of violence; dowry; alcohol; economic dependence; low conviction rates (rape convictions about 25–30%) and long trials; fear of stigma; poor policing and victim-blaming; online anonymity; migration and urban isolation; and harmful portrayals in media.

3. Gaps, the way forward and Andhra Pradesh

  • Gaps: huge case pendency (lakhs of rape and POCSO cases pending in courts); low conviction; delayed forensic reports; marital rape is still an exception in law (the question is before the Supreme Court); victim compensation (Section 357A CrPC / 396 BNSS) is uneven; Nirbhaya Fund under-used in some States; police sensitivity and women in police (only about 12% of police are women).
  • Way forward: gender sensitisation from school; women's economic independence; more women police and women help desks; forensic capacity and time-bound trials; community watch; safe public transport and lighting; digital safety education; strict action against dowry; reaching the 14% who seek help to the 86% who do not.
  • Andhra Pradesh: Disha police stations (one per district, 2020) and the Disha SOS app (the AP Disha Bill 2019, proposing 21-day trials and death for rape, still awaits Presidential assent); Shakti teams and the 'Shakti' app for women's safety (2024–25); Mahila Police in village and ward secretariats; One Stop Centres in all districts; Women's Commission (APSWC); cyber mitras; special drives against ganja and sand-mafia linked violence; 'Sakhi' One Stop Centres link medical, legal and shelter help.

AP ECONOMY · Per capita income (PCI)

Understand it first

What is it? Per capita income is the average income of a person in the State in a year: Net State Domestic Product (NSDP) at current prices ÷ the State's population. It is the most common single number to compare living standards across States and years. But it is only an average: a few very rich people or a big port and refinery in one district can raise it while many stay poor, and it ignores prices, unpaid work and non-income wellbeing. So PCI should be read with inequality, poverty, HDI and MPI. Yesterday we saw AP's GSDP; today we learn how PCI is measured, AP's level and growth, comparison with India and other States, gaps between districts, reasons and limits.

1. Measuring PCI and AP's level

Per capita income: what it shows and hides
  • Three related averages: (1) per capita GSDP = GSDP ÷ population (output per person; AP ₹2,98,058 in 2024–25 vs India's per capita GDP ₹2,34,859); (2) per capita income (PCI) = NSDP at current prices ÷ population (the official State PCI); (3) real PCI = NSDP at constant prices ÷ population (removes inflation). National counterpart: per capita Net National Income (NNI) (about ₹2.05 lakh in 2024–25).
  • AP's PCI: ₹2,66,240 in 2024–25 (MoSPI; growth 11.9%, 3rd-fastest among 18 States/UTs reporting) and about ₹2,94,507 in 2025–26 (advance estimate, +10.4%). AP's PCI is roughly 1.3 times India's average, but below its southern neighbours: Karnataka (~₹3.81 lakh), Telangana (~₹3.80 lakh), Tamil Nadu (~₹3.58 lakh) in 2024–25; it is about ₹1 lakh below Telangana, a gap linked mainly to Hyderabad. Highest PCIs in India: Sikkim, Goa, Delhi; lowest: Bihar.
  • Trend: after bifurcation AP's PCI was close to ₹1 lakh (2014–15); it roughly tripled in a decade in current prices. Growth was hit in 2020–21 (COVID) and recovered after. The State targets a PCI of about ₹5 lakh by 2029–30 and about $42,000 (≈ ₹35 lakh) by 2047 under Swarna Andhra.

2. District gaps and their reasons

Visakhapatnam's skyline: the district with AP's highest per capita income. (Photo: Wikimedia Commons, free licence)
Group of districts (2025–26 AE)Picture and reasons
TopVisakhapatnam (~₹6.59 lakh, highest): port, steel plant, HPCL refinery, pharma, IT; Krishna, NTR: commerce (Vijayawada), aqua, agriculture; Eluru, Tirupati: aqua, horticulture; Sri City and pilgrimage economy
MiddleWest Godavari, Guntur, Bapatla (above ₹3 lakh): delta farming, aqua, chillies, cotton, trade; East Godavari, Konaseema, Kakinada: paddy, coconut, aqua, gas and port
LowerSeveral Rayalaseema districts and Prakasam, Nandyal (below about ₹2.3 lakh), Annamayya (negative growth in 2025–26), and tribal and north-coastal districts (Srikakulam, Vizianagaram, Parvathipuram Manyam, Alluri Sitharama Raju): rain-fed farming, drought, low industrialisation, migration
  • Why PCI differs across districts: resource base (deltas vs dry plateau), irrigation (canals vs rain-fed), industry and ports, urbanisation, infrastructure, human capital (literacy, skills), natural disasters, and historical neglect. A district's PCI can be inflated by capital-intensive units whose profits go out of the district, so it does not always mean local people are richer.
  • Limits of PCI: it is an average (hides inequality); current prices include inflation; it ignores non-market work (housework, subsistence), environmental costs, public services, and purchasing power differences between States; and NSDP counts production inside the State, not income earned by residents elsewhere (e.g. remittances from the Gulf). Better pictures need HDI, MPI, consumption surveys (HCES) and district-level SDG indices.
  • Raising PCI and reducing gaps: irrigation for Rayalaseema (Handri–Neeva, Galeru–Nagari, Polavaram–Banakacherla link proposal), industrial nodes (Orvakal, Kopparthy, Krishnapatnam, Donakonda), Uttarandhra projects (Bhogapuram airport, Mulapeta port, Polavaram left canal), skilling (Skill census), MSMEs and 'one family, one entrepreneur', district action plans under Swarna Andhra, and P4 to link the top 10% with the poorest 20%.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) PCI vs per capita GSDP: PCI uses net product (after depreciation). (2) Mid-year population (1 October) is used as the denominator. (3) PPP comparisons are used internationally, not for State PCI. (4) Kuznets: inequality may rise first as PCI grows. (5) 'Income convergence': poorer States should grow faster; in India divergence has been seen. (6) Disposable income = income after taxes and transfers. (7) Median vs mean income: median is less affected by the very rich. (8) District PCI ranking in AP has changed after the 2022 reorganisation into 26 districts (from 13).
