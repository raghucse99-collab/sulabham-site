INDIAN SOCIETY · Child abuse and child labour

Understand it first

What is it? A child is a person below 18 (UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, UNCRC 1989; India ratified in 1992). Child abuse means any harm done to a child by an adult or older person: physical (beating), sexual, emotional (insults, threats), neglect (no food, care or schooling) and exploitation (labour, trafficking, begging, child marriage, online abuse). Child labour is work that deprives children of childhood, school and health; Indian law calls a person below 14 a child and 14–18 an adolescent for labour. Both rob the nation of its demographic dividend. Day 50 covered crimes against women; today we learn the data, causes, Constitution, laws, institutions, schemes, gaps and Andhra Pradesh's measures.

1. What the data show

Crimes against children, 2020–2024 (NCRB)
  • Crime in India 2024 (NCRB, May 2026): 1,87,702 cases of crimes against children (+5.8% from 1,77,335 in 2023; about +46% since 2020); rate 42.3 per lakh children (39.9 in 2023). Kidnapping and abduction is the largest head, followed by POCSO cases (69,191, about 37%). Most cases: Maharashtra (24,171), Uttar Pradesh (22,222), Madhya Pradesh (21,908). Missing children: 1,47,175. Cybercrime against children: 1,238 cases, about 9 in 10 involving sexually explicit material. Cases against juveniles in conflict with law rose 11.2%.
  • Hidden abuse: the Ministry of WCD study (2007) found about two in three children faced physical abuse and 53% reported some form of sexual abuse; most abusers were known persons (family, neighbours, teachers). Most abuse is never reported because of fear, shame and dependence.
  • Child labour: Census 2011 counted about 1.01 crore working children aged 5–14 (3.9% of the age group), down from 1.26 crore (2001); most in UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, MP; most work in agriculture, then home-based units, brick kilns, eateries, domestic work. Worldwide, ILO–UNICEF (2025) estimates 138 million child labourers (about 54 million in hazardous work); SDG 8.7 aimed to end child labour by 2025. Child marriage (NFHS-5): 23.3% of women aged 20–24 married before 18 (26.8% in NFHS-4); Andhra Pradesh 29.3%, above the national average; highest West Bengal (~42%).
  • Causes: poverty and debt (bonded labour); large families and parental illiteracy; migration (children move with brick-kiln and construction workers); school dropout and poor school quality; demand for cheap, obedient labour; social acceptance ('learning the family trade'); gender norms and dowry (child marriage); broken families, alcoholism; weak enforcement; online exposure without safety; disasters and COVID (orphanhood, lost income).

3. Gaps, the way forward and Andhra Pradesh

  • Gaps: different ages for 'child' in different laws (14 for labour, 18 for POCSO and marriage); family-enterprise exception can hide child labour; POCSO pendency and low conviction; delayed rescue and rehabilitation; weak CWCs and child care institutions; online grooming and CSEAM spreading fast; missing children often trafficked; child marriage still accepted in some communities; lack of fresh data (no national child labour survey since Census 2011).
  • Way forward: every child in school (MV Foundation's idea: *every out-of-school child is a potential child labourer*); cash transfers linked to schooling; mid-day meals; livelihoods for parents; 'good touch–bad touch' and digital safety education; school safety audits; more special POCSO courts; strong CWCs, JJBs and foster care; community vigilance (village child protection committees); tech platforms to remove CSEAM; convergence of police, labour, education and WCD departments.
  • Andhra Pradesh: child marriage 29.3% (NFHS-5) is a major concern; Mission Vatsalya sponsorship and foster care; Operation Muskaan by AP Police to trace and rescue missing and working children; Disha/Shakti teams also cover children; CWCs and JJBs in every district; Thalliki Vandanam (2025; ₹15,000 a year per school-going child to mothers) and Dokka Seethamma mid-day meals to keep children in school; Mahila Police in village and ward secretariats watch for child marriages and dropouts; past concern: children in cotton-seed farms and brick kilns. The MV Foundation (Hyderabad, 1991, Prof. Shantha Sinha, later first NCPCR chairperson) began its work in undivided AP.

AP ECONOMY · State revenue: tax and non-tax

Understand it first

What is it? A State government needs money for salaries, pensions, welfare schemes, roads and interest. Its budget (Article 202, 'Annual Financial Statement') has two kinds of money coming in: revenue receipts, which do not create a debt or reduce assets (taxes, fees, grants), and capital receipts, which do (loans taken, loans recovered, sale of assets). Revenue receipts have four parts: (1) State's own tax revenue, (2) State's own non-tax revenue, (3) share in central taxes and (4) grants from the Centre. The first two are the State's own revenue; the last two are central transfers. Day 49 gave the size of AP's economy; today we learn who can tax what, where AP's money comes from in 2026–27, the main heads, the Finance Commission's role, problems and reforms.

1. Who can tax what?

Taxing powers and transfers under the Constitution
  • Article 265: no tax without the authority of law. The Seventh Schedule divides taxes: the State List (entries 45–63) includes land revenue, excise on alcohol for human consumption, stamp duty (except on some documents), taxes on vehicles, electricity, professions, entertainment by local bodies, and sales tax/VAT on petroleum crude, diesel, petrol, natural gas, aviation fuel and alcohol (kept outside GST).
  • GST (101st Amendment, 2016; from 1 July 2017): Article 246A gives both Parliament and State legislatures power to tax supply of goods and services; a State collects SGST, the Centre CGST, and IGST on inter-State supply is shared (Art 269A). The GST Council (Art 279A) decides rates. Compensation to States for revenue loss ended in June 2022. GST 2.0 (Sept 2025) cut most items to two slabs (5% and 18%).
  • Transfers from the Centre: Article 270: central taxes are shared on the advice of the Finance Commission (Art 280); the 16th FC (chair Arvind Panagariya; 2026–31) kept the States' share at 41% of the divisible pool and raised AP's share to 4.217% (from 4.047% under the 15th FC). Criteria: income distance 42.5%, population 17.5%, demographic performance 10%, area 10%, forest 10%, and a new contribution to GDP 10%. Article 275: grants-in-aid; the 16th FC discontinued revenue-deficit grants (AP had received them since bifurcation). Article 282: discretionary grants, e.g. centrally sponsored schemes.

  • Reading the numbers: about 59% of revenue receipts are AP's own revenue and about 41% come from the Centre, so AP depends heavily on central transfers. Within own taxes, GST, liquor and petroleum together give about three-fourths. The BE expects own taxes to grow 28%, much faster than the economy (~12% nominal), which critics call optimistic; stamps +70% depends on a revival of real estate (Amaravati, ports, industrial corridors).
  • Problems: loss of Hyderabad's revenue base at bifurcation (2014); end of GST compensation (2022) and of revenue-deficit grants (2026); narrow base (agriculture is not taxed; few large cities); dependence on liquor, which raises social concerns; GST rate cuts reduce SGST; tax buoyancy below targets in past years; large committed spending (salaries, pensions, interest about ₹37,281 crore) leaves little room. Revenue deficit 2026–27 BE: ₹22,003 crore (1.1% of GSDP); fiscal deficit ₹75,868 crore (3.8%).
  • Reforms: GST analytics, e-invoice and e-way bill checks to stop evasion; digitised land records and online registration; auction of mineral blocks; property-tax reform in urban local bodies; user charges that cover costs; industrial growth (ports, nodes, data centres) to widen the base; better asset monetisation; and the 16th FC advice to bring off-budget borrowings into the budget and to target cash transfers better.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Tax buoyancy = % change in tax revenue ÷ % change in GSDP (above 1 is good). (2) Tax–GSDP ratio: AP's own tax is about 6–7% of GSDP. (3) Direct vs indirect: almost all State taxes are indirect; land revenue and profession tax (max ₹2,500 a year, Art 276) are direct. (4) Article 268: stamp duties levied by the Centre but collected and kept by States. (5) Article 293: States need Centre's consent to borrow if they owe it money. (6) State Finance Commission (Art 243-I, 243-Y) shares State revenue with panchayats and municipalities. (7) FRBM: AP Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2005. (8) The 15th FC had a 2.5% weight for tax effort, which the 16th FC dropped.
