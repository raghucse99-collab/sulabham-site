INDIAN SOCIETY · Youth unrest and agitation

Understand it first

What is it? Youth in India means people aged 15–29 (National Youth Policy 2014); they are about 27% of the population, and India's median age is about 28. Youth unrest is the discontent, frustration and anger of young people when their hopes (education, a good job, dignity, a voice) do not match reality. It becomes agitation when it turns into organised collective action: protests, rallies, strikes, bandhs, hunger strikes and now online campaigns. Agitation can be constructive (it has driven reform and freedom movements) or destructive (violence, damage to public property). It is the flip side of the demographic dividend: unused young energy can become a demographic disaster. Today we learn the causes, theories, forms, landmark agitations in India and Andhra, recent cases, government responses and the way forward.

1. Causes and theories

How frustration turns into agitation
  • Economic causes: unemployment and underemployment (youth unemployment about 10% by usual status, much higher for urban educated youth and young women; PLFS); degrees without skills (education–job mismatch); slow growth of formal, secure jobs; long waits for government recruitment; rising costs of education and housing; farm distress pushing rural youth to cities.
  • Educational and administrative causes: exam paper leaks and cancelled exams; delays and court cases in recruitment; changes in reservation rules; fee hikes; poor hostels and campus facilities; campus politics. Social and political causes: corruption and nepotism; caste, regional and identity grievances; gap between generations; social media that spreads both awareness and misinformation; drug abuse; a feeling of not being heard by the political system.
  • Theories: Relative deprivation (Ted Gurr, *Why Men Rebel*, 1970): unrest grows when people feel they get less than they expect or than others get; frustration–aggression (Dollard, 1939): blocked goals lead to aggression; structural strain / anomie (Merton): society sets goals (success) but blocks the means; rising expectations (de Tocqueville): unrest often comes when things are improving but not fast enough; resource mobilisation: movements need organisation, leaders and money, not just anger.

3. Responses, the way forward and Andhra Pradesh

  • Government responses: Ministry of Youth Affairs and Sports; National Youth Policy 2014 (five goals: productive workforce, strong and healthy generation, social values, participation and civic engagement, inclusion); NSS (1969) and Nehru Yuva Kendra Sangathan (1972), now with the 'MY Bharat' (Mera Yuva Bharat) platform (31 October 2023); Skill India and PMKVY; PM Internship Scheme (2024); Startup India, MUDRA; ELI (employment-linked incentive) scheme; Public Examinations Act 2024 (jail 3–5 years for individuals; 5–10 years and a fine of at least ₹1 crore for organised leaks); Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (2020); National Youth Day 12 January (Swami Vivekananda), International Youth Day 12 August.
  • Way forward: jobs-rich growth (manufacturing, MSMEs, tourism, care economy); skills linked to industry, apprenticeships; transparent, time-bound recruitment with a calendar; student grievance cells and campus democracy; mental health support; digital literacy against fake news; sports and culture; youth in local governance; treating protest as a democratic right (Art 19(1)(a), (b)) while keeping it peaceful (reasonable restrictions under Art 19(2), (3)).
  • Andhra Pradesh: AP's youth face job migration to Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai and abroad; key agitations include Visakha Ukku (1966), Jai Andhra (1972), Samaikyandhra (2013) against bifurcation, Special Category Status (2015–18) and protests against privatisation of the Visakhapatnam Steel Plant (from 2021). Responses: APSSDC skill centres and Skill Census (2024–25), mega DSC (2025, about 16,000 teacher posts), the promise of 20 lakh jobs in five years, 'one family, one entrepreneur', the EAGLE anti-narcotics force, and job melas in all regions.

AP ECONOMY · State expenditure, debt and interest payments

Understand it first

What is it? Yesterday we saw where AP's money comes from; today we see where it goes and how the gap is filled. Revenue expenditure is spending that does not create assets (salaries, pensions, interest, subsidies, welfare transfers, maintenance). Capital expenditure creates assets or reduces liabilities (roads, dams, buildings, loans given, debt repaid). When spending is more than income, the State borrows; past borrowing becomes debt, and debt needs interest. Too much revenue spending financed by loans is like a family borrowing to buy groceries: it leaves less for the future. Today we learn how AP spends in 2026–27, committed spending, deficits, debt, guarantees, off-budget borrowing, rules (FRBM) and the way forward.

2. Deficits, debt and interest

The key deficit and debt measures
  • Deficits (2026–27 BE): revenue deficit ₹22,003 crore (1.1% of GSDP), down from ₹41,118 crore (2.3%) in 2025–26 RE; fiscal deficit ₹75,868 crore (3.8%), down from 4.6%; primary deficit ≈ ₹38,600 crore. A revenue deficit means part of the borrowing pays for day-to-day spending, not assets; the golden rule says a State should borrow only for capital spending. Borrowings 2026–27: ₹97,315 crore.
  • Debt: AP's outstanding liabilities rose from 33% of GSDP (2021–22) to about 36% (2024–25 and 2026–27 BE), roughly ₹7 lakh crore, well above the FRBM glide path and the 20% State debt limit suggested by the N.K. Singh FRBM Review Committee (2017). Add government guarantees of ₹1.77 lakh crore (about 10% of GSDP, Dec 2025) on loans of State corporations (power, civil supplies, APSDC, Amaravati). Off-budget borrowings through corporations hide debt; the 16th FC asks States to bring them into the budget, and the Centre now counts them in the borrowing limit. AP ranked 17th of 18 large States on NITI Aayog's Fiscal Health Index 2025.
  • Interest: ₹37,281 crore in 2026–27 (16% of revenue receipts; above about 10% is commonly read as a sign of stress). Interest is the fastest-growing committed cost and crowds out capital spending. Rules: Article 293: States may borrow only within India; if they owe the Centre, they need its consent (the Centre sets a net borrowing ceiling, about 3% of GSDP, with extra room for power reforms and capex). The AP FRBM Act, 2005 (amended) sets deficit targets; the 16th FC recommends a 3% of GSDP fiscal-deficit limit for States from 2026–31.

3. Why debt rose and the way forward

  • Why AP's debt rose: bifurcation (2014): AP got 58.32% of the old State's debt but lost Hyderabad's revenue; a revenue deficit from the start; large welfare and cash transfers; power-sector losses and farm power subsidy; COVID (2020–21); pending bills and corporation borrowings; slow growth of own revenue; costly projects (Polavaram, Amaravati) with delayed central funds; and disasters (cyclones, floods).
  • Way forward: raise own revenue (Day 51 reforms); target welfare to the needy and plug leaks with DBT and data; cut power losses (smart meters, DISCOM reform); shift spending to capital and complete projects on time; refinance costly debt; publish all off-budget and guarantee data; outcome budgeting; asset monetisation; follow the FRBM path and the 16th FC advice (subsidy rationalisation, closing loss-making PSUs). The P4 model and private investment can share the burden of infrastructure.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Effective revenue deficit = revenue deficit − grants for creating capital assets (used for the Centre). (2) Debt sustainability: debt is stable if GSDP growth > interest rate (r < g). (3) State Development Loans (SDLs): bonds sold by States through RBI auctions; the main source of State borrowing. (4) Ways and Means Advances from the RBI cover short-term gaps; long use leads to overdraft. (5) Consolidated Fund of the State (Art 266); Contingency Fund (Art 267); expenditure 'charged' on the Consolidated Fund (e.g. Governor's emoluments, High Court judges' salaries, debt charges, Art 202(3)) is not voted. (6) CAG audits State accounts (Art 151(2): reports go to the Governor). (7) Pending bills and arrears are a hidden liability. (8) Zero-based budgeting starts each item from zero.
