INDIAN SOCIETY · Welfare mechanism: public policies and welfare programmes

Understand it first

What is it? A welfare state is one where the government takes responsibility for people's basic needs: food, health, education, work, housing and old-age security, especially for the poor and weak. A public policy is the government's decision and plan of action on a problem (e.g. 'every child in school'); a welfare programme or scheme is the tool that carries the policy to people (e.g. mid-day meals). The welfare mechanism is the whole machinery: laws, budgets, departments, local bodies, delivery systems (DBT, PDS, secretariats) and checks (social audit, CAG). Days 47–52 covered the groups that need protection; today we learn the constitutional basis, the policy cycle, kinds of schemes, how delivery changed, major national and AP programmes, problems, and reforms.

1. Basis, policy cycle and kinds of schemes

How a public policy is made and corrected
  • Constitutional basis: the Preamble (social and economic justice); DPSPs: Art 38 (welfare of the people, reduce inequality), 39 (livelihood, fair distribution, equal pay, no exploitation of children), 41 (work, education, public assistance in old age, sickness, disability), 42 (just working conditions, maternity relief), 43 (living wage), 46 (education and economic interests of SCs, STs and weaker sections), 47 (nutrition, public health); Fundamental Rights like 21 (life with dignity, food), 21A (education); Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules give welfare work to panchayats and municipalities.
  • Kinds of welfare schemes: universal (for all, e.g. free schooling) vs targeted (for the poor, e.g. ration cards; risk of exclusion errors – needy left out – and inclusion errors – non-needy included); rights-based laws (food, work, education, information) vs discretionary schemes; cash (DBT, pensions) vs in-kind (rice, meals, houses); Central Sector (100% Centre, e.g. PM-KISAN) vs Centrally Sponsored (shared, often 60:40; 90:10 for NE and Himalayan States) vs State schemes; social insurance (contributory, e.g. EPF, ESI, PMJJBY, PMSBY, APY) vs social assistance (non-contributory, e.g. old-age pensions).
  • Who does what: Union ministries design national schemes; NITI Aayog and its DMEO (Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office) review them; the Finance Commission and budget fund them; State departments, district collectors, PRIs and ULBs implement; CAG, legislature committees, social audit, RTI and courts hold them to account.

3. Problems and reforms

  • Problems: leakages and corruption; exclusion errors (Aadhaar-biometric failures, missing documents) and inclusion errors; too many overlapping schemes; poor awareness; delays in payments; weak local capacity; fiscal burden of large cash schemes and the 'freebies' debate (*S. Subramaniam Balaji, 2013*; *Ashwini Upadhyay* case referred to a larger bench in 2022); schemes measured by money spent rather than outcomes; political use of beneficiary lists.
  • Reforms: DBT (from 1 Jan 2013; JAM trinity; Aadhaar Act 2016) saves money by removing fake and duplicate beneficiaries; One Nation One Ration Card (portability for migrants); convergence and rationalisation of CSS; outcome budgets and third-party evaluation (DMEO); social audit (Sec 17 of the employment guarantee law; AP's SSAAT, 2009, was a pioneer); grievance portals (CPGRAMS, AP's PGRS); Aspirational Districts (2018) and Aspirational Blocks (2023); data-based targeting through family databases (AP's Family Benefit Monitoring / P4 survey).
  • Andhra Pradesh's mechanism: Village and Ward Secretariats (2 Oct 2019), about 15,000, bring over 500 services to the doorstep; pensions are paid at home on the 1st of every month; Mahila Police, ANMs and digital assistants in each secretariat; WhatsApp governance ('Mana Mitra', 2025) offers hundreds of services online; PGRS for grievances; P4 (Public–Private–People Partnership), launched on Ugadi 2025, links the top 10% with the poorest 20% ('bangaru kutumbalu'). Welfare reaches every region, from Srikakulam to Chittoor.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Welfare state idea: Beveridge Report (UK, 1942); India's DPSPs drew on the Irish Constitution. (2) Right to Food case: *PUCL v. Union of India (2001)* led to cooked mid-day meals and later the NFSA. (3) Aadhaar: *K.S. Puttaswamy (2018)* upheld it for welfare but struck down private use (Sec 57). (4) Section 7 of the Aadhaar Act lets the government require Aadhaar for subsidies paid from the Consolidated Fund. (5) Universal Basic Income was discussed in the Economic Survey 2016–17. (6) Antyodaya ration cards (35 kg a month) for the poorest families. (7) Kudumbashree (Kerala, 1998) and SERP/Velugu (AP, 2000) are model SHG missions. (8) Freebies vs welfare: the Supreme Court has said spending on health, education and food is not a 'freebie'.

AP ECONOMY · Central assistance, externally aided projects and the recent AP Budget

Understand it first

What is it? Central assistance is all the money the Union government gives a State besides the State's own revenue: its share of central taxes, Finance Commission grants, funds for centrally sponsored schemes, special packages and loans. Externally aided projects (EAPs) are State projects funded by loans from foreign or multilateral lenders such as the World Bank, ADB, AIIB, NDB, JICA or KfW; under Article 293 and central rules these loans come through the Centre. For AP, central help matters more than for most States because of bifurcation (2014): the AP Reorganisation Act promised help for the capital, Polavaram, backward districts and revenue gaps. Days 51–52 covered AP's revenue and spending; today we learn the channels of central money, AP's special promises and packages, EAPs, and the highlights of the 2026–27 AP Budget.

1. Channels of central money and AP's special promises

Five channels of central money
  • In AP's 2026–27 budget, central transfers are about ₹96,800 crore: tax share ₹64,362 crore + grants ₹32,458 crore, about 41% of revenue receipts. Grants are mainly for centrally sponsored schemes (e.g. VB-G RAM G ₹8,365 crore, Samagra Shiksha, Jal Jeevan, PMAY, POSHAN), Finance Commission grants for local bodies and disasters (SDRF, 75:25 Centre:State for general States), and SASCI loans. The 16th FC raised AP's tax share to 4.217% but stopped revenue-deficit grants.
  • SASCI (Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment): started in 2020–21 (COVID); 50-year interest-free loans for capital projects, partly tied to reforms (urban planning, land records, old-vehicle scrapping, police housing). AP regularly seeks more under it (e.g. ₹10,000 crore asked in 2025).
  • AP Reorganisation Act, 2014 and after: Polavaram declared a national project (Sec 90), with the Centre bearing the irrigation component; special development package for seven backward districts (four of Rayalaseema and three of north coastal Andhra) on the lines of KBK/Bundelkhand (₹50 crore per district a year); support for the new capital; tax incentives for industry; promises of a steel plant in Kadapa, a railway zone (South Coast Railway, Visakhapatnam, announced 2019), Dugarajapatnam port, and central institutions (IIT Tirupati, IIM Visakhapatnam, NIT Tadepalligudem, AIIMS Mangalagiri, IISER Tirupati, central and tribal universities). Special Category Status was promised in Parliament (Feb 2014) but replaced in Sept 2016 by a special assistance package: the Centre would repay 90% of loans for EAPs signed in 2015–2020.

3. Highlights of AP Budget 2026–27

  • Size: ₹3,32,205 crore (+10.5% over 2025–26 RE); revenue receipts ₹2,34,140 crore; capital outlay ₹48,698 crore (+47%); revenue deficit 1.1%, fiscal deficit 3.8% of GSDP; GSDP about ₹20 lakh crore (2026–27 BE). A separate agriculture budget is also presented.
  • Priorities: welfare (SC/ST/BC/minorities ₹52,855 crore; pensions ₹27,719 crore; Thalliki Vandanam ₹8,456 crore; Annadata Sukhibhava ₹6,600 crore; Stree Shakti ₹1,420 crore); education (₹35,673 crore) and health (₹19,630 crore); capital works (Amaravati ₹6,000 crore, Polavaram ₹6,105 crore, irrigation, roads); rural employment (VB-G RAM G ₹8,365 crore); free power for weavers, police modernisation (3,000 vehicles, a cyber-crime station in every district). The theme links to Swarna Andhra @2047 and its ten principles.
  • Critical view: own-tax growth (+28%) and capital outlay (+47%) look ambitious, since 2025–26 RE fell short of BE (capex 18% below); interest (16% of revenue receipts) and guarantees (₹1.77 lakh crore) limit room; dependence on central funds (~41%) is high. The budget's success depends on timely central releases, EAP disbursement and private investment.
Other angles asked in exams

Other angles asked: (1) Gadgil formula (1969) and Gadgil–Mukherjee (1991) once decided Plan assistance to States; after 2015 (NITI), the 14th FC raised devolution to 42%. (2) Special Category States got 90:10 funding; criteria included hilly terrain, low density, border location, backwardness and weak finances; the 14th FC recommended no new SCS. (6) IBRD (World Bank) lends to middle-income countries; IDA gives soft loans to the poorest. (7) The Department of Economic Affairs clears all external loans. (8) Purvodaya (Union Budget 2024–25) covers the eastern States including AP.
